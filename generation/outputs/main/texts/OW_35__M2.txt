The chandelier in the salon of Lady Ashworth hung like a frozen cascade of diamonds, casting a light so cold and bright that it seemed to bleach the colour from the guests’ souls, leaving them as polished and as hollow as the porcelain figures in the hall. It was a Tuesday evening in Mayfair, that most delightful of days, for it possesses none of the solemnity of Monday nor the desperate exhaustion of Wednesday. The air was thick with the scent of gardenias and old money, and the conversation moved with the precision of a Swiss watch, ticking away the hours in small, meaningless increments.

At the edge of the room, holding a glass of champagne that he had no intention of drinking, stood Mr. Arthur Pym, the junior curate of St. Jude’s, on the Strand. He was a man of such profound shyness that he had once apologized to a lamp-post for bumping into it, and of such modesty that he believed his own shadow was an exaggeration of his true size. He was wearing his best suit, which was a shade of brown that the tailor had assured him was "dignified," though it looked more like a dried leaf stuck to a branch. He had come to the reception only because his mother, a woman who possessed a romantic notion of society that was both charming and dangerous, had insisted that a young man must learn to mingle, or else he would wither in the parish like a fern in a coal cellar.

"Ah, you must be the great explorer, Mr. Pym," said a voice behind him, smooth as oil and twice as slippery.

Arthur turned, expecting to see a clergyman, but instead found himself facing Lord Kensington, a man whose moustache was so large it seemed to be the only thing holding his face together. Lord Kensington’s eyes were bright with a predatory interest that Arthur mistook for genuine curiosity.

"I am, my lord," Arthur said, his voice trembling slightly. "Though I must confess, I have never explored anything further than the churchyard, and I found even that a bit damp."

Lord Kensington laughed, a sound like dry leaves skittering over gravel. "Oh, but the world does not know that, does it? The papers say you have been to the ends of the earth. They say you have spoken to the spirits of the jungle and mapped the rivers that flow backward."

Arthur blinked. He had indeed seen his name in a society column, but it had been a mistake of the sort that is common in London, where a name like Pym is easily confused with a place like Pym’s Corner, or a person like the late Mr. Pym, the forger. "I fear there has been a confusion, my lord. I am a curate. I deal in souls, not coordinates."

"Nonsense," said Lord Kensington, patting Arthur on the shoulder with the casual familiarity of a man who had never been told 'no' and had never had the time to understand why. "The truth is so boring, isn't it? It is all so flat and grey. But the imagination, my dear fellow, that is the only country worth visiting. Come, sit down. Lady Ashworth is asking for you. She wishes to hear of your adventures in the Congo. Or was it the Ganges? Or perhaps the edge of the world? Do tell her something vivid. Make it shine."

Arthur looked at the empty space where a chair should have been, then at the circle of women who were already turning their faces toward him like sunflowers tracking a very expensive sun. He felt a sudden, sharp panic, the kind that rises in the throat like a fish seeking air. He looked at his champagne, then at Lord Kensington’s expectant, masked face. In that moment, Arthur Pym, the curate who had never crossed the street without looking both ways, decided that the truth was a poor garment and that a lie, if well tailored, could be a suit of armour.

"I have not been to the Congo," Arthur began, his voice finding a sudden, unexpected steadiness. "The Congo is too crowded with tourists. I went to the Valley of Whispers, in the highlands of the Andes."

Lady Ashworth’s eyebrows rose in a perfect arc of surprise. "The Valley of Whispers? I have heard of it. Is it true that the trees there are made of glass?"

"They are not made of glass," Arthur said, leaning forward, his eyes taking on a distant, dreamy quality. "They are made of ice. And the wind that blows through them does not sound like wind. It sounds like the voices of ancestors who are trying to return a lost hat."

A ripple of laughter went through the group. It was a light, tinkling sound, like silver bells in a shop window. Lord Kensington winked at him, and in that wink was the entire philosophy of the evening: that if you say it with sufficient confidence, it becomes true.

Arthur continued, and as he spoke, he felt the weight of his own modesty lifting off his shoulders like a heavy coat. He described a waterfall that fell upward, defying gravity in a fit of pique, and a tribe of men who communicated entirely through the colour of their skin, which changed with their mood. He described a serpent that was not a beast, but a metaphor for time, wrapped around the neck of the mountain. He spoke of the way the moon looked down from the sky, not as a celestial body, but as a large, white eye, watching the world with a critical and slightly bored expression.

The guests leaned in. They were not interested in the truth, for the truth is often dull and requires too much effort to verify. They were interested in the beauty of the lie, the elegance of the fabrication. They loved the idea of the upward waterfall, for it suggested that even the laws of nature could be persuaded to do a little dancing. They loved the colour-changing men, for it implied that emotion was visible, and therefore manageable.

Arthur Pym, the shy curate, found himself the centre of a small orbit. He spoke of the food, which was mostly stone, but polished to a high sheen, and therefore very digestible to the eye. He spoke of the silence, which was so loud it had to be measured in decibels. He spoke of the love he had found there, a woman who was made of mist and who evaporated whenever he tried to hold her, leaving only a dampness on his hand and a profound sense of loss.

By the time the clock struck ten, Arthur was no longer the man who had entered the room. He was a figure of authority, a witness to wonders. The champagne glass in his hand was empty, but he did not notice. He was standing now, surrounded by a halo of admiration, his brown suit suddenly looking less like a dried leaf and more like the uniform of a man who had seen the hidden face of the world.

"Mr. Pym," said Lady Ashworth, her voice trembling with a excitement that was almost religious. "My husband has been planning an expedition to the South for years. He has the funds, and he has the ships, but he lacks the vision. He lacks the man who knows how to speak to the ice trees and the mist women. We would be honoured if you would lead the expedition. You will have your own ship. It will be named *The Truth*, as a joke, of course. But you will have the compass. You will have the map."

Arthur looked at her. He looked at Lord Kensington, who was already sketching a plan in the air with his finger. He looked at his own hands, which were steady, and his heart, which was beating a quick, strange rhythm, not of fear, but of a terrible, thrilling freedom.

"I accept," he said. "But I must warn you. The map may change. And the compass may point not to North, but to the place where we are most afraid to go."

Lady Ashworth smiled, a smile that was both sharp and sweet. "That is exactly what we need," she said. "A little fear. It gives the adventure its flavour."

And so, in the drawing room of Lady Ashworth, amidst the scent of gardenias and the glitter of diamonds, a shy young curate became an explorer, not by crossing an ocean, but by crossing the threshold of his own silence. He left the reception that night with a invitation in his pocket and a lie on his lips, and he did not know which of the two would weigh more heavily on his soul as he walked out into the cold London night, where the stars looked down at him with the same bored, white eyes he had described to the guests, waiting to see what he would do next.
