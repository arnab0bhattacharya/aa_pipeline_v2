The wind off the moors did not merely blow through the windows of the Harewood Parsonage; it screamed, a thin, high-pitched keen that seemed to strip the paint from the sash frames and chill the marrow of the bones. It was November, and the Pennines had turned the color of bruised iron. Mrs. Elara Vance, thirty-four and already looking fifty, sat in the drafty study, mending a tear in her husband’s clerical collar. Her fingers, red and chapped from the cold, moved with a mechanical precision that belied the turmoil in her chest.

Across the room, huddled in a large armchair before the dying fire, lay her sister, Lillian. Lillian was twenty-six, a woman of such frailty that her presence seemed more spiritual than physical, a ghost tethered to the earth by a bed of fever and a lung that rattle-wheezed with every breath. She was writing. She always wrote. The scratching of her quill against the paper was the only sound in the room that did not sound like suffering.

Elara’s husband, the Reverend Josiah Vance, had gone out to visit a dying parishioner in the village below, leaving the sisters alone with the storm. It was a rare, heavy silence, broken only by the creak of the house settling into its foundations. Elara had given up her own life to this: her ambition to be a painter, her desire to see the sea, her need for intellectual companionship beyond the scope of parish records and soup tureens. She had traded all of it for the duty of caretaker, for the quiet, steady devotion required to keep Lillian alive. It was a noble exchange, she told herself, a sacrifice worthy of a saint. But saints did not feel the hot, sour taste of envy rising in their throat.

The door opened with a violent bang, admitting a gust of rain and a man who smelled of wet wool and tobacco. It was Mr. Arthur Penhaligon, a literary critic from Leeds who had stayed at the parsonage because the inn in the village was full. He had been charming, if slightly condescending, over dinner, but now he looked haggard, his eyes red-rimmed from the travel.

"Forgive the intrusion, Mrs. Vance," he said, shaking the rain from his coat. "I was walking the lane and saw a light in this window. I know it is impolite, but the cold is a beast tonight."

Elara stood, instinctively straightening her posture. "You are welcome here, Mr. Penhaligon. We have no fire, but there is a pot of tea on the stove."

"I have a fire, actually," Penhaligon said, pointing to the grate. "Though it is dying. I was looking for something to do with my hands before the fire went out, and I noticed a sheet of paper on the table. I am a fool for prying, but I could not help but pick it up."

Elara’s heart stopped. She looked at Lillian, who was staring at him with wide, unblinking eyes, her face pale as the paper itself. The sheet on the table was Lillian’s. Elara had seen her discard dozens of such sheets into the waste bin, only to retrieve them later, reading them by candlelight. Lillian never showed them to anyone. They were private prayers, private screams, locked away in the dark.

"Is it something you would wish me to return?" Penhaligon asked, his voice soft. He held the paper up.

Elara felt a sudden, violent urge to snatch it from him. *Give it back,* she thought. *Let it stay hidden. Let it stay ours.* But Lillian did not move. She did not speak. She simply watched the critic read.

Penhaligon’s eyes scanned the page. His brow furrowed, then relaxed. He read the first stanza again, then the second. He turned the paper over, checking the back, then set it down on the mantelpiece with a reverence that made Elara’s skin prickle.

"Mrs. Vance," he said, turning to face them. "This is... remarkable. The imagery is stark, yet deeply tender. 'The wind is a thief that steals the warmth from the stone, but leaves the cold to stay.' It is a profound observation on isolation. I have read much of the current poetry in the London journals, and I confess I am bored by their polish. This has the rawness of truth. Who wrote this?"

Elara opened her mouth, but no sound came. She looked at Lillian. Her sister’s lips were moving, forming words that did not produce sound. A small, terrified smile played on Lillian’s face, the first real smile Elara had seen in months.

"It is my sister," Elara said, her voice cracking. "Lillian. She is... she is unwell, but she writes."

Penhaligon nodded, a look of genuine appreciation on his face. "A hidden gem. I would be honored to see more, if the author would permit it. I have connections at *The Quarterly Review*. This voice is distinct. It is haunting."

Elara felt a wave of pride, hot and bright, wash over her. Lillian, her invalid, her burden, was a poet. Lillian, who could not lift a teacup without trembling, had captured the soul of the moors in ink. It was a miracle. It was a vindication of their suffering.

But beneath the pride, something else stirred. It was a cold, sharp thing, like a needle in the heart. *She does this,* Elara thought, the jealousy twisting in her gut. *She has always done this. While I scrubbed the floors and boiled the clothes and held her hair back when she vomited, she was writing. She was creating. She was becoming someone.*

Elara looked at her own hands, stained with soap and starch, her nails bitten to the quick. She had put away her paints ten years ago. She had buried her sketches in a trunk in the attic, a trunk she had not opened since the day Lillian’s illness began. She had become the nurse, the wife, the provider of comfort. She had given up her voice to preserve Lillian’s life. And now, Lillian was being praised for the very thing Elara had sacrificed herself to protect.

It was an ugly thought. It was a sinful thought. Elara closed her eyes, trying to pray, but the words of the Lord’s Prayer were drowned out by the image of Penhaligon holding Lillian’s words aloft like a trophy.

"Shall I have more tea brought in?" Elara asked, her voice sounding distant to her own ears.

"Please," Penhaligon said. "And if it is not too much trouble, could you fetch the rest of the papers? I believe I saw a stack in the waste basket."

Elara walked to the basket. It was lined with old newspaper, but inside were the crumpled balls of Lillian’s verses. As she reached in, her fingers brushed against a page. She recognized the handwriting. It was a poem about a garden, about thorns and roses, about the beauty of pain.

She thought of herself in that garden. She was the thorn, perhaps. Or maybe the soil, dark and unglamorous, necessary for the growth. She picked up the crumpled paper, smoothing it out with trembling hands. It was beautiful. It was better than anything she had ever written, better than anything she had ever painted.

And she hated it.

She hated the beauty of it. She hated that it came so easily to a woman who could not walk to the door. She hated that her own pain had produced nothing but dust and silence, while Lillian’s pain produced art. She hated that she felt relief that Lillian was finally seen, and anger that Lillian was finally seen without her.

"Mrs. Vance?" Penhaligon called out. "Is everything alright?"

Elara looked up. Her face was dry, her expression neutral. She placed the stack of poems on the table, arranging them with careful, deliberate precision.

"She is shy," Elara said, her voice steady. "But I believe she would like you to have them. All of them."

She did not look at Lillian. She could not bear to see the gratitude in her sister’s eyes, nor the confusion. She turned back to the fire, watching the embers fade, and felt a profound sense of loss that had nothing to do with the poems, and everything to do with herself. The wind screamed outside, but Elara no longer heard it. She only heard the scratching of the quill, a sound that had once been a comfort, and was now a wound.
