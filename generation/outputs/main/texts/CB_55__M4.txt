The rain had beaten against the windows of St. Hilda’s for three days without ceasing, and the mill-yard below was a churn of brown water and sodden straw, when I first saw Miss Honora Garthwaite. She sat with her father in the second pew from the front—a position of privilege that Mr. Garthwaite occupied with the complacent air of a man who owned not only the mill but, by extension, the living that sustained the parson who preached at him. I had been at Netherby only six weeks, and my sermons had already made me a marked man.

I confess I had not meant to thunder so fiercely against the sin of holding labour cheap. But the sight of the children filing into the mill at five of a morning, their small faces grey with fatigue, had worked upon me like a goad. And so, on the third Sunday of my ministry, I had spoken of the mill-owner who grinds the faces of the poor and calls it providence; and Mr. Garthwaite, who was not a fool, had understood me perfectly. He had not spoken to me since.

But his daughter had.

She came to me after the service—when the congregation had dispersed and the rain had driven all but the most devout to their homes—and stood in the porch of the church with her bonnet dripping and her eyes fixed on mine. She was not beautiful in the fashion of the age: her face was too strong for that, her jaw too decided, her gaze too direct. But there was a fire in her that drew me as the hearth draws a frozen man.

“You spoke the truth,” she said, without preamble. “My father will dismiss you for it. But you spoke the truth.”

I was so taken aback that I could only stammer something about the duties of my office.

“I am not a child, Mr. Ellerby,” she returned, and there was a flash of something like contempt in her voice. “I know what it costs to speak against my father. I have done it myself, in smaller ways, and I know the price.” She paused, and her gaze softened. “I wanted you to know that there is one in his household who does not think you a fool.”

Then she was gone, her pattens clicking across the wet flags, and I was left with the rain and the echo of her words.

After that, we met often—by accident, or so I told myself. She walked on the moor above the mill, and I walked there too. She read the same books I read, and we spoke of them with a freedom I had never known with any woman. She had a mind like a blade, and she used it without mercy on my arguments, and I loved her the more for every wound she gave me.

But there was a gulf between us that no amount of talk could bridge. Her father was a hard man, and a proud one, and he had plans for his daughter that did not include a curate with a conscience and a stipend of eighty pounds a year.

“He will never consent,” she said to me one evening in October, when the leaves were falling and the smoke from the mill chimneys hung low over the valley. We stood on the bridge above the mill-race, and the water roared beneath us. “He has told me as much. He says you are a firebrand and a fool, and that if I marry you I shall starve.”

“And what do you say?” I asked, my heart hammering against my ribs.

She was silent for a long moment. The wind tore at her shawl, and she clutched it about her shoulders with hands that I saw were trembling.

“I say that I love you,” she said at last, and her voice was steady though her hands were not. “But I say also that I will not marry against my father’s wishes. Not because I fear him—though I do, a little—but because I will not have it said that I stole my happiness from under his roof, and left him to curse my name in his old age. He is a hard man, Mr. Ellerby, but he is my father, and I have a duty to him that I cannot in conscience set aside.”

“Even if it breaks your heart?” I demanded, and my voice was harsh with pain.

“Even then,” she said, and her eyes met mine with a look that was both tender and implacable. “I am not made for easy choices, sir. If I were, I should have run away with you a month ago, and let the world say what it would.”

I turned from her and gripped the parapet of the bridge, staring down into the black water. The temptation to rage at her—to call her cold, to accuse her of loving her own virtue better than she loved me—rose hot in my throat. But I knew, even in that moment of bitter disappointment, that she was right. It was the very firmness of her principle that I loved; to ask her to abandon it would be to ask her to become someone else.

“Then we must wait,” I said at last, and my voice was hoarse. “We must wait, and pray, and hope that your father’s heart may soften.”

She shook her head, and a single tear escaped her eye and ran down her cheek, though she did not brush it away.

“You do not know my father,” she said. “He does not soften. He only breaks.”

And so we parted—not in anger, but in sorrow, which is worse. I walked back to my lodging through the rain, and the words of the Psalmist came to me unbidden: *My soul is athirst for God, yea, even for the living God: when shall I come to appear before the presence of God?* But it was not God I thirsted for that night; it was Honora Garthwaite, and the sound of her voice, and the touch of her hand upon my arm.

The weeks that followed were bitter. Mr. Garthwaite, who had heard of our meetings—for nothing in Netherby escaped the mill-owner’s notice—summoned me to his office and told me, in terms of studied contempt, that I was to keep away from his daughter. I told him that I would obey his command as it concerned his house, but that I would not promise to avoid the moors, which were God’s earth and not his.

He laughed at that—a hard, dry laugh—and told me I was a fool, and that fools were better employed in the mill than in the pulpit.

I left his office with my ears burning and my heart sick within me. But I did not leave Netherby. I could not. For every morning, as I walked to the church, I saw her light in the window of the mill-house; and every evening, as I returned, I saw it again. And I knew that she was there, and that she was waiting, even as I waited, for a door that might never open.
