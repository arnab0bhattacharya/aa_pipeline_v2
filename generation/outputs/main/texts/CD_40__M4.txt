The London coach set down Dr. Abel Hartley at the foot of Crowsley Hill on a dripping afternoon in the November of 1854, and the town of Saint Wilfrid’s-in-the-Marsh received him with that moist and melancholy hospitality which cathedral towns reserve for returning sons who have not prospered. He was eight-and-twenty years of age, brown as a walnut from the Madras sun, and thinner than the boy who had gone out; and as he stood in the yard of the Black Swan with his one portmanteau at his feet, looking up the crooked High Street to where the great grey towers of the cathedral lifted themselves out of the mist like the masts of some stranded ark, it must be confessed that his heart beat with a foolish violence quite unbecoming a man of science.

For it was here he had been born; here he had run wild with the sexton’s children among the tombstones; here he had first learned his letters at the grammar school, and here he had first loved—ah, there was the rub!—Miss Clara Winterbourne, the daughter of the very apothecary-surgeon to whose shop he had been apprenticed, and whose partner he might have been, had not a certain letter from an uncle in the Company’s service, enclosing a passage and a promise, turned his head and his fortunes eastward at the age of nineteen.

He had written to her. He had written, indeed, no fewer than eleven letters, each of them a masterpiece of eloquence and constancy; and she had answered three, each of them shorter than the last, until at length the answers had ceased altogether, and the silence of the Indian Ocean had closed over his hopes. He had supposed her lost to him. He was to discover, within the week, that she was lost to him in a manner far more particular and far more galling.

It was in the shop of Mr. Theophilus Winterbourne, apothecary and surgeon, that he learned the news; for thither he bent his steps on the second morning, having hired for himself a small and dusty practice in Pudding-Lane, at the lower end of the town, where the patients were few and the fees fewer, and where a brass plate, inscribed *Dr. Hartley, Surgeon*, was screwed to the door with two of his own screws, because the landlord would not pay for a carpenter. The shop smelt, as it had always smelt, of camphor and laudanum and the bitter sweetness of jalap; and behind the counter stood Mr. Winterbourne himself, older, greyer, rounder, with his spectacles pushed up on his bald forehead and his hands, as of old, making a little steeple of themselves above his stomach.

“Abel!” cried the old man. “Abel Hartley! God bless my soul! Home from the Indies, and not a word to warn us! And how brown you are, my boy—how prodigiously brown!—and thin!—and—but you will stop to dine? You must stop to dine. Clara will be overjoyed. Clara!” he called, turning his head towards the parlour door. “Clara, my love! Here is a surprise for you!”

The door opened, and there entered—not the Clara of the grammar-school holidays, all ribbons and giggles and blown kisses—but a woman of five-and-twenty, composed, handsome, with a certain quiet lustre about her that comes of happiness settled and secure; and upon her arm, with the air of a man who has come into port and means to stay there, rested the hand of Mr. Josiah Prout, solicitor, of Prout, Prout and Prout, of the Close—a gentleman of forty, portly, ruddy, with a gold watch-chain and a laugh that began somewhere in the region of his boots and came rolling up like a coach on a turnpike road.

“Dr. Hartley,” said Clara, and her colour rose a little, but her voice was steady, “you are very welcome home. You know Mr. Prout, I think? My husband.”

The word went through Abel Hartley like a lancet. He bowed; Mr. Prout shook him by the hand with a heartiness that was worse than any coldness; and the whole party sat down to a dinner of which the doctor tasted nothing, and talked of the climate of Madras and the state of the harvest, and parted at the gate with expressions of the warmest mutual regard, and Abel Hartley walked back to Pudding-Lane with his hands in his pockets and his hat over his eyes, and told himself that he was a fool, and that the world was a very dusty, dry, unprofitable sort of place, and that he wished he had stayed in India and died of the cholera.

It might have ended there. It would have ended there, I dare say, but for the illness of the boy.

For Clara had a brother—a lad of twelve, by name Timothy, or, as the family called him, Tim—a merry, freckled, wiry little urchin, who had been the plague and the delight of the grammar school, and who now, in the December of that same year, was taken with a fever that no one could name and Mr. Winterbourne could not master. He had been bled, and blistered, and calomeled, and dosed with bark and with wine and with everything that the pharmacopœia could supply and the old man’s affection suggest; and still he lay in the little room over the shop, burning and babbling, while the snow fell in the Close and the cathedral bells rang out the old year.

It was on the third night of the crisis that Clara came to Pudding-Lane in a cab, at two in the morning, with her hood drawn over her face and her eyes wild with watching. “Dr. Hartley,” she said, standing in the little surgery with her hands clasped before her, “I have come to you because there is no one else. My father is worn out; Mr. Prout—Mr. Prout is a good man, but he is not a physician. Tim is dying. Will you come?”

He came. He came every night for a fortnight, and every day; and it was his hand that turned the fever, his voice that coaxed the boy back from the very brink of the grave, his care that watched the long convalescence through the grey January mornings and the snowy February afternoons. The boy, who had adored him as a child, adored him now with the passionate hero-worship of a sick lad; would take his physic from no other hand; would sleep only when the doctor sat by his bed; and would cry out, in the delirium of the worst nights, for “Abel—Abel—don’t go to India!”

And the gratitude of the family! It was poured out upon him in a hundred ways—in presents of game and of wine, in invitations to dinner, in the pressing offers of Mr. Winterbourne to “make a partnership of it, my boy, and hang out the old shop together”—and in the eyes of Clara Prout, who could never thank him enough, who pressed his hand at the door with a warmth that was almost a caress, who said to him one evening, as he was putting on his great-coat in the hall, “Abel, I have been very wicked. I thought you had forgotten us all.”

“Forgotten!” said Abel Hartley, with a laugh that was not a laugh. “Forgotten! Oh, yes, Mrs. Prout. I had forgotten.”

And Mr. Prout—honest, blundering, well-meaning Mr. Prout—who would shake him by the hand at the gate of the Close and say, “My dear sir, my dear sir, you have saved the boy’s life, and I am proud to call you my friend, and I hope you will dine with us on Sunday, and bring your appetite, ha, ha!—and, by the way, if you should ever want a little advice about a lease or a will, Prout, Prout and Prout are at your service, and no fee, my dear sir, no fee, I assure you!”—Mr. Prout, who suspected nothing, who saw nothing, who was as blind as a beetle in a flower-pot, and as happy as a man could be.

So the winter passed, and the spring came, and the rooks began to build in the cathedral elms, and the boy Tim was out again, pale and thin, but merry as ever, and the whole town rang with the praises of Dr. Hartley. And Dr. Hartley, sitting alone of an evening in his little surgery in Pudding-Lane, with the lamp lit and the ledger open before him, and the brass plate glimmering in the fog outside, would sometimes take out of his desk a little packet of three letters, tied with a faded ribbon, and read them over, and put them back, and sigh; and then he would go to the window and look up the crooked street to where the great grey towers of the cathedral stood out against the stars, and say to himself, with a smile that was half a sigh, that the world was a very dusty, dry, unprofitable sort of place, but that a man must do his duty in it, and that, after all, there were worse things than a good practice in Pudding-Lane.
