Mr. Cuthbert Pomfrey, known to the wider world of fashionable London as the most immaculate of dandies, stood before his cheval glass in a state of profound and uncharacteristic dishabille. His cravat, a waterfall of dove-grey silk, was perfection itself. His coat, a masterpiece of charcoal superfine, fitted his narrow shoulders without a single crease. His trousers were a triumph of mathematical tailoring. And yet, his right hand flew to his lapel, to the buttonhole that was as much a part of him as his own nose, and found only the memory of a stem. The carnation, a pristine white one with petals edged in a delicate, blushing pink, was gone. In its place was a small, cruel tear in the buttonhole’s silk binding, the sole evidence of its recent, violent removal.

The horror of it was absolute. It was the morning of the great wedding, the union of the season, where the Earl of Ashby’s daughter was to be joined to the Duke of Marchmont’s heir. To appear without a flower would be a social suicide more spectacular than any ever witnessed in the annals of Mayfair. It would be a confession of failure, a public declaration that Cuthbert Pomfrey was, after all, merely a man and not a monument to sartorial perfection. His valet, a stoic fellow named Pim, had already been dispatched to scour the house, but Cuthbert knew the truth. The flower had been his last. He had meant to order more, but the week had been a whirl of fittings and rehearsals, and now it was Sunday.

Sunday. The word tolled in his head like a funeral bell. Every florist in London, from the grand emporiums of Covent Garden to the humblest barrow-boy, was shuttered. The city was in the grip of a Sabbath stillness, a silence broken only by the distant clang of church bells, each one a mocking reminder of his predicament. He could not, he simply could not, attend the wedding without a flower. He would sooner appear naked.

With a feverish energy, he dismissed Pim’s apologetic return and, seizing his silver-topped cane, fled his house on Half-Moon Street. The air was crisp and clean, the sky a pale, watery blue. The streets were near-deserted, save for a few soberly dressed souls making their way to matins. Cuthbert’s polished boots clicked a frantic rhythm on the pavement. He knew the city, he knew its secret byways and hidden trades. There had to be a flower somewhere. One single, perfect carnation.

His first thought was the great glass palaces of the West End. He hurried towards Piccadilly, his eyes scanning the shuttered fronts of the fashionable shops. He passed the imposing edifice of Mr. Algernon Finch’s, the most celebrated florist in the city. Its windows, which on any other day would have been a riot of colour and exotic scent, were now blank, the iron grilles drawn down like eyelids. A small, hand-painted sign hung on the door: “Closed for the Lord’s Day.” Cuthbert felt a physical blow. He rapped sharply on the glass with the head of his cane, a futile, angry gesture. The sound echoed in the empty street, unanswered.

He turned, his mind racing. There were other places. The less reputable ones. The market boys who sometimes sold leftovers from the great houses. He headed for the warren of streets around Covent Garden. The grand piazza was eerily quiet, the cobblestones slick with the memory of yesterday’s rain. The usual cacophony of hawkers and porters was replaced by the cooing of pigeons and the distant rumble of a solitary cart. He found the stalls empty, the great baskets and barrels stacked and silent. He spotted a lone figure, an old woman hunched over a bucket, scrubbing the steps of a tavern.

“Madam,” he called, his voice unnaturally loud. “A flower. I need a flower. A carnation, specifically. White with a pink edge.”

The old woman looked up, her face a map of wrinkles. She squinted at his immaculate attire, a flicker of amusement in her tired eyes. “Sunday, sir,” she said, her voice a dry rasp. “Flowers is for the week. Today is for the Lord.”

“I am aware of the day, madam,” he snapped, his patience fraying. “But surely, somewhere… a stray bloom, a discarded stem?”

She shook her head slowly. “Nothing, sir. Nothing but cabbage leaves and yesterday’s hopes.”

Despair began to claw at him. He pressed on, his route becoming a desperate, zigzagging pilgrimage through the sleeping city. He crossed into the Strand, its theatres and music halls dark and lifeless. He thought of the hotels. The grand ones, like the Savoy, might have hothouses. He hurried towards the river, his breath coming in short, sharp gasps. At the Savoy, a haughty concierge in a pristine uniform informed him that the hotel’s floral arrangements were for the exclusive pleasure of its guests, and that the head florist was, in any case, not on the premises. The man’s gaze lingered on Cuthbert’s empty buttonhole with an expression of pity that was more wounding than any insult.

He wandered north, into the labyrinth of Soho, then east towards the City. The bells had ceased, and a profound, almost oppressive silence had settled over the rooftops. He was no longer thinking, merely moving, his mind a whirlwind of shame and panic. He pictured the wedding, the sea of faces turning to stare at his bare lapel, the whispers, the scandal. The image of Lady Beatrice, the bride, a girl of exquisite taste, noticing his failure and giving him that small, pitying smile… it was unbearable.

It was in a narrow, forgotten alley off Cheapside that he saw it. A sliver of colour against the grime. He stopped dead. There, in a grimy window, behind a dusty pane of glass, was a single, perfect white carnation, its petals delicately edged with pink. It sat in a plain earthenware pot, looking utterly out of place amidst the clutter of an old curiosity shop.

He tried the door. It was locked. He peered inside. The shop was a cavern of shadows, filled with the ghosts of furniture and the glint of tarnished brass. He knocked, politely at first, then with increasing desperation. He was about to give up when he saw a movement in the gloom. A slow, shuffling figure approached the door.

The door creaked open to reveal a man so old he seemed to be made of parchment and dust. He wore a velvet smoking jacket that had long ago lost its nap and peered at Cuthbert through spectacles so thick they magnified his eyes to comic proportions.

“We’re closed,” the old man whispered.

“Please,” Cuthbert said, his voice cracking with a vulnerability he had not felt since boyhood. He pointed a trembling finger at the window. “The flower. The carnation. I must have it. I will pay you anything.”

The old man followed his gaze. A slow smile spread across his wrinkled face. “Ah, the flower. It’s not for sale, sir. It’s for remembering.”

“Remembering?” Cuthbert’s voice was barely a whisper. “I will give you ten pounds for it. Twenty.”

The old man chuckled, a dry, rustling sound. “Money cannot buy a memory, sir. It was my wife’s favourite. She wore one just like it on the day we were married. Fifty years ago, it was. I keep it there to remind me.”

Cuthbert stared at him, his own frantic need suddenly seeming small and absurd. He looked at the old man, at the faded velvet, at the dusty shop, and then back at the flower. It was no longer just a buttonhole. It was a symbol of a life, a love, a memory. His own problem, the social terror of a morning, felt suddenly hollow.

He took a breath, the frantic energy draining from him. “Your wife,” he said softly. “She had excellent taste. A white carnation with a pink edge is a flower of great distinction. I am to attend a wedding today, a great wedding. And I find myself without a flower. It is a small thing, I know, but it feels like the end of the world.”

The old man looked at him for a long moment, his magnified eyes seeming to see right through to Cuthbert’s soul. He nodded slowly. “A wedding, you say?” He turned, shuffling back into the gloom. He returned a moment later, the carnation in his hand. He held it out.

“Take it,” he said. “For the wedding. A flower should be worn, not kept in a dusty window. My Elsie would have wanted it so.”

Cuthbert took the flower, his fingers closing around the stem as if it were a holy relic. He looked at the old man, his throat tight. He reached into his pocket, pulling out a handful of sovereigns, pressing them into the man’s gnarled hand. “For your kindness,” he said. “For the memory.”

He didn’t wait for a reply. He turned and walked out of the alley, the carnation held carefully in his hand. He pinned it to his lapel with fingers that were still trembling, but for a different reason now. The flower felt different. It was no longer a mere accessory. It was a gift, a story, a small act of grace in a silent city.

He hailed a passing hansom cab, its driver one of the few souls braving the Sabbath quiet. As the cab rattled towards the church, Cuthbert Pomfrey, the most immaculate of dandies, looked at his reflection in the window. He was still perfect. But for the first time in his life, he felt truly complete.
