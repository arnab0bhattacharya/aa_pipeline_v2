The building had not yet begun to breathe; that was the thing she noticed first, every morning, the way the building held its breath. Six o'clock and the river still going about its business under a sky the colour of a knife blade, and Mrs. Ada Petticrew let herself in at the side door with the key on the bit of string, and stood a moment in the passage, listening. No telephones ringing. No Mr. Craddock clearing his throat in that way he had, as though the law were lodged somewhere between his collar and his lungs. Only the lift cage hanging in its shaft like a stopped pendulum, and the slow drip of a tap, and somewhere far off the first tram groaning along the Embankment. She took off her coat, rolled her sleeves, and began.

The offices of Craddock, Bligh & Mallowby, Solicitors, occupied three floors, and it was her private opinion—never spoken, but polished and repolished in her mind over years of Thursdays and Fridays—that the firm had furnished them out of a bet. Mahogany everywhere, so dark you could drown in it; brass lamps with green glass shades; chairs that groaned when you touched them, as if they had heard things. She went through the rooms with her bucket and her cloth, and the letters lay on the desks like leaves fallen in the night.

*Dear Sir, We are in receipt of yours of the 14th inst.* She wrung out the cloth and read on, sideways, without shame. *We are instructed by our client, Mrs. Ethelwyn Tarbuck, to state…* The names came up at her out of the typewriting like faces in a crowd. Tarbuck. Quennell. The Reverend A. J. Skrine. A whole city of people who did not know that Ada Petticrew had read their troubles at six in the morning with a duster in her hand, and pitied them, and thought them mostly fools, and wished them well. She moved the paper knife an inch to the left and wiped beneath it. The ink had a smell, a cold inky smell that was the smell of the whole building; and mixed with it the beeswax, and the gas from the fire she had lit in Mr. Mallowby’s grate, and the river coming in at the windows, that greeny-brown smell of the Thames at low water.

At the window she stopped, because one always stopped. The tide was out and the mud shone, and a barge went down with its one brown sail, and the gulls—you would not credit it, gulls, in the middle of London—stood about on the mud as neat as ornaments, waiting for what the river would give them. Her son Wilfred was in Canada. He had sent a photograph at Christmas of himself standing in front of a barn, and the barn was bigger than any building she had ever entered, and his face in the photograph was a stranger’s face, broad and burnt and cheerful, and she had set it on the mantelpiece between the clock and the tin of cocoa and looked at it every evening until it seemed less like Wilfred and more like a place. *The snow is ever so deep here, Mother*, he wrote, *but it is a dry cold.* A dry cold. As if the cold in London were a wet cold, a cold that got into the bones and stayed, like the damp in the walls of the churchyard.

The churchyard. She polished the window ledge and thought of the churchyard, which was not morbid, whatever people might say; it was only that at this hour, with the river going one way and the gulls standing still, her mind went loose and drifted where it liked, and it liked the churchyard at Wren’s Hill, where the yew tree made a shadow like a hand, and where Alfred lay under the flat stone with the letters going green. *Alfred Petticrew, 1869–1908, At Rest.* She had chosen the words herself, and she had wondered since whether *At Rest* was not a little forward, as though she had spoken for him; but the mason had said it was very suitable, and it was done now, and there was no altering it. He had been a great one for the river, Alfred. He knew the barges by name. He would stand at the bridge of a Sunday with his pipe and say, There goes the *Maud Emily*, she draws too much water, and be right about it too. And now she looked at the river for both of them, and it was going out, the colour of a knife blade, and the gulls standing on the mud as if they had been set there.

She did Mr. Craddock’s room next, and it was there, under the desk, that she found the pencil. It had rolled to the very back, and she got down on her knees—at her age, on her knees, the things one does—and fished it out, a common pencil, chewed at the end, and she stood turning it in her fingers. Somebody had chewed it, thinking. Mr. Craddock himself, perhaps, sitting late over the Tarbuck business, gnawing his pencil like a schoolboy, and the great brass lamp throwing his shadow on the wall. She put it in the tray, and then took it out again and put it in her apron pocket, and could not have told you why, except that it was warm from her hand now and had been nobody’s a moment before.

The sky had opened a little, a long grey seam of light above the water, and the first of the clerks would be coming soon, stamping their feet on the step, and Mr. Bligh would put his head round the door and say, Morning, Mrs. P., and she would say, Morning, sir, and the building would begin to breathe, and the letters would be gathered up and answered, and the whole city would go on about its business. She stood a moment in the middle of the room with the duster hanging from her hand, and the light coming up the river and lying along the floor in long pale bars, and she thought of Wilfred in the dry cold and of Alfred at rest and of the pencil in her pocket, and it seemed to her that she was the only person in London who knew the building as it was before it woke—its silence, its smells, its letters, its stopped pendulum of a lift—and that this was a kind of position, a kind of office, though she had no name for it and would never have dreamed of claiming it; and then the clock on the stairs struck seven, and she tied her apron tighter and went down to the tap to rinse her cloth, because the mud was shining and the gulls were standing still and there was the whole of the third floor yet to do.
