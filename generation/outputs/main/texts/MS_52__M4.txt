The summer of eighteen hundred and twenty-three was one of uncommon heat and stillness, and the village of Ashcombe, nestled as it is between the dark shoulder of the moor and the grey indifference of the sea, lay panting beneath a sky of brass. The roses about the parsonage drooped and shed their petals untimely; the brook that runs by the mill was shrunk to a thread of silver; and the birds, which in other years had filled the noontide with their clamour, were silent as if the very air had grown too heavy for song. It was in this season of languor and of drought that my daughter Ellen formed the habit which has since filled my heart with an anguish I can scarcely name, and which I set down here, not for my own justification, for I know not that I have any, but that I might in some measure unburden a spirit oppressed beyond its strength.

Her friend, Clara Winterbourne, the only child of the physician who had attended our household through many seasons of sickness, had been taken from us in the April of that same year, after a long and wasting illness of the lungs. She was a creature of such singular sweetness, so patient in her sufferings, so full of a gentle and unearthly cheerfulness even when the hand of death was visibly upon her, that her loss seemed to us less like the common bereavement of mortality than the withdrawal of some kindly spirit from a world unworthy of its presence. Ellen had loved her with an ardour which I had ever thought beautiful, though perhaps too absolute, too entire, for the frailty of all earthly attachments; they had been as sisters in all but blood, and had grown together from infancy in that perfect confidence which knows neither reserve nor fear. When Clara died, my daughter did not weep as other mourners weep; she stood beside the grave in the little churchyard by the yew-tree, pale and composed, and spoke no word, and from that hour she seemed to me to walk in a world apart, into which I could not follow her.

It was in the early days of June that I first observed her seated at the old escritoire in her chamber, absorbed in the act of writing with a feverish diligence which I had never before remarked in her. She wrote, and paused, and wrote again, and sometimes she would smile as she wrote, and sometimes a tear would fall upon the paper, and she would press her hand against her heart as though to still some inward pang. I asked her, with the tenderness which I hoped might win her confidence, whether she was composing verses, for she had ever a turn for the muses; but she answered me with a strange and wandering look, and said that she was writing letters, and that I must not disturb her, for the post went out but twice in the week and she had much to say. I thought no more of it at the time, believing that she corresponded with some schoolfellow of her happier days; but as the summer wore on, and she still sat daily at her task, and grew paler and more abstracted, and took less and less pleasure in the walks and occupations which had once delighted her, a vague uneasiness began to take possession of my mind.

At length, upon a day in the latter part of July, when the heat had driven all the household to seek what shade they might, I entered her chamber, thinking to find her sleeping, and discovered it empty. Upon the desk lay a packet of letters, folded and directed, and, as I thought, sealed. The devil of curiosity, which lurks even in a mother's heart, prompted me to look upon the superscription; and what was my horror when I beheld, written in my daughter's hand, the name and direction of Clara Winterbourne, who had lain these four months in her grave! A cold terror seized upon me; I took up the letters, one after another, and turned them over in my trembling fingers, and saw that they were many, and that the ink upon them was of various dates, some recent, some faded as though they had been long preserved. I could not bring myself to break the seals, for they were sacred to my child's secret soul; but I stood there in the silent chamber, with the letters in my hand, and the sun streaming through the lattice upon the empty chair and the unfinished page, and I felt as if the foundations of my reason were giving way beneath me.

When Ellen returned, she found me thus, and her countenance, which had been calm, became at once agitated and confused; she sprang forward and would have snatched the letters from my grasp, crying out that they were hers, that no one had the right to touch them, that I had betrayed her. I strove to soothe her, to reason with her, to implore her to tell me what these writings meant; but she only wept with a violence which frightened me, and clasped the packet to her bosom, and declared that Clara was not dead, that she lived, that she wrote to her and received her answers, and that if I could not understand this, I must leave her to her grief and her belief. I confess that I was overcome; I sank upon the bed, and wept with her, and entreated her to have pity upon me, if not upon herself; but she would hear nothing, and at last, when her passion had spent itself, she sat down beside me and spoke more calmly, though with a wildness in her eye which I had never seen before.

She told me that from the first hour of her loss she had found no comfort in the common consolations of religion or of friendship; that the world seemed to her a vast and empty place, from which the light had gone out; and that, in her despair, she had conceived the thought of writing to her friend as though she still lived, of pouring out her soul in letters which should keep alive the intercourse that death had severed. At first, she said, it was a mere exercise of grief, a bitter mockery of her own misery; but by degrees the practice had become a necessity, and then a solace, and at last a kind of dreadful joy. She wrote to Clara of all that happened in the village, of the flowers in the garden, of the books she read, of the thoughts that came to her in the night-watches; and she found, she said, that in writing thus she could almost persuade herself that her friend was near her, that she would presently come in at the door with her gentle smile and her voice of music, and that the grave was but a dream from which she should awake. She had even, she confessed with a shudder, begun to fancy that she received replies, that in the whisper of the wind among the trees, in the song of the birds at dawn, in the silence of the twilight, she heard the accents of Clara's voice, and that these were the answers to her letters, and that thus a communion was established between them which death itself could not interrupt.

I listened to this recital with a heart torn between pity and alarm; and when she had done, I took her hand, and spoke to her as a mother may speak, with all the tenderness and all the solemnity that I could command. I reminded her that the dead are not permitted to return; that the veil which separates us from them is not to be rent by the hands of the living; that to indulge in such imaginations was to cherish a delusion which, however sweet, must in the end destroy the mind that harboured it. I implored her, by her love for me, by her duty to her Maker, by the memory of her friend, who would surely be the first to warn her from a path so perilous, to abandon this practice, to give up the letters, to turn her thoughts to the duties and the hopes of the living. She heard me in silence; and when I had finished, she kissed my hand, and said that she would try to obey me, and that she was sorry she had caused me pain. But I saw that her compliance was but the submission of the moment, and that in her secret heart she clung to her delusion as the drowning man clings to the plank which is his only hope.

Since that day I have watched her with an anxiety which knows no rest. She no longer writes with the same openness; she has hidden her letters, or perhaps destroyed them, for I cannot tell; but she is more silent than before, and her eyes have a look of abstraction which appals me. Sometimes I find her in the garden, standing by the rose-tree which Clara loved, with her lips moving as if in speech, and a smile upon her face which is not of this world. The physician, when I consulted him, shook his head, and spoke of a mind overwrought by grief, and of the necessity of change of scene and of cheerful society; but I know not how to accomplish this, for we are not rich, and my husband is a man of settled habits, and Ellen herself shrinks from the world with a morbid dread which it is impossible to overcome. And so the summer passes, and the harvest draws on, and I sit by my child's bedside at night, and listen to her breathing, and pray that God may preserve her reason, and that the shadow which has fallen upon her spirit may be lifted before it be too late. But my prayers, I fear, are weak and wandering; for I am but a woman, and my heart is torn with a double grief, and I know not whether I am not myself becoming a partner in her delusion, when I find myself sometimes, in the solitude of my own chamber, addressing words to the dead Clara, as though she could hear me, and imploring her, if the spirits of the departed have any knowledge of the things of earth, to look down upon her friend and to save her from the abyss which yawns before her.
