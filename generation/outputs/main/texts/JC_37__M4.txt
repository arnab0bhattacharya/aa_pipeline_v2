The affair of the *Bride of Fowey*—if I may call it so, for she was a brig of no great tonnage but of a name that clung to her memory like a burr—had passed from the columns of the local press and from the loose talk of the quayside long before I came to know Elias Gann, and yet it was the shadow of that old business, I am convinced, which lay upon him in the years of our acquaintance like the shadow of a cloud upon a field, moving with him, darkening whatever he touched. I had come down to Porthcurnick in those days—a town of steep streets and narrow alleys that seemed to have been built by men in a hurry and abandoned by them in a sulk, where the smell of tar and fish and the wet stone of the harbour wall was the very breath of the place—and there I took lodgings above a chandler's shop, and there I first saw Gann sitting on the sea-wall of an evening, a heavy, silent man with a face like a weathered beam, staring out at the water as though it held some answer he had long since ceased to expect.

He was a seaman, or had been, and the manner of his life ashore was that of a man who has never quite learned to be idle. He mended nets for the fishermen though he had no boat of his own; he carried crates for the merchants though he had no stake in their trade; and he drank his grog at the *Three Anchors* with a deliberation that suggested he was measuring each glass against some private reckoning of his own. The men of the town respected him and kept their distance, which is a combination of attitudes not uncommon in ports where a man's history is known to all and spoken of by none. It was only by degrees, and through the slow accumulation of those small confidences which are the currency of lonely men, that I came to understand what it was that Gann carried with him, and why he sat of an evening on the sea-wall with his eyes fixed upon the western horizon as though he expected something to come out of it—or feared that it might.

The matter, as he told it to me at last, in the halting manner of a man who has rehearsed a story many times in his own mind but never permitted it to cross his lips, concerned a voyage of the *Bride of Fowey* from the coast of Brazil to the home port, in the course of which the captain, a man named Hallett, had run her upon a reef off the Scillies in a fog that was not so thick as his own obstinacy. There had been an inquiry, as there always is, and Gann, who was the mate and had been on deck at the time, had sworn that the fog had come down sudden and without warning, that the captain had done all that seamanship could devise, and that the loss of the brig was the act of God and not of any man. It was a lie, or something so near to a lie that the distinction had long since ceased to comfort him. The fog had been visible from the masthead for a full hour before Hallett would consent to shorten sail, and Gann had known it, and had said nothing, because he was a young man then and Hallett was a hard man and the sea is a hard school and a mate who crosses his captain in mid-voyage may find himself discharged in a foreign port with no wages and no passage home. So he had held his tongue, and later he had opened his mouth, and the words that came out of it had saved Hallett's certificate and his own berth and, as Gann put it to me with a kind of bitter emphasis, his own soul, though not in the sense that the parsons mean.

Hallett had been acquitted, of course, and had gone on to command other ships, and Gann had gone with him for a time, and then had parted from him—not in anger, for there had been no anger between them, but in that slow and silent divergence which comes to men who have shared a secret they do not speak of. Gann had drifted from port to port, from ship to ship, and had come at last to Porthcurnick, where he had thought to end his days in the obscurity which he felt to be his due. And then, some five years before I knew him, there had come a letter from a solicitor in Fowey, informing him that Captain Hallett had died at sea—of a fever, in the Mozambique Channel—and that he had left to his former mate, Elias Gann, the sum of two hundred pounds, in token of his gratitude and esteem.

I remember the evening he told me this, for it was one of those grey and windless evenings that come upon the Cornish coast in autumn, when the sea and the sky are of the same colour and the whole world seems to be holding its breath. We were sitting on the sea-wall, and Gann had his pipe in his hand and was looking at it as though it were some strange object he had never seen before. "Two hundred pounds," he said, slowly. "It is not a fortune, but it is not nothing, either. It would keep me ashore for a year, or it would buy me a share in a lugger, or it would—" He stopped, and put the pipe in his mouth, and took it out again. "It would do many things," he said.

"And you have not taken it?" I asked.

"I have not taken it," he said. "The solicitor has written to me three times, and the last time he wrote that if I did not claim it within the year it would go to the residuary legatee, whoever that may be, and I wrote back that I would come to Fowey and see him, and I have not gone. That was two years ago. The money is still there, I suppose. Or it is not. I do not know. I do not know that I care."

He was silent for a long time, and I did not press him, for I had learned by then that Gann's silences were not empty but full, and that whatever was working in him would come to the surface in its own season. At last he said, in a voice that was almost inaudible against the lap of the water against the wall: "He meant it kindly. That is the thing I cannot get past. He meant it kindly. He thought he was doing me a service, and perhaps he was, and perhaps that is why I cannot touch it. It is not the money. It is the kindness. It is the gratitude. He was grateful to me for what I did, and what I did was to lie for him, and he knew it, and he was grateful. And if I take his money I am saying that the lie was worth it, that the lie was a service, that the lie was—" He stopped again, and made a gesture with his pipe that seemed to take in the whole grey expanse of sea and sky. "I have done many things in my life," he said, "that I would not do again, but I have never taken payment for them. That is the last thing. That is the thing I have kept."

I asked him, then, what he would do, and he said that he did not know, and that perhaps he would go to Fowey and see the solicitor and refuse the money in person, and that perhaps he would not, and that perhaps the money would go to the residuary legatee and that would be an end of it. But I could see that this was not an end of it, and that the thing would go on working in him, as it had been working in him for years, and that the two hundred pounds, whether he took it or left it, would remain what it had always been—not a legacy but a judgment, not a gift but a sentence, pronounced upon him by a dead man who had meant only to be kind.

I left Porthcurnick not long after that, and I did not see Gann again. But I have thought of him often, sitting on the sea-wall with his pipe in his hand and his eyes fixed upon the western horizon, and I have wondered whether he ever went to Fowey, and whether he took the money, and whether, in the end, he found some way of accepting it that did not feel like a betrayal of the thing he had kept. I do not know. I only know that there are men for whom the truth is not a matter of words but of the whole texture of their lives, and that when they have once departed from it, even for the sake of a kindness, even for the sake of a friend, they carry the departure with them as a wound that will not heal, and that no amount of money, however kindly meant, can ever make it right.
