The kettle had been singing for some minutes—a thin, spiralling note that rose through the musty rooms like a thread drawn through fabric, pulling everything with it. And the roses on the mantelpiece, which Eleanor had cut that morning and set in the Chinese vase with its chipped lip, were already dropping their petals, one by one, as if they too had heard and could not bear what had been said.

"I shall leave on Tuesday," she had said, at breakfast, while her father was cutting his toast into the four exact triangles he preferred, and the morning light, which was grey and diffuse and had in it something of the particular greyness of Kensington in October, fell across the tablecloth where a small stain of marmalade had dried into a hard amber crescent. She had said it looking not at him but at the stain, or rather through it, the way one looks through a window when one is about to say something that has been forming for years in the deep and undisturbed sediment of the mind, where words lie like coins at the bottom of a fountain, darkened but legible. And he had stopped, the knife suspended, and the triangle of toast remained incomplete, which was perhaps the most remarkable thing, for he had not failed to complete his toast triangles in nineteen years, not since the year after her mother died, when the habit had established itself with the permanence of a geological formation.

"Leave," he said, and the word hung between them, and then settled, the way a leaf settles on still water, creating the faintest of rings.

"Rooms," she said. "In Bloomsbury. I have found them. Three rooms on the top floor of a house in Tavistock Square, and there is a bath on the landing which is shared, but only with a clerk who is out all day." She had not meant to say about the bath; it was a detail, and the detail had rushed forward with the eagerness of a small animal escaping through a gap in a hedge, and she watched it go with something between alarm and relief. "I shall take in typing," she said. "Mrs. Marlowe has told me I may have work from the publisher she sits for. And there is the reviewing, if Papa thinks—"

But her father had returned to his toast. He completed the triangle with a single decisive stroke and placed the knife parallel to the edge of his plate, exactly as he placed it every morning, and he said nothing, which was worse than speaking, because his silences had always been more articulate than other men's speeches, being the silences of a man who had spent forty years reconstructing the diplomatic history of the eighteenth century from correspondence that other historians had declared illegible or irrelevant. He had found, in those letters, the very shape of an age; and now his daughter sat across from him in a breakfast room that smelled of cold toast and coal smoke and the faintly mineral odour of the rubber plant in its brass pot, and she was telling him that she meant to leave, and the eighteenth century, with its clear alignments of power and obligation, its men who went to Vienna and signed treaties and its women who stayed in country houses and wrote letters by candlelight, offered no precedent for this at all.

He thought of the passage in the third volume of his History where he had described the Countess of Marchmont's management of her household while her husband was at Utrecht. He had written it with a particular tenderness that had surprised even himself, dwelling on the way she had ordered the linen, supervised the children's Latin, and sat in the long gallery at Strathfield reading the letters that arrived every fortnight, their seals cracked and their pages crossed and recrossed with a news that was always three weeks old. It had seemed to him, writing it, a picture of perfect completeness, of a woman enclosed in a design that gave her shape and meaning, as a frame gives shape and meaning to a portrait; and he had not known, or had not wished to know, that the Countess, in her recrossed letters, had once written a sentence—discovered by him in a private collection in Edinburgh and suppressed as irrelevant to the diplomatic record—which ran: *I would give all the tapestry in the long gallery for one hour in which no one asks anything of me at all.*

For he had suppressed it, as one suppresses a cough in a cathedral, feeling that it belonged to another order of enquiry, one that could not be pursued without disturbing the architecture of the whole. And now it returned to him, floating up through the chambers of his mind with a buoyancy that suggested it had never truly sunk, while Eleanor buttered the completed triangle and the light shifted and the kettle's note rose to a thin wail that nobody attended to, until it stopped, and the silence was worse than the sound.

Aunt Agatha arrived at half past two, having taken the omnibus from Notting Hill Gate, a journey she described as having aged her by a decade. She wore her velvet cape and carried her reticule with the air of a woman approaching a sickbed, and when she was shown into the drawing room she did not sit but stood before the fire, which was not yet lit, and said, "Eleanor, I have come from Maud, who is prostrate."

Prostrate was the word they used for Maud, who had taken to her sofa in Ladbroke Grove upon hearing the news, having received it by telephone that morning in a voice that had cracked twice during the transmission, so that she had not been certain whether Eleanor had said rooms or ruins, and had spent the intervening hours imagining her niece in a state of dereliction that belonged, properly, to a Russian novel.

"She cannot mean it," Agatha said, turning to her brother-in-law, who had risen from his armchair with the slow formality of a man receiving an envoy from a foreign court. "The girl cannot mean it. A young woman alone, in that part of London—I have heard it said that there are Italians in Tavistock Square."

"There are clerks," said Eleanor, from the window, where she had been standing with her back to the room, looking at the plane tree in the square whose leaves were turning and whose uppermost branches caught a pale and fugitive sunlight that had no warmth in it, only a luminous, exhausted beauty, the beauty of a thing that is about to let go. "There are clerks and a schoolmaster and a woman who paints miniatures, and the landlady is a widow from Newcastle who keeps the stairs scrubbed."

"Scrubbed stairs!" said Agatha, as if this confirmed her worst suspicions.

And the conversation might have continued in this way, circling and diving like a bird over water without ever breaking the surface, had not Eleanor turned from the window and said, with a clarity that surprised even herself, so that the words seemed to have been spoken by someone standing just behind her shoulder, someone she had not known was there: "I am thirty-one. I have kept this house for eleven years. I have ordered the linen and supervised the coal and sat with Papa while he worked, and I have done it willingly, or at least without examining the nature of my willingness, which may be the same thing, or may not, and I have arrived at an age where the question of what I am for presses itself upon me with a force that I can no longer deflect by polishing the brass."

The brass, in fact, gleamed. She had polished it that very morning, and it shone now in the grey light with a lustre that seemed, momentarily, to mock her, so that she felt the pull of the old obedience, the old satisfaction in the shining surface, and had to set her teeth against it.

Her father looked at her. He saw a woman who resembled his wife in the particular angle at which she held her head when she was resolved upon something, and this resemblance, which had comforted him for nineteen years as a proof that the best of Eleanor had survived, now struck him as something more complicated, a proof that she had perhaps been living inside an imitation of a life that had not been her own, and that the imitation had been so faithful that even he had been deceived. He thought of the Countess of Marchmont, and the sentence he had suppressed, and he understood, with the slow understanding of a man who has spent his life among documents, that what he had called irrelevance had been, in fact, the point.

"You will want books," he said, after a silence so long that Agatha had opened her mouth twice and closed it again. "If you are to review—" and he stopped, because the sentence had begun to carry him in a direction he had not intended, toward an admission that the plan might have substance, that rooms in Tavistock Square might contain a life, that the Countess might have had her hour after all; and he drew back from it, but not entirely, so that he stood on the threshold of the sentence like a man in a doorway, half in and half out, while the October light faded and the plane tree shed a leaf which turned once in the air before settling on the iron railing below with a sound that was barely, but distinctly, audible.
