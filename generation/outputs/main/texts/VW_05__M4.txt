The gas-jets in the corridor had a way of gasping—a small, dry, catching of the breath, as though the building itself were nervous—and Miss Thorne, coming up the stone stairs with her bundle of exercise books, heard them and thought, as she did every Thursday, that she must speak to Mr. Pemberton about the mantles; and knew, as she did every Thursday, that she would not. The smell was of chalk and coal-smoke and the faint, sweetish damp of the cloakroom where the men hung their caps. Thursday. The best evening of the week, she had once said to her sister, meaning only that the men were more attentive on Thursdays, that the class had begun to cohere; and her sister, who was not clever, had said, "You work too hard, Edith," and gone on with her mending.

He sat in the third row, always, at the end nearest the window—Hollis, the printer—with his broad, inked hands flat upon the desk as though the desk were a press and he were steadying it. A fair man, going grey at the temples, with a slow way of lifting his eyes. When she read from *The Deserted Village* he had looked at her, not at the book, and she had felt the words come out of her mouth strangely, as if she were reading them for the first time, and had faltered—actually faltered—on the line about the hollow-sounding bittern, and had covered it with a question about the metre, which no one had answered.

Tonight it was the essay. "The use of leisure," she said. "Two hundred words. Think of the hours after the shop closes. What are they for?" And they bent their heads, the six of them, and the pens scratched; and she walked between the desks with her hands behind her back, and the rain on the high windows made a soft, steady hiss, so that the room seemed to be moving through water. She stopped by the window. Below, the lamplit street glistened; a cart went by; the wet cobbles held the light in long, broken streaks, and a woman with a shawl over her head stood under the lamp as though waiting for someone who would not come. Then she turned and looked at the back of Hollis's neck, at the line where his hair met his collar, and a wave of something—not thought, not quite feeling, a sort of dark warmth—went through her, and she thought: I am forty-one years old. And: this is ridiculous. And: I shall not look at him again tonight.

But at the end, when the others had gone clattering down the stairs, he lingered, as he sometimes did, stacking the chairs; and she stood at her table sorting the books, and the gas-jet over her head made its small nervous catch, and he came forward with something under his arm.

"A book," he said. "I printed it. At the works. After hours."

She took it. A small book, bound in grey boards, with her name—no, not her name; the title, *The Quiet Hours*—stamped in gold upon the cover, and inside, on the title page, the name of the press, which was his own name, and the date. The paper was thick and rough, the letters sharp and black, standing up from the page as though they had been pressed there by a strong hand. She turned the leaves. Poems. Not his, she saw—no, the poems of a man named Ellery, dead these twenty years, a Northamptonshire shoemaker, and a note at the front in Hollis's plain, upright hand, saying where the manuscript had been found and how it had come to him.

"You did this yourself?" she said.

"Every page," he said. "The type is old Caslon. I set it at night, when the machines are quiet." He paused, and she heard him breathe. "I thought—you spoke of the village poets, in the autumn. Of how they were lost. I thought you might—" and then he stopped, and looked at the window, at the rain, and said, "It's nothing much. Only it seemed a pity."

She held the book and did not know what to say, because the book was warm from being under his arm, and because it seemed to her that he had given her something and had not said what it was, and she could not ask. A wife, she thought. Three children. A house in Kentish Town with a yard and a pump. She saw it all in a flash, the whole of his life, and her own life—the lodgings in Gower Street, the sister, the gas-fire, the cocoa at ten—and the two lives stood side by side, quite separate, like two houses in a terrace that share a wall and never speak through it.

"Thank you," she said. "I shall read it."

"Please," he said, and made a small gesture with his inked fingers, as though to say it was not worth reading, or not worth thanking, or perhaps both; and then he took his cap from the peg and went out, and she heard his boots on the stairs, going down, down, and the door at the bottom, and the rain.

She sat down at her table. The gas-jet sang. The exercise books lay in their bundle, and the clock on the wall above the blackboard, which was always three minutes fast, said ten past nine. She opened the book at random and read: *The hedges are cut in the winter, and the cut places bleed.* And she closed it and put it in her bag, and sat with her hands folded, and thought of nothing at all; and then, all at once, she saw him again as he had stood there, the grey light of the corridor falling on his face, and the words he had not said hung in the room like smoke, like the smell of the gas, like the rain on the glass—and she thought, with a curious, cold clearness: he means nothing. He means the book. He means a kindness to a dead shoemaker. And again: he means something. He means me to know. And again: he does not know himself what he means.

She put on her coat and her hat and turned down the gas, and went out into the wet street, and the rain was fine and steady, and the lamps made their long broken streaks on the cobbles, and she walked to the corner and waited for the omnibus, and held her bag against her side, where the book was, and felt it there, a small square warmth, all the way home.
