The wind from the moor came down upon Millwood like a beast seeking its prey, rattling the narrow lattice of my lodgings with a fury that suited the turbulence of my own spirit. I had spoken that morning with a zeal not wholly my own; a fire had been kindled in my heart, and the words had poured from my lips as scalding water pours from a crucible. I had looked down from the pulpit of St. Jude’s upon the bowed heads of my congregation, and I had denounced the oppression of the poor. I had spoken of the mill, of the pale, wasted faces of the children who laboured within its walls, of the grinding avarice that starved the labourer to fatten the master. I had spoken truth, and I had spoken it directly to the face of Mr. Thornton, the owner of that vast, smoking manufactory, who sat in his pew with a countenance of dark, impenetrable granite. But my gaze had not rested on him alone. Beside him sat his daughter, Eleanor; and it was upon her that my eyes had lingered, even as my tongue delivered its unwelcome message. She had looked up, and I had seen in her grey eyes a gleam of profound and sorrowful intelligence—a look that told me she recognized the scripture I wielded, and acknowledged its justice, even as it condemned her own father.

I was a poor curate, with no fortune but my living, and no name but that of my calling. I was plain of face, awkward in figure, and burdened with a shy and hesitating disposition that made every social intercourse a torment to me. Yet the very weakness of my nature seemed to compel me toward a strength I did not possess; I was driven to speak what I felt to be right, even when my voice trembled and my hands grew cold with the effort. It was this contradiction in my character that had brought me to Millwood, and it was this same contradiction that now left me pacing my narrow chamber, consumed by a passion I dared not name.

I had not long to wait before the object of my thoughts presented herself. I had wandered out onto the moor, seeking the bleak solace of its open expanse, when I perceived a solitary figure approaching along the rough path. It was Eleanor Thornton. She wore a dark cloak, the hood of which was drawn about her face against the wind, but I knew her step, and I knew the proud, gentle carriage of her head. She stopped when she saw me, and I saw a faint colour rise in her cheeks—whether from the cold or from some emotion I could not tell.

“Mr. Leigh,” she said, and her voice, though low, carried clearly above the keening of the wind. “I have come to speak with you. I could not leave what passed this morning without a word.”

“I spoke as my conscience dictated, Miss Thornton,” I replied, and my voice, I am ashamed to say, was neither steady nor commanding. It had the harsh, strained quality of a man who feels himself at once justified and exposed.

“I know it,” she said. “I do not come to rebuke you. I come to tell you—” She paused, and her eyes, those clear, searching eyes, met mine with a directness that unmanned me. “I come to tell you that I agreed with every word you spoke.”

The confession, so simply and so bravely made, struck me silent. She stood before me on that wild, desolate moor, the daughter of the man I had publicly accused, and she confessed that his guilt was even as I had painted it. I saw the struggle in her face—the filial piety warring with the stern dictate of conscience—and I loved her. I loved her then, as I had never loved any creature, with a sudden, consuming fire that seemed to seize upon the very marrow of my being. It was not a gentle feeling, not a soft and tender inclination; it was a violent, almost painful awakening, as though some iron hand had closed upon my heart and would not release it.

“You are very courageous to say so,” I murmured.

“I am only honest,” she returned. “You spoke of mercy and of justice, Mr. Leigh. I was reared in the shadow of the mill, and I have seen what it does to those who serve it. Do you think I am blind? Do you think a daughter can look upon the suffering of the poor and feel nothing?”

She spoke with a quiet intensity that matched, in its own way, the fervour of my pulpit denunciation. There was in her no theatrical display, no sentimental excess; only a firm, resolute conviction that lent her words the weight of scripture itself. I felt, standing there beside her, that I was in the presence of a soul kindred to my own—perchance a stronger soul, for she bore her knowledge of wrong in a silence I could not emulate.

In the days that followed, I sought her out, or rather, I found myself drawn to her by an attraction I was powerless to resist. We met by chance—or by providence—in the lanes about the parish, or at the door of the Sunday school where she taught the children of the mill workers. Each meeting deepened the wound. I learned the goodness of her heart, the breadth of her understanding, the quiet fortitude with which she endured the isolation of her position in her father’s house. She was alone among her own people; her father, a man of iron will and narrow vision, tolerated no dissent within his walls. She bore his tyranny with a patience that shamed my own restless indignation.

It was inevitable that I should at last declare myself. I did so in a manner most unworthy of the depth of my feeling—haltingly, clumsily, with the words tumbling over one another like stones loosened by a torrent. We stood in the churchyard, beneath the bare branches of an ancient yew, and the evening light fell grey and cold upon the graves around us. I told her that I loved her; that I loved her not with a fleeting, superficial fancy, but with the whole force of my being; that I believed God had made us for one another, and that I asked nothing but the privilege of devoting my life to her service.

She heard me in silence. I saw her hands tremble, and I saw the tears gather in her eyes—tears she resolutely kept from falling. When I had finished, she looked at me long and searchingly, and I read in her face the answer before she spoke it.

“I believe you, Mr. Leigh,” she said. “I believe you, and I honour you. But I cannot be your wife.”

“Why?” I demanded, and my voice broke with the violence of my disappointment. “Because I am poor? Because I have nothing to offer you but a curate’s stipend and a heart full of love?”

“No,” she said, and her voice was steady, though I saw the cost of her composure in the whiteness of her face. “Not because you are poor. I do not value the world’s goods. But my father—” She stopped, and her lips compressed into a line of pain. “My father is my father. He is a hard man, and I have judged his actions severely, as you know. But I cannot set myself against him in this. He would never consent, and I will not marry against his wishes. I will not bring that strife upon you, nor upon my own conscience. Honour forbids it.”

“Honour!” I cried. “Is it honour to sacrifice your happiness and mine to a man who—”

“Do not speak against him,” she interrupted, and a flash of something fierce and protective kindled in her eyes. “I have condemned his deeds, but I will not condemn his person to another. He is my father. I owe him obedience in this, even where I cannot owe him obedience in conscience.”

I was silenced. I saw that her resolution was founded not upon weakness, but upon a strength I could not assail. She had weighed the matter in the balance of her own moral sense, and she had found her duty. I could not shake that foundation, for it was the same foundation upon which my own convictions rested—the same stern allegiance to the dictates of right.

I turned from her and walked back across the moor, and the wind tore at me with a savage joy, as though it revelled in my desolation. The mill smoked in the valley below, a dark monument to the greed I had denounced, and I thought of Eleanor within its shadow, faithful to a duty that condemned her to solitude. I loved her; I should love her until the day God saw fit to silence this beating heart; but I was shut out from her life as completely as if a wall of iron divided us. I had spoken truth from the pulpit, and the truth had cost me the only happiness I had ever desired.
