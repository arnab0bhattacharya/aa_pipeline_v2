In that obscure and odorous region of Clerkenwell where the daylight seems always to be struggling through a fog of its own making, and where the very bricks appear to have absorbed the melancholy of a thousand rainy seasons, there stood—or rather, there squatted—a rag-and-bone yard belonging to one Mr. Grottle. It was not a yard in the sense of a tidy enclosure, but a wilderness of refuse, a chaotic empire of decay, presided over by Mr. Grottle in life, and, as it now appeared, in death also.

Mr. Grottle had been a miser of the most rigid and unyielding description—a man who loved gold with a passion that transcended the love of humanity, and who hoarded it in places where only the rats and the spiders were privy to the secret. He had expired, not in his bed, as becomes a respectable citizen, but in the very heart of his dominion, seated upon a throne of compressed rags, with a bone in one hand and a half-guinea in the other, as if to illustrate the fleeting nature of earthly possessions. It was young Peg Flinders, the servant girl who had nursed him through his final, wheezing illness with a patience that would have shamed an angel, who discovered him thus, stiff and cold, a monument to his own parsimony.

The news spread through the labyrinthine alleys of Clerkenwell with the speed of a fire in a powder-magazine. Neighbours, who in Mr. Grottle’s lifetime would not have crossed his threshold for all the tea in China, now descended upon the yard like a swarm of locusts upon a field of wheat. There was Mrs. Crumpet, the laundress, whose face was perpetually flushed with the steam of her labours and the heat of her indignation; there was Mr. Tibbs, the cobbler, a man with a twitch in his left eye that seemed to signal his every avaricious thought; and there were a dozen others, men and women of the lower sort, with eyes that glittered in the gloom like those of hungry wolves.

“He’s gone, Mrs. Crumpet, he’s gone!” cried Mr. Tibbs, rubbing his hands together with a dry, rasping sound. “And where is the money, that’s what I say! Where is the money?”

“Where indeed!” echoed Mrs. Crumpet, casting a scornful glance at the piles of filth. “Buried in this muck, I’ll be bound. He loved it better than his own soul, the wretch!”

And so the search began—a frantic, undignified scramble through the heaps of rags, the mountains of bones, the drifts of broken crockery and rusted iron. It was a scene of comic pandemonium, a tableau of greed that would have made a satirist weep with joy. Men who had not bent their backs for a twelvemonth now toiled like galley-slaves, tossing aside filthy garments and gnawed femurs in their desperate quest for lucre. Women, with their skirts tucked up and their bonnets askew, plunged their hands into the slime, unmindful of the stench, unmindful of everything but the phantom of hidden wealth.

“Here’s a sixpence!” shouted one, holding aloft a coin tarnished by the damp earth.

“Liar! It’s a button!” retorted another, and a scuffle ensued.

Through this chaos moved Peg Flinders, a small, pale girl with large, sorrowful eyes that seemed to take in the whole sordid spectacle with a quiet, heartbreaking understanding. She had loved the old man in her own simple way, not for his gold, but for the moments of lucidity when he had spoken to her of his boyhood, long before the love of money had hardened his heart into a stone. Now, she watched her neighbours desecrating the place where he had lived and died, and she felt a pity that was almost too heavy to bear.

Suddenly, a cry went up from the far corner of the yard, near the rotting fence where the nettles grew highest. It was Mr. Tibbs who had found something—a small, iron strong-box, buried deep beneath a drift of oyster shells. The mob converged upon him with a roar, their faces distorted by a frenzy that was half hope, half madness.

“Break it open!” screamed Mrs. Crumpet. “Break it open, or I’ll break your head, you thief!”

Mr. Tibbs, trembling with excitement and fear, applied a crowbar to the lid. The lock, weakened by rust and time, gave way with a groan, and the box was thrown open. But it was empty—save for a single, mocking mouse-nest.

A groan of disappointment went through the crowd, a collective exhalation of thwarted avarice. The frenzy subsided into a sullen, simmering resentment. They looked at one another with suspicion, each wondering who had been there before them, who had stolen the treasure they felt was rightfully theirs.

It was then that Peg Flinders spoke. Her voice, though soft, cut through the murmur of the crowd like a silver bell in a belfry.

“You are looking in the wrong place,” she said.

Every eye turned to her. Mr. Tibbs narrowed his gaze. “And what do you know of it, girl? You’re just a servant. He wouldn’t have told you his secrets.”

“He told me everything,” Peg replied, her chin lifting slightly. “He told me that he didn’t trust the banks, and he didn’t trust the ground. He trusted only one thing.”

“And what was that?” demanded Mrs. Crumpet, stepping forward.

Peg pointed a slender finger towards the center of the yard, to a heap that looked no different from any other—a mound of old sacks, rotting straw, and discarded petticoats. “There,” she said. “The old horse. The one he stuffed with straw and set up to scare the birds. He said it was the only honest thing in the yard, because it never asked for anything.”

The crowd stared at the grotesque effigy—a sagging, headless shape made of canvas and sacking, propped up on a wooden stake. It was a ridiculous object, a thing of mockery and decay. But as Peg walked towards it, the crowd fell back, their greed warring with a superstitious awe.

She did not touch the straw, nor the sacking. Instead, she reached into the hollow neck of the horse, where the canvas was torn and frayed. Her hand disappeared into the darkness of the stuffing. There was a moment of breathless silence, a silence so profound that one could hear the scratching of the rats in the walls of the adjacent tenements.

Then, she withdrew her hand. She was not holding coins, nor banknotes. She was holding a small, oilskin packet, bound with twine.

She held it up for them to see. “He said this was for me,” she said, her voice trembling slightly. “For nursing him. For being kind when the world was not.”

She did not open it. She did not need to. The weight of it, the secrecy of it, spoke volumes. The crowd surged forward, their faces ugly with greed, their hands outstretched.

“It’s ours!” cried Mr. Tibbs. “It belongs to the estate! Give it here, you thief!”

But Peg Flinders, small as she was, stood her ground. She clutched the packet to her breast, her eyes flashing with a fire that none of them had seen before.

“You have had your fill of his bones and his rags!” she cried out, her voice ringing with indignation. “You have picked his pockets while he lay cold! But this—this is the one thing he kept for a human heart, and you shall not have it!”

And with that, she turned and ran. She darted through the gap in the fence, a small, swift figure vanishing into the labyrinth of the London streets, leaving the mob to their filth and their fury, to their empty boxes and their broken dreams. And as they stood there, bewildered and cheated, the old horse seemed to sag a little lower, as if relieved that its secret, at last, was safe.
