It was a truth universally acknowledged in the parish of St. Chad’s, Helpringham, that Mr. Amos Pettlebury, the miller, doted on his son. The boy, Benjamin, was a handsome, quick-limbed lad of twelve, with a laugh that rang like a bell across the water-meadows, and a way of tossing back his flaxen hair that seemed to say the world was a fine place and he its favoured tenant. His father, a man of slow speech and ponderous build, whose face was a map of honest toil, would watch him with a satisfaction so profound it was almost solemn; and when he spoke of Ben’s future—of the mill, the land, the place in the county that the boy would one day hold—it was with the air of a man reciting a creed.

The elder sister, Ruth, was of a different make. She was two years Benjamin’s senior, a quiet, observant girl with her mother’s grey eyes and a habit of stillness that some mistook for dullness. She had never been her father’s favourite. That was a fact she had long since ceased to resent, having arrived, by that slow process of moral arithmetic which is the peculiar work of unregarded children, at the conclusion that love, like the sun, must fall where it will, and that it was no more her brother’s fault that he was beloved than it was her own that she was not. She loved him, too, in her fashion—loved him with a protecting tenderness that was half maternal, for she had watched over his infant steps and mended his small grievances since before she could properly spell her own name.

It was in the autumn of the year 1824 that the trouble came. The school at Helpringham was kept by a Mr. Cattermole, a thin, precise man with a black coat and a blacker temper, who taught the sons of the parish the rudiments of Latin and the fear of God in nearly equal proportions. Among the boys was one Tobias Grit, a heavy, sullen lad, the son of a prosperous grazier, who was understood to be the school’s chief dunce and its chief miser. It was Tobias who, one November afternoon, discovered that the half-crown his father had given him for the purchase of a new Virgil was missing from his satchel; and it was Benjamin Pettlebury who, having been seen near the coat-pegs at the noon hour, was accused of the theft.

The accusation was not made loudly. Mr. Cattermole, who had a horror of scandal, conducted his inquiry in the little parlour behind the schoolroom, with the door shut and the blinds drawn. But secrets in a village are like water in a leaky boat: they will find their way. By evening the story had reached the mill, and Mr. Pettlebury, who had been in the midst of his accounts, rose from his desk with a face of thunder.

‘Ben!’ he called, and the boy came, white and trembling, from the kitchen where he had been sitting with his mother.

‘Did you take it?’ said the father, fixing his eyes upon his son.

‘No, father,’ said Benjamin, and his voice, though it shook, had in it a note of injured innocence that was very convincing. ‘I never touched it. I was by the pegs, but I never touched his bag. I swear it.’

Mr. Pettlebury looked at him for a long moment. Then he turned to his daughter, who had come in from the dairy and stood by the door, her hands still wet with the morning’s cream.

‘Ruth,’ he said, ‘you were at the school this afternoon. You took the boy his dinner. Did you see anything? Did you see him take it?’

The question fell into the room like a stone into still water. Ruth felt the colour rise to her cheeks, and then recede, leaving her cold. She had seen. She had seen her brother, thinking himself unobserved, slip his hand into Tobias Grit’s satchel and draw out the coin; she had seen him look at it with a kind of wondering greed, and then thrust it into his own pocket. She had said nothing at the time, hoping she had been mistaken, hoping some explanation would present itself. But none had.

‘Ruth!’ said her father, more sharply. ‘Answer me. Did you see him take it?’

She looked at Benjamin. His eyes were fixed on her with an expression she had never seen before—a desperate, pleading look, as of a creature caught in a trap. And she knew, with a certainty that was like a physical pain, that if she spoke the truth, she would break her father’s heart. She would take from him the thing he loved best in the world, the image he had cherished of his son’s honour, and she would do it with her own lips. And Benjamin, her Benjamin, would be branded a thief, and the brand would never leave him.

‘I—’ she began, and stopped.

‘You saw nothing?’ said her father, and there was a hope in his voice that was almost an entreaty.

Ruth thought of the truth. She thought of it as a thing apart from herself, a hard, bright, terrible thing that would cut whatever it touched. She thought of her father’s face when he knew. She thought of her brother’s life, blighted in its opening bud. And she thought, with a sudden, sickening clarity, that she was being asked to choose between two kinds of loyalty, and that whichever she chose, something would be lost.

‘I saw him by the pegs,’ she said, slowly, and her voice sounded strange to her own ears, as if it came from a great distance. ‘But I did not see him take anything. I was in a hurry. I did not look closely.’

It was not a lie. It was a truth so pared and qualified that it had the shape of a lie, and she knew it. She saw the relief flood her father’s face, saw the tension go out of Benjamin’s shoulders, and she felt a shame that was worse than any punishment.

Mr. Pettlebury went to the school the next morning and spoke to Mr. Cattermole in a voice that brooked no contradiction. The matter was dropped, though not forgotten. Tobias Grit was caned for carelessness, and Benjamin Pettlebury held his head high among his fellows. But Ruth, watching from the window as her brother ran off to school, felt a new and bitter knowledge settle in her heart. She had saved him, and in saving him she had lost something she could not name. She had learned that the truth is a luxury the loving cannot always afford, and that the price of a father’s happiness may be a daughter’s peace.
