I had been but three weeks at Thruscross when Mrs. Ellerby first required of me the keeping of a diary. The house stands low among the water-meadows north of York, a grey stone house with many chimneys, and the river runs so near its garden wall that on still nights one hears the water muttering among the reeds like a secret that will not be kept. It was a house of long corridors and sudden draughts, where the candles guttered though no window stood open, and where I was often sensible, though I could not have proved it, of being watched.

My charge was Miss Adela, a child of nine years, pale and quick and given to long silences, who would sit at her sums with her pen arrested and her eyes fixed upon the middle distance, as if she listened to some discourse inaudible to me. I loved her from the first, with that fierce, anxious love which a dependent woman gives to the creature committed to her care; and I believe she loved me, after her fashion, though she never prattled as children do, and would sometimes withdraw her hand from mine as though she had remembered a prohibition.

“You will keep a record of her improvement,” said Mrs. Ellerby to me one afternoon, in the small parlour that looked upon the river. “Her father will wish to see it. He is much abroad — much abroad,” she repeated, and her voice, which was low and measured, sank a little upon the words. “Write plainly and fully. Note what she learns, her temper, her health, her hours. I would know everything.”

She was a handsome woman, dark and still, with a complexion of that clear pallor which in some faces is a beauty and in others a portent. Her eyes were large and seldom softened; they rested on me while she spoke with an attention that was almost a scrutiny. I promised obedience, as I must, and that same evening began the book.

I wrote it, I confess, with diligence, even with pleasure; for I found in the task a vent for the fullness of my thoughts, and it is a comfort to a solitary spirit to set down its observations in order, as a traveller marks the road. I described Adela’s reading, her progress in French, her terror of the dark staircase, her singular gift for music. I wrote that she was truthful, but reserved; affectionate, but not demonstrative; and I added — for I was young and warm, and my pen ran on — that I believed the child’s heart to be a deep and hidden well, and that whoever would draw from it must go down in patience.

It was some ten days after, that, entering the parlour at an hour when Mrs. Ellerby was from home, I found the diary lying not where I had left it. I had placed it in the drawer of the little escritoire by the window; it lay now upon the table, closed, indeed, but with a leaf doubled down at the corner — a mark I had never made. My blood stirred strangely. I took it up; the leaf was that on which I had written of Adela’s silences, and of her habit of listening at the stair-head when her father’s name was spoken.

I said nothing. But that night I wrote a passage of another kind. I wrote that the house was full of rumours, and that I had heard below-stairs how Mr. Ellerby was expected daily from the Continent, though no letter announced him; that the servants whispered of debts and of a duel, and that the mistress of the house went often to the window at dusk, and stood there long after the light had failed. I wrote it with a beating heart and a steady hand, and I watched.

Within three days the leaf was turned down again, and a new circumstance appeared. Mrs. Ellerby sent for me and questioned me closely — not upon Adela, but upon the servants, and upon what I had heard in the kitchen; and when I answered with a plainness that cost me something, she looked at me long and said: “You are observant, Miss Fenwick. It is well. Continue.”

I understood then that I was not writing for a mother alone. I was writing for a woman who could not ask her own household what she burned to know, and who used the governess’s hand as a spy’s, and the child’s name as a cover for her fears. I felt the indignity of it — the shame, the anger. I was a dependent, but I was not a hireling; I had a conscience and a pride, and both revolted. That evening I sat with my pen in my hand and the book before me, and I resolved that I would write the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth, and let her read it if she dared.

And so my diary became a strange thing — a double instrument. To the mother I gave a faithful chronicle of the child. To the woman who read in secret I gave a faithful chronicle of the house. I wrote of the man who came at night to the back door and spoke long with the butler; of the letter that was burned unopened; of the portrait of Mr. Ellerby in the gallery, and how Adela had stood before it and said, “He is not so tall as that.” I wrote of my own fears too, for I was not ashamed of them; I wrote that I was watched, and that I knew it, and that I would not be bought with kindness nor silenced with frowns.

One evening, as I wrote by my single candle, the door opened without a knock, and Mrs. Ellerby stood there. She wore her dark dress, and her face was whiter than common, and she held in her hand a letter.

“You have written,” she said, “that the servants speak of a duel.”

“I have written what I heard.”

“And what you heard — was it that he had killed his man, or that he had fallen?”

“Neither, madam. I heard only that there had been a meeting, and that a surgeon had been called.”

She came nearer. Her hand trembled upon the letter. “Read this,” she said, and held it to me. It was in a foreign hand, and it said that Mr. Ellerby was living, but that he would not return until certain affairs were settled, and that his wife should not expect him before the spring.

I read it, and gave it back. She stood a moment, and then she said, in a voice that was not her own: “You think me wicked.”

“I think you unhappy, madam.”

“I have used you ill,” she said. “I have read your book — yes, I have read it. I have read it because I am alone, and because I dare not ask, and because a woman in my place must have eyes somewhere. You have hated me for it.”

“I have not hated you,” I said, “but I have been angry. I am angry still. You might have asked me to your face, and I would have told you what I knew.”

“Would you?” she said, and looked at me with a strange, sharp sadness. “Then you are braver than I. I have never in my life asked a question I feared the answer to.”

She turned to go, and at the door she paused. “Write on,” she said. “Write what you will. I shall not read it again — or if I do, I shall tell you.” And she went out, and I heard her steps go down the long corridor, and the door of her own room close softly.

I sat long by the candle. Then I took the book and wrote: “This day I have learned that the mistress of this house is a proud and a fearful woman, and that I am sorry for her. I have learned also that I am not to be made a spy against my will, and that a governess may hold her head up though she be poor and dependent. And I have written it, not for her, nor for any reader, but for myself — and for God, who sees the heart.”
