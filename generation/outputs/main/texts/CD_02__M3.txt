In a narrow, damp, and exceedingly malodorous court, lying somewhere behind the Tower of London — a court where the houses seemed to lean against one another for mutual support, as if they were all heavily in debt and very much in liquor — there lodged, in the year of our Lord one thousand eight hundred and forty-something, a young man by the name of Septimus Todge. Septimus was a clerk in the shipping office of Grigson, Mumps & Gripe, a firm whose counting-house was situated in Crooked Lane, and whose principals were, in the pleasant phrase of the neighbourhood, tight-fisted enough to skin a gnat for its tallow. For this establishment Septimus worked from eight in the morning until seven at night, copying bills of lading into immense ledgers that smelled persistently of damp paper, salt, and the unutterable sadness of commercial enterprise.

Now, Septimus Todge had a family. Let us not mince the matter, or seek to drape the naked truth in any genteel fiction: he had a great many relations, and they were, without exception, grasping, predatory, and singularly accomplished in the art of extracting coin from a relative. There was his uncle, Captain Bungay, a seafaring man who had long since retired from the sea, but who had not retired from borrowing; there was his cousin, Mrs. Gudgeon, a lady of copious tears and a perpetual infant in arms; and there was his Aunt Kettleby, who kept a marginal shop in the New Cut, and who professed an unceasing Christian concern for Septimus’s soul, combined with a very un-Christian desire to handle his purse.

It is the way of the world, and was especially the way of the London world in the eighteen-forties, that a young man might labour for years in a damp counting-house, living on bread and scrape, and never receive a visit from any member of his family. But let that same young man win a modest sum — say, a matter of forty pounds — in a public lottery, and the very bricks of the metropolis will conspire to betray him. Septimus had won forty pounds. He had purchased the ticket in a moment of wild, delirious optimism, and when the result was published, he folded the newspaper under his arm, walked home to his lodging in Whipple Court, and sat down upon his truckle-bed to marvel at his fortune. He resolved, with the fiercest determination, to keep the matter an inviolable secret. He would say nothing of it. Not a syllable! Not a whisper!

Within a week, every one of his relations had heard of it.

The first to arrive was Captain Bungay. The Captain was a man of a very red countenance, a very blue coat, and a very hollow purse. He descended upon Whipple Court on a Tuesday morning, smelling strongly of rum, and found Septimus in the act of tying his cravat before a small, cracked mirror.

“My dear boy,” said the Captain, shaking him warmly by the hand, and glancing about the room with a nautical eye, “how are you? How are you, Septimus? I heard you were — ahem — flourishing. Yes. The fact is, the old craft has met with a little leak. Nothing serious, you know. A mere trifle. But I find myself short of — well, to be plain with you, my boy, I want ten pound. Just ten pound. You’re a young man of prudence, and I thought — but there, I won’t press you. No, no. I won’t press you. Only — ten pound would save the old man’s bacon, Septimus.”

Septimus, who had not yet had time to spend a single shilling of his winnings, and who was, besides, constitutionally incapable of refusing a relative, however predatory, gave the Captain a ten-pound note. The Captain wrung his hand, bestowed upon him a paternal blessing, and departed with an alacrity that would have done credit to a much younger and more agile seaman.

On the Wednesday came Mrs. Gudgeon. She arrived carrying the perpetual infant, which she brandished before Septimus like a passport to his sympathies. The infant, who was singularly red and somewhat mottled, was, according to its mother, in the last stage of decline. It required — so Mrs. Gudgeon informed him, between copious sobs — a change of air. The sea air. The sea air was the only thing for the poor little creature. And the sea air, alas, cost money. Ten pound would take the infant and its afflicted mother to Margate. Ten pound was the precise sum required. Not nine pound nineteen and elevenpence — but ten pound.

Septimus, feeling that resistance was useless, produced a second ten-pound note. Mrs. Gudgeon wept upon his shoulder, called him a preserver and a benefactor, and withdrew, the infant still maintaining its mottled and impassive silence.

On the Thursday came Aunt Kettleby. She was a woman of an imposing aspect, a black bonnet, and a reticule of formidable dimensions. She seated herself in Septimus’s only armchair, opened the reticule, drew forth a handkerchief, and applied it to her left eye, which she was accustomed to press when she wished to produce a tear.

“Septimus,” she said, in a voice of deep solemnity, “I have come to you as a Christian woman to a Christian man. I will not deceive you. The shop — the little shop in the New Cut — is in difficulties. The bailiffs are in. I want ten pound. I do not ask it for myself. I ask it for the orphan nephew whom I have supported since infancy” — this was Septimus himself, who had certainly not been supported by Aunt Kettleby since any age whatsoever — “and for the memory of your sainted mother. Ten pound, Septimus. Exactly ten pound. It is a remarkable coincidence, is it not, that the sum should be so precise? But Providence works in mysterious ways.”

Septimus, by this time in a state of considerable bewilderment and no small dismay, handed over his third ten-pound note. He had now thirty pounds remaining — or rather, he had ten pounds remaining; for the thirty pounds had been distributed in three equal portions to three separate relations, each of whom had required exactly one-third of his winnings, and each of whom had arrived within the space of four days.

He sat down upon his truckle-bed and contemplated the remaining ten pounds. He contemplated it as a man might contemplate the last plank of a shipwreck, clinging to it in a stormy sea, and knowing full well that the sharks are circling. He had no doubt that the sharks would arrive. He had, in truth, a fourth relation: a great-uncle, one Obadiah Todge, who lived in a state of perpetual impecuniosity in a lodging-house in Drury Lane, and who was, of all the family, the most accomplished and pertinacious borrower.

On the Friday, Septimus did not go to work. He remained at home, sitting upon his bed, staring at the ten-pound note, which lay upon the table like a small, green, and fated bird. He had a presentiment — a dark, prophetic certainty — that Great-uncle Obadiah would call. And call he did. He arrived at half past two in the afternoon, in a state of considerable agitation, wearing a hat that appeared to have been sat upon by a person of substance, and carrying a umbrella which leaked from every seam.

“Septimus!” cried the old gentleman, seizing his hand. “My dear boy! My own flesh and blood! I am undone. Undone, I tell you. I have been — but I will not distress you. No. I will not. I will say nothing. Only — the rent. The landlord. The — ten pound.”

“Ten pound,” said Septimus, faintly.

“Just so,” said Great-uncle Obadiah, with an eagerness that was almost painful. “Exactly ten pound. It is a singular coincidence, is it not? I remarked upon it to myself as I walked here. Ten pound! Why, it is almost as if Providence — but there. I will not presume. Ten pound, Septimus, and old Obadiah will bless you to his dying day.”

Septimus, with the resigned air of a man who has accepted his fate, handed over the note. Great-uncle Obadiah clasped it to his bosom, bestowed upon his nephew a benediction of such length and fervour that it might have served for an entire cathedral service, and departed, leaving Septimus standing in the middle of his room, possessed of nothing but his ledgers, his truckle-bed, and the melancholy reflection that he had won forty pounds in a public lottery, and that his relations had relieved him of the entire sum, to the last penny, within the space of seven days.

He sat down once more upon his bed, and looked about him. The room was very quiet. The houses opposite leaned against one another in their customary drunken fashion. The odour of the court rose from below, and the cracked mirror reflected a young man of pale countenance and thoughtful aspect.

“Well,” said Septimus Todge, aloud, to the empty room. “Well.” And then, after a long pause: “At least — at least they won’t come again.”

But even as he spoke, he heard the sound of footsteps on the stair; and a voice — a voice that belonged, without any manner of doubt, to Captain Bungay — was heard to say, in a hoarse and confidential whisper: “The fact is, the old craft has met with a little leak. Nothing serious. A mere trifle. But I thought, perhaps — another ten pound —”
