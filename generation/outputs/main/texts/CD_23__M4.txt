Mr. Achille Pommeroy, of No. 14 Cinnamon Street, Mayfair, was a man who had built his entire fortune, his reputation, and his prodigiously curled head of hair upon the twin pillars of discretion and flattery. In the year seventeen hundred and ninety-two, when the world beyond the Channel seemed to be turning itself inside out with a most alarming and unseemly haste, Mr. Pommeroy’s establishment was a haven of civilized calm, a temple of powder and pomade where the only revolutions were those performed with a hot iron upon the noblest heads in England.

His shop was a marvel of tasteful deception. The mirrors were framed in gilt that had never known a base metal, the chairs were upholstered in a crimson velvet that seemed to sigh with comfort when a lord reclined upon it, and the air was perpetually heavy with a fragrance compounded of rose-water, beeswax, and the subtle, unctuous scent of deferred payments. Mr. Pommeroy himself, a small, round man with a face like a polished apple and hands that danced with the agility of hummingbirds, would move among his clients with an air of profound and sympathetic attention. He had a word for every earl, a compliment for every viscount, and a special, hushed reverence for the mere mention of a duke. He could listen to the most tedious political maundering with an expression of rapt fascination, and he could agree with two opposing gentlemen on the same afternoon without ever once contradicting himself. He was, in short, the perfect barber for a nervous age, a man who could smooth the troubled brow as expertly as he could frizz the reluctant curl.

But a canker, a secret and corroding canker, had begun to worm its way into the heart of this perfumed Eden. It had arrived in the form of a letter, a thin, foreign-looking missive that had come to him not through the post, but by the hand of a grubby urchin who had vanished before Mr. Pommeroy could offer him a copper. The seal was broken, the hand was cramped and hurried, and the signature was that of his brother, Henri.

Henri Pommeroy! The very name was a discordant note in the harmonious symphony of Mr. Achille’s life. While Achille had been perfecting the art of the powdered wig and the discreet murmur, Henri had remained in their native town of Saint-Flour, a brooding, dark-eyed man with a tongue like a scythe and a heart full of books that Achille considered most unsuitable. Henri was a clockmaker by trade, but a philosopher and a firebrand by inclination, and his letters, when they came at all, were filled with such inflammatory rhetoric and dangerous talk of liberty, equality, and the rights of man that Achille was wont to burn them immediately, for fear the very paper might contaminate his ledgers.

This latest letter, however, was different. It was not a philosophical treatise, but a demand, blunt and terrifying. “Brother,” it read, “the time for pretty phrases is over. The Club of the True Friends of Liberty in Saint-Flour has need of funds. You, who preen and powder the very lackeys of tyranny, will not refuse to aid the cause of the people. Send money. Send it by the hand of the courier who brings this. Do not fail, Achille, for the memory of our mother, and for the sake of your own neck, which is as French as mine.”

Achille had read it three times, his hands trembling so violently that he had nearly dropped a jar of pomade on the toe of a passing baronet. The Club of the True Friends of Liberty! He had heard whispers of such societies, of their oaths and their secret signs, of the shadowy figures who met in back rooms to plot the downfall of kings. And Henri, his own brother, was not merely a member, but a leader! The thought was a physical pain, a cold, sharp splinter lodged beneath his carefully manicured fingernails.

The consequences were not long in coming. A subtle change, a chill in the air, began to pervade the shop. Lord Featherstone, who for years had entrusted his head to Achille’s care, now sat in the chair with a strange, sidelong look in his eye. “I say, Pommeroy,” he drawled one afternoon, “read the papers lately? All that business in Paris. Dashed unpleasant. Fellow named Danton, or some such. They say he’s quite a fire-eater. You Frenchmen, eh? All a bit hot-blooded, what?” He had laughed, a dry, mirthless cackle, but his eyes had been fixed on Achille’s reflection in the mirror, and the barber had felt a cold perspiration start out upon his brow.

The Dowager Countess of Windermere, a lady of formidable proportions and even more formidable opinions, had sniffed audibly as he had assisted her from her chair. “My dear Pommeroy,” she had declared, “I trust you are a loyal subject of His Majesty? We must all be on our guard. One hears such dreadful things. A man in your… line of business… might hear a great deal, you know. Might even be a party to… secrets.” The word had hung in the scented air like a guillotine blade.

He had protested, of course. He had bowed, and smiled, and assured them all of his undying devotion to the English constitution, the Church of England, and the inestimable virtues of a well-powdered wig. But the seed of suspicion had been sown, and it grew with a horrible, silent fecundity. His custom began, imperceptibly at first, to dwindle. A morning appointment would be cancelled with a mumbled excuse; a regular patron would be “unavoidably detained in the country.” The great houses of Mayfair, which had once opened their doors to him with such cordiality, now seemed to close them with a quiet, deliberate finality. He was no longer Achille Pommeroy, the artist of the coiffure; he was Achille Pommeroy, the Frenchman, the potential Jacobin, the brother of a revolutionary.

He was sitting alone in his shop one evening, the candles burning low and casting long, accusing shadows from the wig-blocks, when the bell above the door gave a mournful tinkle. It was not a customer, but a messenger, a sallow-faced man in a shabby brown coat, who handed him a note and stood waiting with the imperturbable stillness of a gargoyle.

The note was from Henri. It was brief, and it was brutal. “The money, Achille. The Club grows impatient. If I do not receive it by the end of the week, I shall be forced to inform our friends in London of your… connections. I am sure they would be most interested to learn that the fashionable Mr. Pommeroy is the brother of Citoyen Pommeroy, and a contributor to the cause. The sum is one hundred pounds. Do not disappoint me.”

Achille stared at the paper, his mind a whirlwind of terror and rage. One hundred pounds! It was a fortune, a substantial portion of his carefully hoarded savings. And the threat! To be denounced as a traitor, to be dragged from his own shop, his powdered wig askew, and thrown into some noisome English prison! The vision was so vivid, so grotesquely complete, that he nearly swooned. He looked at the messenger, who returned his gaze with the flat, dead eyes of a fish. He thought of his brother, of the dark, fanatical face he remembered from their boyhood, a face that had always seemed to be looking beyond the present world to some violent and glorious future.

With a trembling hand, he went to his strongbox, a hidden compartment beneath the floorboards in his back room. He counted out the notes, each one a little pang of loss. He wrapped them in a piece of brown paper, sealed it with a plain wafer, and handed it to the messenger.

“Tell my brother,” he said, his voice a dry croak, “that this is the end. Tell him I am no longer his brother. Tell him I am an Englishman.”

The man took the packet, nodded once, and was gone. Achille sank into a chair, his heart hammering against his ribs like a trapped bird. He was safe, for the moment. But the canker remained. The flattery upon his lips had turned to ashes, and the discretion in his heart had curdled into a cold, hard fear. The mirrors of his shop no longer reflected a prosperous and contented artist, but a small, frightened man, surrounded by the ghosts of his own lies and the distant, menacing thunder of a world he could not control. And somewhere across the water, in a dingy room in Saint-Flour, a clockmaker with a scythe for a tongue was counting the money of his brother’s shame, and smiling.
