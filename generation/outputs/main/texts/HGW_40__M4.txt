I had taken the first floor back in a house in Camden Town, a district of London that is in a perpetual state of transition, neither respectable nor frankly disreputable, but always smelling faintly of coal-smoke and damp plaster. My landlady was a Mrs. Prendergast, a widow of considerable energy and a strictly practical turn of mind, who viewed my scientific apparatus with a suspicion she was too well-bred to voice directly, but which she conveyed through a series of tight-lipped sighs and a tendency to dust the workbench with an unnecessarily vigorous hand. My rooms were cluttered with the paraphernalia of my researches—coils of wire, galvanic batteries, and the great, ungainly bulk of my chronal field generator, which squatted in the corner like a brooding iron toad.

The discovery, when it came, was not the flash of inspiration the novelists describe, but the slow, grinding result of two years of tedious calculation and frustrating experiment. I had been working on the problem of temporal variance, the notion that the rate at which time’s arrow flies is not a universal constant but a local condition, a property of the space one occupies. My machine, when properly tuned, created a field in which the chronal flux was diminished. To put it plainly, within the sphere of its influence, time ran slow. A minute inside the field was, to an observer without, a period of some ten minutes. The effect was confined to a small area, roughly the size of a modest drawing-room, and the boundary was as sharp as a soap-bubble’s edge.

My first successful trial was with a housefly. I placed the creature under a glass tumbler within the field, and observed it from without. For the space of a minute by my pocket-watch, the fly was frozen in the act of preening its legs. It was a curious and rather unsettling sight. When I shut the machine down, the fly resumed its business with an aggrieved buzz, none the worse for its temporal sojourn.

It was Mrs. Prendergast who gave me the notion of its practical application. I had encountered her on the stairs one Tuesday morning, her face flushed and her arms full of damp linen. “It’s this washing, Mr. Anstruther,” she said, her voice tight with exasperation. “The mangle’s broken again, and the girl’s gone and scalded her hand, and if I don’t get these sheets dry and ironed by noon, the whole week’s rent is spent on the laundry. A body needs a day of forty hours to get it all done.”

A day of forty hours. The phrase stuck in my mind. Here was a woman whose chief complaint was a dearth of time, and I, by a fortunate coincidence of science and circumstance, was in a position to sell it to her.

I broached the subject that evening. She was in the kitchen, a steamy, low-ceiled room that smelled of soap and boiled mutton, engaged in a losing battle with a collar. “Mrs. Prendergast,” I began, with as much casualness as I could muster, “you remarked this morning on the insufficiency of the day. I believe I may be able to assist you.”

She looked up, her iron poised. “I’ve no money for new-fangled labour-saving devices, Mr. Anstruther, if that’s your meaning.”

“No, no, nothing of the sort. It is a matter of… perspective. Of the rate at which things occur. My machine in the sitting-room, the one you so kindly dust for me, has the property of slowing the passage of time within its field. A quarter of an hour in that room, from your point of view, would be nearly two and a half hours for the rest of the world. You could finish your ironing in what would feel like a brief respite, while the morning outside would have barely advanced.”

She put down the iron and stared at me, her broad, sensible face a study in conflicting emotions: disbelief, curiosity, and a profound, practical skepticism. “You’re having me on, sir.”

“I am perfectly serious. I can demonstrate it now, if you wish.”

And so, with a mixture of reluctance and a housewife’s desperate hope for a shortcut, she followed me into my sitting-room. I explained the operation of the machine in the simplest terms, that she was to stand within the marked circle on the floor, that she would feel nothing, and that I would operate the controls from without. She stood there, a stout, aproned figure, clutching a basket of unironed linen to her bosom as though it were a shield against the unnatural. I engaged the generator. The great iron toad began to hum, a low, insistent note that seemed to vibrate in one’s very teeth. A faint, shimmering distortion, like the air above a hot stove, filled the space within the circle. Mrs. Prendergast stood motionless, her eyes wide. To my senses, she was a statue, a waxwork in a diorama.

I waited. I read a chapter of a novel. I smoked a pipe. After a quarter of an hour by my watch, I shut the machine down. The shimmering ceased, the hum died away, and Mrs. Prendergast staggered slightly, as if waking from a doze. She looked at the clock on the mantelpiece, then at me, then at the clock again. Her basket was empty. The pile of perfectly folded, immaculately ironed linen lay on the table beside her.

“Well, I never,” she breathed. “It’s… it’s like a dream. I did it all, every last piece, and it took no time at all.” A slow, triumphant smile spread across her face. “Mr. Anstruther, you’re a marvel. A positive marvel.”

For a few weeks, all was harmony. Mrs. Prendergast became my most fervent disciple. She finished her washing, her ironing, her mending, and even found time to write long letters to her sister in Aberdeen, all within the space of what she called “a comfortable sit-down.” She spoke of my machine in hushed, reverent tones to the grocer and the baker, and I was elevated in her estimation from a shabby, eccentric lodger to a species of domestic magician.

But the suspicions began, as they will with practical people, when they perceive a power they cannot comprehend being used for purposes they do not understand. I had begun to spend longer and longer hours in the field myself. The world outside was a distraction, a noisy, hurried chaos. Inside, I could work with a serene and god-like deliberation. A single afternoon in the field gave me the better part of a week for my calculations. I was on the verge of a great synthesis, a theory that would unite the chronal flux with the luminiferous ether, and I needed the time.

I noticed that Mrs. Prendergast had taken to watching me. Her gaze would follow me as I adjusted the dials, a new and disquieting speculation in her eye. One afternoon, she cornered me in the hall. “Mr. Anstruther,” she said, her voice low, “I’ve been thinking. About your machine. You say it slows time down. But what else might it do? What goes on in there when you’re by yourself for all those long hours that are only minutes to me?”

“My work, Mrs. Prendergast. Only my work.”

“That’s as may be,” she said, her arms folded across her chest. “But a body has to wonder. You could be doing anything in there. Anything at all. And who’s to know? You might be… you might be ageing, or… or meddling with things that don’t bear meddling with. It’s not natural, that’s what it is. Not natural for a man to have so much time and no one to see what he’s about.”

Her words, with their hint of a dark and secret vice, chilled me. The very isolation that was the condition of my work was now the source of her suspicion. I saw that I had given her a gift she could not understand, and her mind, unable to grasp its nature, had supplied a sinister explanation. The machine hummed in the next room, a patient, potent presence, and I knew with a sudden, cold certainty that my season of tranquil research in Camden Town was drawing to a close.
