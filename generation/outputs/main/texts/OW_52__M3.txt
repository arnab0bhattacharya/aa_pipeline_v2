Miss Arabella Forsythe had been raised, by a most singular paradox, to believe that wealth was the one unforgivable vulgarity. Her guardian, the Dowager Lady Cumnor, who had been born in a draughty castle and married into a damp fortune, held that the only true aristocracy was the aristocracy of the empty pocket. “Money,” the Dowager was fond of remarking over the rim of her unpowdered teacup, “is merely the grime left behind by those who have handled life too eagerly.” Arabella, therefore, possessing an income of six thousand a year, which she spent entirely on old tapestries and the relief of distressed poets, was considered by her set to be entirely unspotted by the world.

It was at a gathering in Chelsea that she first saw Mr. Julian Vance. He lived in a studio that admitted the rain through a skylight of cracked glass and the wind through a door that had forgotten its hinges. He was very beautiful, in the fragile, pallid manner of a winter moon, and he painted pictures of decaying cathedrals and faded poppies, using colours that seemed to have been drained of their coarser elements. Arabella, who wore a gown of grey velvet that clung to her like a mist, was profoundly moved by his art. She felt that here, at last, was a soul entirely uncorrupted by the grossness of silver.

“You paint with the very essence of melancholy,” she said to him, as he stood before a canvas representing a ruined abbey in a fog. “It must be a great consolation to you to have nothing.”

Julian turned his large, dark eyes upon her. “It is a great irritation to me to have nothing,” he answered. “I desire to be rich. Only the rich can afford to be entirely tragic. Poverty is merely a bourgeois inconvenience; it forces one to think about butcher’s bills when one should be thinking about the fall of the Roman Empire.”

Arabella shuddered. It was as though a lily had suddenly begun to argue the merits of the stock exchange. “You are surely jesting,” she murmured. “Beauty and wealth are eternal enemies.”

“On the contrary,” replied Julian, dipping a brush into a pot of thin, grey wash, “they are the closest of allies. Wealth buys the leisure to be beautiful, and beauty is the only currency that wealth cannot debase. I wish to cover the world with yellow gold, merely so that I may have a suitable background against which to place a blue vase.”

Arabella left the studio in a state of high moral distress. That a creature so exquisitely formed should harbour a desire for carriage-loads of sovereigns seemed to her a flaw in the divine order of the universe. She determined that she would teach him the beauty of poverty. He, in his turn, resolved that he would teach her the poverty of beauty.

A fortnight later they met again, by accident, in a narrow lane behind a second-hand bookshop. Arabella was dressed in a gown of coarse linen, unadorned by any jewel. She looked like a saint who had renounced the world, and was rather pleased about it.

“I have come to walk among the poor,” she said, “to remind myself that the absence of possessions is the presence of perfection.”

Julian, who was carrying a parcel wrapped in brown paper, smiled. “I have just purchased a length of crimson silk,” he said. “It cost me the last of my quarterly allowance. I intend to drape it over my starving sofa. One cannot starve aesthetically on a bare linen cover. One requires crimson.”

“Crimson is the colour of sin,” said Arabella, severely.

“It is the colour of life,” he retorted. “Grey is the colour of sin. Grey is the colour of compromise, of fog, of people who are neither hot nor cold. I intend to be very hot. I intend to be rich.”

“Then I must show you my world,” cried Arabella, “for it is evident you do not know it.”

She took him to a tumbledown cottage on the outskirts of the city, where an old woman sat by a fire of dried sticks. The walls were bare, the floor was of earth, and the only ornament was a broken pewter cup. Arabella gazed upon the scene with the rapt expression of one looking at a masterpiece.

“See,” she whispered, “the grandeur of nothing. The soul expands when the pockets are empty. Here is true poetry.”

Julian looked at the old woman, who was coughing, and then at the damp wall. “It is very affecting,” he said, “but the poetry would be improved by a carpet. And the cough would be improved by a physician. Poverty, my dear Arabella, is only beautiful when it is a pose. When it is real, it is merely a disease.”

“You are hopelessly material,” she sighed.

“I am hopefully material,” he corrected. “Come with me tomorrow, and I will show you the other side of the shield.”

The next day he took her to a house in Mayfair, belonging to a friend of his, a certain Lord Molyneux, who had recently inherited a vast estate and had spent the morning rearranging his emeralds. The rooms were filled with objects of marvellous rarity: tapestries of silver and blue, cabinets of lacquer, and great bowls of porcelain filled with late roses that seemed to have been steeped in sunset.

“Here,” said Julian, “is the true poetry. Money has bought leisure, and leisure has bought taste. See how the light falls on that velvet. It is a light that never shines on earthen floors. Beauty demands a frame, and the only frame for beauty is gold.”

Arabella, looking at the roses, felt a strange stirring in her heart. The coarse linen of her gown seemed suddenly harsh and unlovely. She thought of the old woman’s cough, and the bare walls, and she felt that, after all, a carpet might be a very moral thing.

“It is very splendid,” she admitted, “but it is all bought. It has no soul.”

“It has a soul,” said Julian. “The soul of the man who chose it. To choose beautifully is the highest art. To be poor and choose nothing is merely an abdication.”

They stood for some time in silence, looking at the roses. Arabella plucked one, and its petals were like silk against her fingers. She felt the thrill of colour, the intoxication of perfect form. The grey world of renunciation seemed very far away, and very cold.

“You are trying to corrupt me,” she said, at last, with a faint smile.

“I am trying to complete you,” he answered. “You have worshipped at the altar of poverty, and you have found it empty. I am asking you to worship at the altar of beauty, and find it full.”

“And what of the soul?” she asked.

“The soul,” said Julian, “is merely a name we give to our desires. I desire emeralds, and therefore my soul is green. You desire sainthood, and therefore your soul is grey. But green is the colour of spring, and grey is the colour of winter. I prefer spring.”

Arabella looked at him, and saw that he was very beautiful, and very sure of himself. She realized, with a shock that was almost physical, that poverty was, after all, only a theory, and that theories, like umbrellas, are useful only in bad weather. The sun was shining through the window, and the roses were glowing like jewels, and she felt that she was very tired of the rain.

“I think,” she said, slowly, “that I have been very foolish.”

“We are all foolish,” he replied. “The only difference is that some of us are foolish in velvet, and some of us are foolish in rags. I greatly prefer velvet.”

“And I,” said Arabella, “think I prefer emeralds.”

So they were married, and lived in a house of perfect taste, filled with beautiful things, and Arabella never mentioned the beauty of poverty again. She had discovered that the only thing more tedious than having money is pretending not to have it, and that the highest form of art is the art of living well.
