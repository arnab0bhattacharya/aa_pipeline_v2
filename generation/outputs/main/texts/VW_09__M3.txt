The gravel crunched—crunched, and the sound went up into the elms and was lost, as if the very weight of his boots upon the white stones was an impertinence; and he stood for a moment, his hand on the iron latch, thinking how odd it was that the air here should smell of damp earth and woodsmoke, and not at all of the slag heaps and the bitter smoke of mills, but only of damp earth, and of violets, perhaps, crushed somewhere under the yew trees. The house, grey, immensely old, with its wings stretching out as if to gather in the whole of the soft Wiltshire afternoon, seemed to him less a building than a living thing, breathing, quietly cognisant of his presence. He had been here since Friday. It was Sunday now.

In the hall the light fell in long shafts through the diamond panes and made pools upon the flagstones, and in those pools of light motes of dust danced, endlessly, perpetually, as if they were the very atoms of the house's ancientry made visible. A grandfather clock ticked with a slow, fat, unctuous regularity that was quite different from the sharp, deferential tick of the school clock in the boardroom at home, which always sounded as if it were hurrying the boys along to their Scripture lesson. This clock had no interest in hurrying anyone. It had stood there since the Georges, perhaps, and would stand there when all the Eltons and all the schoolmasters from the north were dust, and it ticked with a sublime indifference, measuring out time not in lessons and factory shifts but in some larger, more leisurely unit, some vast astronomical unit that belonged to the seasons and the slow growth of the ivy.

Mrs. Elton had said, when he arrived, holding out her hand—a hand so white, so fine, so entirely unacquainted with the grime of the schoolroom—“How good of you to come, Mr. Marsden. Guy has talked of nothing else.” And she had smiled, and the smile had seemed to him to contain the whole of the weekend, to gather it up into one bright, effortless gesture, so that he had felt himself instantly at home, instantly at ease, as if he had stepped out of a rough coat into a silken one. Guy, his pupil, the boy with the dark eyes and the slow, sullen intelligence that was like a river moving through clay, had said nothing, but had stood at his mother’s elbow, twisting a button, and Marsden had thought: he is ashamed of me; or no, not ashamed, but shy, as if bringing one world into another were a delicate operation that might easily go wrong.

But it had not gone wrong. On Saturday they had walked across the downs, and the skylarks had risen, and Guy had pointed out the barrows, the ancient graves, and Marsden had stood on the chalk and looked out over the valley and felt something within him loosen, some knot of northern austerity untie itself, and he had thought: this is how life should be lived; not in rows, under gaslight, with the smell of coal dust in one’s nostrils, but here, under this immense sky, with the grass underfoot and the clouds sailing over, and one’s pupils not pupils but companions, fellow travellers, walking beside one in the wind. And in the evening they had sat in the drawing-room, and the candles had been lit, and the silver had glowed on the table, and Miss Elton—Isobel, Guy’s sister—had played the piano, and the music had risen and fallen like a tide, and he had sat in his corner and thought: I am happy; I am, for the first time in my life, happy; and the thought had seemed to him a revelation, a breaking open of a shell he had not known was there.

Now it was Sunday evening, and the house was quiet. They had been to church, and the bells were still ringing in his ears, not the bells of the church but the bells of the psalms, the words that had rolled out over the congregation like a sea: “The earth is the Lord’s, and all that therein is.” And Mrs. Elton had sung in a clear, thin voice that had seemed to him the very voice of the house itself, the voice of its grey stones and its old wood and its silver and its long, unbroken, unreflecting ease. He had looked at her in her pew, and at Guy beside her, and at Isobel, and at the old father, asleep, his mouth slightly open, and he had thought: they belong here; they are part of it; and I am a visitor, a guest, a man who has been invited, and the invitation is a kindness, and the kindness is real, but it is not the same as belonging.

He found Guy in the library, standing by the window, looking out at the lawn. The lawn was wet with dew, and the shadows of the trees lay across it in long, blue bars. A thrush was singing, and the sound came in through the open window, and filled the room, and mixed with the smell of the old leather bindings and the polish on the shelves.

“I say, Marsden,” said Guy, without turning round, “you don’t mind being here, do you? I mean—it was awfully decent of you to come. Mother was in a frightful taking. The Folliots cried off at the last minute—some illness or other—and she was left with thirteen for dinner. Thirteen! You know what she’s like about that. So she said, ‘We must have Guy’s master,’ and I wrote, and you came, and it was awfully decent.”

He spoke in the tone of one who is explaining a small social inconvenience, a minor mechanical failure in the smooth running of the household, a broken cog that has been temporarily replaced. And Marsden, standing in the library, with the smell of leather and the sound of the thrush and the blue shadows on the lawn, felt the word “thirteen” fall into his mind like a stone into a pool, and the ripples spread out and out and out, touching everything, changing everything, so that the grey stones of the house were no longer friendly but indifferent, and the silver on the table was no longer beautiful but merely expensive, and Mrs. Elton’s smile was no longer a welcome but a device, a contrivance, a means of filling a gap, of making up a number.

Thirteen. He was the fourteenth. He was the man who had been invited to make up numbers.

“Oh, not at all,” he said. “Not at all. Delighted.” And his voice sounded, in his own ears, exactly as it always sounded: thin, correct, the voice of a schoolmaster, a man who has learned to say the right thing because to say the wrong thing would be to lose his place. And Guy turned and looked at him, and for a moment the boy’s dark eyes seemed to hold something—a flicker of understanding, of sympathy, of shame even—but it passed, and he said, “Jolly good. Coming in to tea? Mother’s doing the honours,” and he went out, and Marsden was alone in the library.

The thrush was still singing. The dew was still on the lawn. The shadows of the trees lay across the grass in long, blue bars, and the light in the room was the light of a Sunday evening, soft, golden, declining, the light that makes everything beautiful and nothing true. And Marsden stood at the window and looked out at it all, at the garden, at the downs beyond, at the immense, indifferent sky, and he thought: it is all real; it is all beautiful; and I am not part of it; I am the fourteenth; I am the man who makes up the number; and the thought was not bitter, because the beauty of the evening was too great for bitterness, but it was cold, and it settled upon him like a frost, and he knew that he would go back to the north, to the slag heaps and the smoke and the schoolroom, and that he would remember this weekend not as a happiness but as a vision, a glimpse of something that was not, and could never be, his.

The gong sounded. It was a deep, rich note, and it rolled through the house like a wave, and he heard Mrs. Elton’s voice, bright, clear, effortless, calling, “Tea! Tea, everybody!” and he straightened his tie, and turned from the window, and went in.
