It was upon the third morning after my return to London that the conviction, which had until then been no more than a shadow upon my mind, hardened into a certainty. The man who called himself Mr. Silas Crowe was not what he appeared to be, and I was in a position of some peril. I took up my pen, therefore, and wrote to the only man in England who I believed might help me.

My name is Miss Eleanor Vance, and I had come to London from my father’s sheep station in the Canterbury Plains of New Zealand. My father, a man of quiet habits and immense perseverance, had died in the previous autumn. His solicitors, Messrs. Grigg & Sons, had written to me of a legacy left by my late mother’s brother, a Mr. Alistair Finch, who had prospered in the City and died a bachelor. The sum was considerable, and the property—a house in Kensington and certain funds—was to be mine upon my reaching the age of five-and-twenty, an event which had occurred just before my embarkation.

The voyage was a long one, and by the time the good ship *Rotorua* docked at Tilbury, I felt myself a stranger in the land of my birth. The grey sky, the press of people, the very smell of the damp air were all alien to me. I had expected a clerk from Grigg & Sons to meet me, but instead, a tall, thin gentleman with a clean-shaven face and an air of severe professionalism detached himself from the crowd. His suit was of a fine black broadcloth, his gloves were of grey kid, and he carried a silver-headed cane. He raised his hat.

“Miss Vance, I presume?” he said, his voice flat and without inflection. “My name is Crowe. I am come from your uncle’s estate. Messrs. Grigg have been obliged to travel to Scotland on a matter of urgency, and have deputed me to act on their behalf.”

I was tired and bewildered, and his plausible explanation, coupled with his precise manner, satisfied my immediate questions. He saw to my luggage, hailed a four-wheeler, and conveyed me to a respectable boarding-house in Bloomsbury which he had, he said, taken the liberty of securing for me. He spoke of my uncle’s affairs with a fluency that impressed me at the time: the state of the investments, the tenants of the Kensington house, the small bequests to servants. He produced papers for me to sign, and a bank draft for my immediate expenses. His hands, I noted, were long and white, but the nails were cut a little too short, and there was a certain restlessness in the way his fingers tapped upon his cane.

For the first week, all seemed in order. He called upon me daily, always at four o’clock, his arrival heralded by the sharp rap of his cane upon the floorboards. We discussed the necessary legalities. He was a man of punctilious habit, yet his knowledge, I began to notice, had curious gaps. He could speak for an hour on the precise wording of a trust deed, but when I asked him of the character of my uncle Alistair—a man I had never met—he could offer only the vaguest of generalities. He spoke of him as a man of business, a man who kept himself to himself. It was a portrait without a face.

The first true doubt was planted by a trifle. I had asked him of the firm of Grigg & Sons, in whose office my father had placed such trust for thirty years. “A most reputable firm,” Mr. Crowe had said smoothly. “Established in Lincoln’s Inn Fields.” But I remembered my father speaking of their offices in Chancery Lane, a detail of no importance to a stranger, but one which struck me with the force of a physical blow. I made a show of agreement, but my mind was now awake and watchful.

From that moment, I observed him with a new eye. The restlessness of his hands was not that of a professional man, but of a gambler. There was a faint, persistent odour of cheap tobacco about his clothes, quite at odds with his fine suit. And his eyes, which I had at first thought merely cold, held a furtive, calculating light when he believed my attention to be elsewhere. I began to fear that the papers I had signed were not what he had represented them to be. The house in Kensington, the funds at the bank—all might be gone. I was a young woman, alone and ignorant of the ways of the city, and I was in the hands of a man I was now certain was a scoundrel.

To confront him would be useless; he would only confuse me with more lies. To go to the police would be to present a story without proof, the mere vapourings of a hysterical girl from the colonies. It was then that I thought of the name I had seen in the newspapers, the name of a man whose exploits were a byword for deduction and courage. I took a sheet of the boarding-house notepaper and, with a hand I strove to make steady, I wrote my appeal. I addressed it to Mr. Sherlock Holmes, 221B Baker Street, and sent the maid out to post it at the nearest pillar-box. I felt a small measure of hope return to me.

My hopes were answered with a speed that astonished me. The very next evening, as Mr. Crowe was taking his leave, the landlady announced two gentlemen. The first was a man of middle height and sturdy build, with a kind, open face and a gentlemanly air. The second was tall and gaunt, his face pale and ascetic, his eyes of a piercing, steely brightness that seemed to take in every detail of the room in a single glance. He wore a long grey travelling-cloak and held a soft felt hat in his long, thin fingers.

“Miss Vance,” said the stouter of the two, raising his hat. “I am Dr. Watson. My friend, Mr. Sherlock Holmes.”

Holmes said nothing, but his gaze swept past me to the door through which Mr. Crowe had just departed. “A tall man,” he said, his voice quiet but distinct. “He favours his right leg slightly, but it is a pretence; the wear on his left boot heel is the heavier. He smokes shag tobacco, and he has been handling coins—copper, by the smell on his gloves. You are in some danger, Miss Vance, but perhaps not as much as you fear. Now, I beg you, sit down and tell me everything, from the moment you first saw this gentleman at the docks.”

His manner was so assured, his observations so sharp and true, that I felt a great weight lift from my shoulders. I told him my story, from the grey morning at Tilbury to my growing suspicions of the day before. He listened without interruption, his long fingers steepled before him, while Dr. Watson jotted occasional notes in a small pocket-book.

When I had finished, Holmes rose and began to pace the room. “It is a bold and clever scheme,” he mused. “They knew of your uncle’s death and of the existence of an heir. They knew she would be a young woman, alone and unversed in London’s ways. The gamble was that she would accept a plausible official at face value. Crowe is a confidence trickster, of course, but he is merely the instrument. The brain of the matter lies elsewhere.” He stopped and fixed me with his bright gaze. “You said he spoke of your uncle’s investments. Did he mention any particular name? A broker, a company, a partner?”

I thought for a moment. “He mentioned a name once, in passing, when speaking of some shares that had been sold. He called him Mr. Cornelius.”

A slow smile spread across Holmes’s thin lips. “Cornelius,” he repeated softly. “The same Mr. Cornelius who figures in the Norwood affair. Watson, the circle is complete. This is no simple fraud, Miss Vance. It is part of a much larger and more sinister web. But we have our starting-point. You will remain here and admit no one. Come, Watson, we have an appointment with a gentleman in Cheapside who has a great deal to tell us about the late Mr. Alistair Finch’s business affairs. The game is afoot, and I fancy it will not be long before we bring Mr. Silas Crowe to a very abrupt and unwelcome reckoning.”
