The light in Mr. Arthur Pym’s studio on the sea-front was of a peculiar, clinical quality. It was not the warm, amber glow of the gas lamps that flickered in the drawing-rooms of the old town, but a hard, white brilliance generated by a new-fangled arc lamp. It cut through the dust motes with the precision of a surgeon’s knife. Pym, a man of moderate stature and unassuming intellect, had always considered photography a matter of mechanics and chemistry rather than art. He did not pose his subjects; he arranged them. He did not capture souls; he recorded surfaces. It was a safe, respectable existence in Brighton, catering to the visiting gentry and the local tradesmen who wished to leave a likeness for their grandchildren.

It was the lens that changed everything.

It had arrived three days prior, a strange, heavy glass instrument imported from a German optician whose name Pym could not quite recall. The salesman had been nervous, his eyes darting around the shop as if expecting a police raid. "It is a matter of refraction," the salesman had muttered, wiping sweat from his brow. "Mostly. The glass is ground differently. It catches things the eye misses." Pym had paid the exorbitant sum without a moment’s hesitation, driven by a professional curiosity that had long since atrophied into routine boredom.

The first subject was Mrs. Higgins, a stout woman of fifty who came in for a formal portrait to mark her late husband’s anniversary. She sat on the velvet chair, her fan held rigidly, her expression one of practiced, empty dignity. Pym adjusted the bellows, the heavy brass knobs, and looked through the viewfinder.

The image was sharp, yes, sharper than any he had ever seen. But behind Mrs. Higgins, in the shadowed corner of the studio where the black backdrop hung, there was a shape. It was faint, like a double exposure, a ghostly smudge of grey against the dark cloth. It had the suggestion of a head, a pair of shoulders, and long, thin limbs that seemed to dissolve into the air. Pym blinked, rubbed his eyes with a handkerchief, and looked again. The shape was still there. It stood slightly to the right of Mrs. Higgins, its head tilted at an angle that suggested either curiosity or malice.

"Is there something wrong with the light, Mr. Pym?" Mrs. Higgins asked, her voice tight.

"No, no," Pym said, his voice steady despite the cold prickle that had started at the base of his neck. "Just... focusing. The new lens has a very wide aperture. It picks up a great deal of detail."

He took the photograph. He developed it in the back room, surrounded by the smell of acid and silver nitrate. As the image swam into existence on the paper, Pym felt a lurch in his stomach. There, in high definition, was Mrs. Higgins. And behind her, unmistakably, was the figure. It was not a smudge. It was a man, or something resembling a man, rendered in a translucent, watery grey. It had no face, only a smooth, featureless oval where eyes and mouth should have been. It stood with its hands resting on Mrs. Higgins’s shoulders, fingers splayed wide.

Pym stared at the print for a long time. He thought of the German salesman, of the words *catches things the eye misses*. He was a practical man. He believed in lenses, in chemicals, in the mechanical laws of light. He did not believe in ghosts. And yet, the image was there, indelible, a fact of the physical world.

He told himself it was a flaw in the glass. A scratch. A bubble in the coating. He tested the lens on the studio’s cat, a tabby named Barnaby, who slept in a basket by the stove. The cat was sharp, clear, and alone. There was no figure behind the cat.

The next day, a young man came in. He was a student, perhaps, with a nervous energy and a smile that did not reach his eyes. He wanted a portrait for a university application. Pym arranged him, checked the focus, and looked through the lens.

The figure was there again.

This time, it was not in the background. It was standing directly behind the student, its head nearly touching the boy’s. The grey, formless thing seemed to lean forward, its head dipping as if to whisper. The student shivered, rubbing his arms.

"Cold in here, sir?" Pym asked.

"A bit, yes. The sea breeze gets in, I suppose."

Pym took the picture. He did not show it to the customer. He kept it in a locked drawer. That night, he sat in his study, the lamp burning low, and examined the print under a magnifying glass. The detail was horrifying. The "face" of the figure was not merely blank; it was a void. A hole in the image. And the hands... the hands were long, the fingers jointed too many times, curling around the student’s neck.

A week passed. Pym’s business boomed. Word spread that his new lens produced portraits of startling clarity. People flocked to his studio. He took dozens of sitters. And in every single one, the figure was there.

Sometimes it was close, hovering inches from the subject’s ear. Sometimes it was further back, standing in the shadows, watching. But it was always there. And as the days went by, Pym began to notice a change. The figure was getting closer.

In the first photos, it was a distinct separation, a ghost in the background. In the later ones, it was overlapping the subjects. In the most recent portrait, taken of a elderly lady with a diamond necklace, the figure’s hand was clearly visible, resting on the back of her head, its fingers sinking into her hair.

Pym’s hands began to shake. He stopped eating. He slept in short, fitful bursts, waking up with the feeling that he was being watched. He looked out of his window at the street below. The gas lamps flickered. The shadows stretched long and thin. He tried to convince himself that the lens was defective, that it was picking up some kind of atmospheric distortion, a residual image from the emulsion. But the logic of it was flawed. The lens was new. The plates were fresh. The light was controlled.

One evening, the studio was empty. The arc lamp was off. The room was dim, lit only by the dying daylight from the window. Pym sat at his desk, the latest prints spread out before him. He was looking at a portrait of a child, a little girl with red ribbons in her hair. In the photo, the figure was not behind her. It was in front of her. It had its arms wrapped around her small frame, its head bowed, its featureless face pressed against her cheek.

Pym stood up. His legs felt weak. He walked to the window and looked out. The street was empty. The sea was a dark, churning mass in the distance.

He turned back to the room. The air was cold. He could hear the tick of the clock on the wall. And then, he heard a sound. A soft, wet sound, like breathing. It was coming from the corner of the room, behind the black backdrop.

Pym froze. He did not turn around. He knew, with a certainty that bypassed his rational mind, that if he turned, he would see it. He could feel the presence of it, a heavy, cold weight in the air, a pressure against his skin. He could smell it, a faint, metallic scent, like old blood and ozone.

He looked at the lens on the tripod. It was dark, a black eye staring back at him. He remembered the German salesman’s words. *It catches things the eye misses.*

Pym realized then that the lens had not revealed the figures. It had been a conduit. A bridge. The light had been drawing them in, pulling them from some other plane into his, just as the silver nitrate pulled the image from the darkness. He had not been photographing ghosts. He had been inviting them in.

The breathing grew louder. It was right behind him. He could feel a cold breath on the back of his neck.

Pym reached for the switch to the arc lamp. His hand trembled. He flicked it.

The room burst into blinding white light. The hum of the current filled the air. Pym spun around, expecting to see the figure, to see the grey, formless thing that had haunted his work.

The room was empty.

The black backdrop hung still. The chair was empty. The cat was asleep in its basket. There was no one there.

Pym stood in the center of the studio, gasping for air. His heart hammered against his ribs. He looked at the lens. It was just glass. Just a piece of ground, polished glass.

He laughed. A dry, hysterical sound. It was his imagination. The stress. The lack of sleep. He was a man of science, of logic. He had been fooled by his own fears.

He walked to the desk and picked up the print of the little girl. He looked at it. The figure was there, clearly, undeniably. Its arms were around the child. Its head was bowed.

Pym’s hand began to shake again. He looked at the lens. In the reflection of the dark glass, he saw his own face. Pale, drawn, terrified.

And behind his reflection, just for a second, he saw a shape. A grey, formless shape, with no face, reaching out.

Pym dropped the print. He ran. He ran out of the studio, out into the street, into the cold night air. He did not stop running until he reached the sea-front, until he was far from the shop, far from the light, far from the lens.

He looked back at the building. The light in the studio was still on. A bright, white square in the darkness.

He knew he would never go back. He knew he would never take another picture. But as he stood there, shivering in the wind, he knew with a chilling certainty that it was too late. The light had been on. The door had been open. And the things that the eye misses, the things that the lens catches, they do not leave. They wait. They watch. And they are always, always, getting closer.
