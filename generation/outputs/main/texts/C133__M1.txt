The air in the Old Bailey’s Number One Court was a thick, stale broth of humanity, damp wool, and the ghosts of a thousand previous verdicts. Mr. Arthur Penhaligon, his barrister’s wig feeling a size too large and his black gown still smelling of the moth-balls from which it had been rescued, felt his own fresh-faced youth as a kind of accusation. Before him, in the dock, stood Silas Croft, a man built like a ship’s anchor, with hands the size of dinner plates and a face carved from worry. He was a coal-heaver, accused of stealing a sovereign and two shillings from the strongbox of his employer, Mr. Josiah Croft, a name that was no relation but a cruel irony nonetheless. The evidence was a brick wall. Mr. Croft had closed up his wharf office for the night, only to find the lock on his desk forced and the money gone. Silas, who had been in the last barge of the day, was the only one seen lingering near the office. In his pocket, a constable had found the exact sum, wrapped in a grimy handkerchief.

The prosecutor, a bulldog of a man named Davies, presented the case with a weary, almost bored, finality. It was, to him, a simple transaction: a crime, a culprit, and a punishment. Penhaligon’s cross-examination of the constable was a fumbling affair, met with a series of terse, unshakeable denials. The constable had found the money. Silas had been there. Case closed. The judge, a man whose face seemed to have been eroded by a lifetime of such tedious sorrows, was already making notes for the sentencing. Penhaligon felt the cold dread of failure settle in his stomach. He had taken the case *pro bono*, a gesture of idealism he was now beginning to see as mere foolishness. Silas had sworn his innocence, his voice thick with the dialect of the Wapping docks and the terror of a man seeing the gallows in the distance. But a man’s word against a constable’s find was a whisper against a thunderclap.

It was then that Penhaligon remembered the summons. In the frantic preparations for the trial, he had neglected a minor civil matter and had dispatched his office boy, a sharp, quick-witted urchin named Finn, to deliver a notice to a Mr. Croft’s clerk. A coincidence of names, nothing more. He had sent Finn to the very same wharf office in Wapping, hoping the boy could find the clerk there before the day’s work began. That had been hours ago. The boy was meant to return to the office, not here. Yet, as Penhaligon despondently scanned the crowded public gallery, he saw a flash of a familiar flat cap. There was Finn, perched on the end of a hard wooden bench, his small, pale face intense with concentration. He caught Penhaligon’s eye and, with a subtle urgency, gestured downwards with his chin.

Penhaligon’s gaze followed the boy’s. Below the gallery, standing near the court’s entrance, was a man he recognized from his brief visit to the Wapping docks: Mr. Croft’s clerk, Mr. Abernathy. He was a thin, fastidious man in a respectable but threadbare coat, and he was here, no doubt, to see the thief put away. But it was not Abernathy’s face that held Penhaligon’s attention. It was his feet. The man was wearing a new pair of boots. They were fine, black leather, with a high polish that gleamed even in the gloom of the court. They were not the boots of a clerk earning a pittance; they were the boots of a man with money to spend. And they were, Penhaligon noted with a jolt, a half-size too large, the leather creasing oddly at the ankle. They were bought in haste, or bought to impress.

A desperate, fragile hope began to uncoil in Penhaligon’s chest. He stood, his voice finding a new, sharper timbre. “My lord, I have just been reminded of one final witness. A crucial point of procedure.” The judge sighed, a sound like gravel being raked. “Be quick about it, Mr. Penhaligon.” Penhaligon turned to the public gallery. “Finn! Come down here. You are called as a witness.” A murmur went through the court. The boy, all elbows and knees, scrambled down the steps and was sworn in, his hand trembling slightly on the Bible.

“Finn,” Penhaligon began, his voice clear and deliberate. “Where were you sent this morning?” “To Mr. Croft’s wharf office on Wapping High Street, sir. To deliver a summons to Mr. Abernathy.” “And did you see Mr. Abernathy?” “I did, sir.” “Was he wearing those boots?” Penhaligon asked, pointing a steady finger at the clerk, who was now shifting uncomfortably on his feet. “Objection!” Davies snapped. “What is the relevance of this man’s footwear?” “It is of the utmost relevance, my lord,” Penhaligon countered, his confidence growing. “I ask the witness to answer.” “No, sir,” Finn said, his voice ringing with the simple truth. “He was not. He was wearing his old ones. They had a hole in the left sole. I saw it when he stepped on a loose floorboard.”

The judge leaned forward, his interest finally piqued. “Continue, Mr. Penhaligon.” “Finn,” the lawyer said, turning back to the boy. “What did you notice about the office when you were there?” “The strongbox on the desk, sir. It had a new lock on it. And on the floor, near the desk, there was a smell of oil. Lamp oil. And a little burn mark on the floorboards.” Penhaligon felt the pieces clicking into place. He turned to the judge. “My lord, I request that the witness, Mr. Abernathy, be examined. The boots he is wearing were not purchased from a respectable cobbler. They are cheap, and they are too big. A man does not buy ill-fitting boots unless he is in a hurry to replace a pair that are now evidence.” He spun back to Finn. “When you were in the office, did you see a pair of old boots anywhere?” Finn’s eyes lit up. “Yes, sir! In the bin by the door, ready for the dust-cart. A pair of old boots, just like the ones he was wearing before. But the left one… it had a hole burned right through the sole. And it smelled of lamp oil.”

A wave of understanding, followed by a wave of murmurs, swept the court. The constable’s face had gone slack. The prosecutor, Davies, was staring at his notes as if they had betrayed him. The judge rapped his gavel. “Mr. Abernathy, you will take the stand.” The clerk’s protest was feeble. Under Penhaligon’s relentless questioning, the story unravelled. Abernathy, seeing Silas Croft lingering near the office, had seen his chance. He had used the lamp oil and a heated poker to burn through the weak sole of his own boot, creating a mark identical to one Silas had accidentally made on the office floor weeks before. He had forced the lock, taken the money, and then, in a panic to establish his own innocence, had bought a new pair of cheap, ill-fitting boots before coming to court to watch the scapegoat hang. The hole in his old boot, a clumsy attempt to destroy evidence, was the very thing that condemned him.

The trial’s conclusion was a blur of motion and sound. Silas Croft was acquitted, his face a mask of stunned disbelief that slowly crumpled into tears of relief. He stumbled from the dock and, ignoring all protocol, clasped Penhaligon’s hand in his own great paw, a grip that spoke more than any words. As the court emptied, Penhaligon found Finn waiting by the door. The boy was trying to look nonchalant, but his eyes were shining. “Good work, Finn,” Penhaligon said, placing a hand on the boy’s thin shoulder. “You have a better eye for detail than half the detectives at Scotland Yard.” He fished a half-crown from his pocket and pressed it into the boy’s palm. Finn’s fingers closed around it, but his gaze was not on the coin. It was on the door through which the freed Silas Croft had just passed, a man walking back into the light of a London afternoon. In the grimy, gaslit streets outside the Old Bailey, the world felt a little less hopeless than it had an hour before.
