The letter lay upon the table in my little chamber like a white stone upon a grave. I had read it twice—nay, thrice—and yet my eyes returned to it, tracing the neat, clerkly characters as though repetition might alter their import. The Bank of Wrenfield had suspended payment. The modest sum which represented eight years of toil—of early rising and late watching, of the slow accumulation of guineas hoarded from a salary already too slender—was gone. Not stolen, the letter said, but lost; swallowed in the failure of a house whose name had stood for probity since before my father's birth. There is a species of ruin which carries with it no drama, no crash of falling walls, no cry of alarm; it is accomplished in silence, in a solicitor's office, in the scratching of a pen, and the world knows nothing of it.

I folded the paper carefully and placed it in the recess of my writing-desk, beneath the packet of letters from my mother, now five years dead. Then I sat quite still, my hands folded in my lap, and looked out at the gray roofs of the city, glistening with the thin rain that had fallen since morning. The bells of Saint Gudula were striking four. In half an hour I must descend to the refectory for the evening meal; at five I must hear the younger girls repeat their French verbs; at seven I must preside over the study-hour, maintaining that silence which is the peculiar discipline of our establishment. These duties remained unchanged. The loss of my fortune could not excuse me from them; indeed, it made them doubly necessary, for without them I had neither bread nor shelter.

I remember that I rose presently and bathed my face in cold water, and looked at myself in the small mirror above the basin-stand. The face that regarded me was not young—I was then thirty-four—but it was not, I think, without a certain composure. The eyes were grave; the mouth had learned, in long years of self-command, to hold its peace. I said to myself, in the plain words which I have always found most serviceable: "Thou art alone, and thou hast nothing. Thou must work, and thou must hold thy tongue." And having said this, I went down to the refectory as usual.

Mademoiselle Dupont, our directress, presided at the head of the long table. She was a woman of fifty, with a face like a winter apple—ruddy, wrinkled, and shrewd. Her eyes, small and black, missed nothing; but her tongue was less active than her observation, and I had learned in three years to respect her silences. She spoke to me that evening of the new pupil, a child of eleven, who had wept at her first Latin lesson.

"Elle est trop sensible," said Mademoiselle Dupont, helping herself to a second portion of stewed prunes. "You will have patience with her, Mademoiselle Grey."

I answered that I would, and that sensibility in a pupil was not, in my estimation, a fault to be corrected, but a quality to be guided. Mademoiselle Dupont inclined her head, and observed that I was perhaps too indulgent; but she said it without acrimony, and I knew that she was not displeased.

The girl, Lucie Varennes, came to me after supper in the study-hall. She was a thin child, with dark eyes too large for her small face, and hair that escaped perpetually from its ribbon. She stood beside my desk, twisting her fingers in her pinafore, and said, in a voice that trembled: "Mademoiselle, I cannot learn the declensions. My head is stupid. I am not worthy to be here."

I looked at her, and I confess that my heart smote me. For was I not also, in my own way, repeating the same lesson? Was I not also saying to myself, in the secrecy of my soul: "Thy head is stupid; thou art not worthy; thou hast no place in the world"? I laid my hand upon her shoulder and drew her closer.

"Ecoute, ma petite," said I. "The head is not stupid which acknowledges its own difficulty. It is the empty vessel that makes the loudest sound. Thou shalt learn the declensions, and I will teach thee a method. But first, dry thine eyes; for tears are not arguments, and I have never yet seen a verb conjugated by weeping."

She smiled—a watery, uncertain smile—and I set her beside me, and we worked together for an hour. By the time the clock struck eight, she could repeat the first declension without error, and her face had lost its look of terror. She curtsied when she left me, and said: "Good-night, Mademoiselle. You are very good."

"Good-night, my child," I answered. "Be diligent, and fear nothing."

When she was gone, I sat alone in the great, dim room, with the lamp before me and the rain still beating against the windows. The silence was profound; the fifty girls had gone to their dormitory; the corridors were empty. I took out my pencil and a scrap of paper, and began to calculate.

My salary was twelve hundred francs a year, paid quarterly. Of this, three hundred went for my board and lodging—a sum deducted by Mademoiselle Dupont with a punctuality that was admirable, if not generous. Two hundred more I had been accustomed to lay by against the future; one hundred I sent to my cousin in England, who had a large family and a small living; the remainder—some six hundred francs—sufficed for my clothes, my books, and the small charities which I could not deny myself. It was not luxury; it was barely competence. But it had been enough, and I had thought myself secure.

Now, I calculated, if I ceased at once to lay by, if I reduced my charities to the minimum, if I wore my dresses a second winter and mended my gloves instead of buying new ones, I could maintain my present mode of life for perhaps eighteen months. At the end of that time I must either ask Mademoiselle Dupont for an advance of salary—a request which my pride revolted from—or seek a situation elsewhere, or starve. The last alternative was not, I reflected, entirely without its attractions; but I had a duty to perform, and I had never yet deserted a post because it was uncomfortable.

I put away the paper, and took up my Bible, which lay always upon my desk. I opened it at the Psalms, and read: "Lord, thou hast been our refuge from one generation to another." The words were familiar; I had read them a hundred times; but that night they came to me with a new force. I thought of my father, who had been a poor clergyman in a Yorkshire village, and who had died leaving nothing but his blessing and his library. I thought of my mother, who had borne poverty with a cheerfulness that I had never been able to imitate. I thought of the long years of teaching, of the girls I had trained, of the good I had perhaps done, of the evil I had certainly avoided. And I said to myself: "Thou hast been a refuge to others; thou shalt not despair of thy own refuge."

The rain ceased towards midnight. I heard the wind rise and sigh among the chimneys, and then die away. I rose and opened the window, and looked out at the city. The moon was up, and the wet roofs shone like silver. In the distance, beyond the dark mass of the cathedral, I could see the river, a pale ribbon of light, winding towards the sea. I thought of England, of the green fields and the gray skies, of the little churchyard where my parents lay, of the bank whose failure had undone me. And I confess that for a moment my heart rose against the injustice of things—that the honest savings of a lifetime should be swept away by the folly or the villainy of men whom I had never seen. But the moment passed. I was not made for rebellion; I had been trained too long in the school of submission. I closed the window, and knelt down, and said my prayers.

The next morning I rose at six, as usual, and went down to the schoolroom. Lucie Varennes was already at her desk, her grammar open before her, her lips moving silently. She looked up as I entered, and smiled—a bright, confident smile, as though the terrors of the night had been forgotten.

"Good-morning, Mademoiselle," she said. "I have learned the second declension also. Shall I say it to you?"

I answered that she should, and that I was glad to see her so diligent. And as I listened to her small, clear voice reciting the Latin cases, I felt that I had my answer. The world had dealt me a hard blow; but I was still standing. I had my work, my pupils, my duty. I would not ask for the advance—not yet. I would live as I had always lived, with economy and with dignity; and if the day came when I must ask, I would ask it as one who demands a right, not as one who begs a favour. For I had learned, in the long years of my obscurity, that a woman's pride is not always a sin, and that self-respect is a better shield than the pity of the world.
