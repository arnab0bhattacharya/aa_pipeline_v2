The shop of Mrs. Miriam Tussock, purveyor of thread, tape, and small household necessaries, stood in a narrow by-lane of the town of Cawdlebury, where the light came reluctantly and the smell of damp plaster contended with the more genial odour of the bakery next door. It was a shop of modest dimensions but of considerable consequence in the estimation of its owner, who had for five-and-twenty years weighed out her pennyworths with a scrupulous honesty that had come to be regarded in the neighbourhood as a species of moral capital, bearing interest in the form of steady custom. Her son, Abel Tussock, had risen from the humble circumstance of a boy who swept the counting-house floor to the position of junior clerk in the Cawdlebury Bank, a progression which his mother regarded with a pride tempered by that prudent anxiety which is the peculiar inheritance of persons who have known the precariousness of small gains.

It was on a Thursday evening in the autumn of the year 1836 that Abel came into the shop with a countenance ill-suited to the humble interior. He had the air of a man who carries news of moment and yet hesitates to deliver it, and his mother, who was arranging skeins of worsted upon a shelf, observed him with that quiet scrutiny which long affection renders both tender and penetrating.

“You are late, my boy,” said she, without turning. “The tea has been standing this half-hour, and the muffin is what you might call leathery.”

“I was detained at Mr. Peckham’s,” said Abel, and the name was pronounced with a certain conscious weight, as though it were not merely a name but a portent.

Mrs. Tussock turned. She was a woman of fifty, with a face that had been handsome and was now shrewd, and eyes that had learned to read the small print of human motive in the ledger of daily dealings. “And what should Mr. Peckham want with you at this hour? It is not the auditing, for that was done at Michaelmas.”

“He was pleased to speak to me on a matter of some importance,” said Abel, seating himself and drawing his chair nearer the fire, though the evening was not cold. “He was pleased to say—that is, he gave me to understand—that he had observed my conduct at the bank with approval, and that he thought me a young man of some capacity.”

“That is very handsome in him,” said Mrs. Tussock, dryly, “considering that you have been his clerk these four years and he has never before discovered it.”

“You are always inclined to think the worst, Mother. He spoke very kindly. He asked me to dine at his house on Sunday next.”

Mrs. Tussock was silent for a moment. She took up the teapot and poured out the tea with a steady hand. “And Miss Peckham?” said she, at length. “Was she present at this conversation?”

Abel coloured. It was a fair, open countenance, his, with the transparent ingenuousness of youth not yet schooled in the concealment of its feelings, and the flush upon it was answer sufficient.

“Miss Peckham,” he said, with an attempt at dignity, “happened to come in. She was very condescending. She said she hoped I should find the Sunday not dull. She is a young lady of very agreeable manners.”

“Agreeable manners,” repeated his mother, and there was that in her tone which might have warned a more experienced man. “And how old is Miss Peckham, Abel?”

“I am sure I do not know. Two or three and twenty, perhaps.”

“She is nine-and-twenty if she is a day,” said Mrs. Tussock, “and her father has been at his wits’ end this twelvemonth to find a husband for her. There was the young surgeon from Brattleby, and there was the lawyer’s son from Wexford, and neither of them would have her. And now you are to dine on Sunday. Abel, my son, do not be a fool.”

The young man set down his cup with an impatient gesture. “I do not see why you should always suppose me to be hunted like a hare. Miss Peckham has been very civil to me. And her father is the manager of the bank. It is not every young man who is invited to dine at Mr. Peckham’s table.”

“No,” said his mother, “and it is not every young man who is wanted for a son-in-law because there is no one else to be had. Do you think Mr. Peckham would have noticed you if he could have got a man with a better coat and a father who was not a shopkeeper? I have lived in this town five-and-twenty years, and I know what the Peckhams are. They are very fine, and they are very proud, and they are very poor. The bank has not paid these three years as it used to pay, and there is talk in the market of the dividends being irregular. And Miss Peckham wants to be married, and her father wants her off his hands, and you are a decent, honest, saving young man who will work like a horse and never ask questions. That is the meaning of your Sunday dinner.”

Abel rose from his chair and walked to the window, where the light of the shop-lamp fell upon his flushed face. He was a young man of three-and-twenty, with a forehead that spoke of intelligence and a mouth that spoke of good-nature, and he was at that moment experiencing the peculiar discomfort of a flattered vanity confronted with an unwelcome truth. He had been conscious, in Miss Peckham’s presence, of a certain agreeable agitation; she had a way of looking at him from under her lashes, and of saying “Oh, Mr. Tussock!” in a tone that seemed to confer upon him a distinction he had never before enjoyed. And he had thought—it is the common folly of young men—that this distinction was the tribute paid to his personal merit, and not the calculated advance of a woman who had a purpose to serve.

“You are very hard,” he said, at last. “You speak as if I were a child to be warned against a trap. I am old enough to manage my own affairs.”

“You are old enough to break your heart and ruin your prospects,” said Mrs. Tussock, with a sigh. “I do not say that Miss Peckham is a bad woman. I daresay she is as good as most, and better than some. But she is not marrying you for love, Abel. She is marrying you because she is tired of her father’s house and afraid of being left upon the shelf. And if you marry her, you will find that you have taken a mistress and not a wife. She will want a servant to wait at table and a carriage to ride in, and she will be ashamed of the shop in the lane, and she will not let me come to see you. And when the bank fails, as I believe it will fail, she will say it was your fault, and she will go back to her father, and you will be left with nothing but the remembrance of your folly.”

The young man stood silent. There are moments in the lives of all of us when the truth is spoken by a voice we cannot disregard, and yet when we are most unwilling to receive it. Abel Tussock had a sincere affection for his mother; he knew her to be a woman of sound sense and unselfish devotion; and he felt the force of her reasoning. But he was also a young man, and the image of Miss Peckham’s dark eyes and the memory of her low voice saying “Oh, Mr. Tussock!” were not to be dismissed by any logic of prudence. He had a vague sense that he was being offered a choice between two worlds—the narrow, honest, laborious world of his mother’s shop, and a wider world of ease and elegance and agreeable society—and he was not yet old enough to know that the wider world is often the narrower, and that the elegance which is bought at the price of self-respect is the most costly of all vanities.

“I shall go to dinner on Sunday,” he said, at last, with a certain sullenness. “It would be rude to refuse. And I do not see that I need commit myself to anything.”

“No,” said his mother, “you need not commit yourself. But you will commit yourself, Abel, because you are a man and because you are flattered. And when you are committed, you will remember that I warned you.”

She turned away and began to fold up the skeins of worsted with a careful hand. The fire crackled in the grate, and the clock upon the wall ticked with that steady, indifferent sound which seems to mock the agitations of mortals. Abel stood looking at her for a moment, and then he took his hat and went out into the dark lane, where the lamplighter was just beginning his rounds, and the first stars were appearing above the chimneys of Cawdlebury.

Mrs. Tussock, left alone, sat down in her chair by the fire and looked into the glowing coals with a face that was very grave and very sad. She was not a woman given to tears, but there was a moisture in her eyes which she wiped away with the corner of her apron, and she said to herself, in a low voice, “He will go his own way, and he will learn what he has to learn. And I shall be here when he comes back, if he comes back at all.”

It is the common fate of mothers to foresee the sorrows of their children and to be powerless to avert them; and it is the common fate of children to mistake the wisdom of experience for the jealousy of age, and to purchase knowledge at the price of suffering. The shop in the lane kept its modest custom, and the bank in the market-place kept its specious credit, and the autumn days passed over Cawdlebury, bringing the Sunday nearer and nearer, and with it the dinner at Mr. Peckham’s, and the dark eyes of Miss Peckham, and the beginning of a history which was to be written, as such histories commonly are, in the plainest and most painful characters of human disappointment.
