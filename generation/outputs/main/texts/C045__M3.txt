


Seraphina had been hatched on the first day of May, in a nest of woven reeds at the southernmost bend of the lake at Harrowmere, the royal estate whose parklands stretched across three counties and whose gardens had been designed, two centuries ago, by a Frenchman with an obsessive fondness for symmetry. The lake itself was the one exception to this symmetry—a long, irregular crescent of water bordered on its western shore by a stand of ancient oaks and on its eastern by a sloping lawn where the royal family sometimes picnicked in summer, though they had not done so for several years, the present king being fond of neither picnics nor his children. It was widely considered among the waterfowl of Harrowmere to be the finest lake in the kingdom, and Seraphina was widely considered to be the finest bird upon it.

She was, it must be said, beautiful. Her plumage was the colour of new cream, unblemished, and her neck curved with such effortless grace that even the old heron, who despised all swans as vain and overfed creatures, had been heard to mutter a grudging compliment when she glided past him in the shallows. Seraphina knew she was beautiful. She had known it from the moment she had first caught her reflection in the still water of a summer morning, and the knowledge had settled into her like a bone—permanent, structural, load-bearing. She carried herself accordingly, her head held high, her wings folded with the precision of a gentleman folding his coat, her wake cutting the lake's surface in a clean silver line behind her.

The ducks of Harrowmere she regarded with a disdain so complete it had curdled, over time, into something resembling indifference, which is always the final and most lethal form of contempt. There were mallards mostly, a dozen or so, led by a grizzled old drake named Aldous who had lost half a foot to a fox in his youth and walked with a corresponding list. There were a pair of tufted ducks who kept to themselves near the reed beds. There were three Canada geese who arrived every autumn and departed every spring with mechanical regularity, like merchants following a trade route. Seraphina tolerated the geese, because they were large and foreign and therefore possessed a certain dignity. The mallards she did not tolerate at all.

It was Aldous who first approached her, on a golden afternoon in early October, when the oaks had just begun to turn and the lake reflected their colours back in broad, smeared strokes of amber and rust. The groundskeeper had thrown bread from the stone bridge, as he did each day, and the scraps had scattered far across the water. Seraphina had claimed the largest cluster—a generous heap of crusts floating near the boathouse—and was eating slowly, deliberately, selecting only the softest pieces and letting the rest drift away.

Aldous paddled toward her with his awkward, hitching stroke, the three younger mallards trailing behind him in a loose formation. "Good afternoon, Seraphina," he said. He was a plain-spoken bird, without airs, and his voice carried the low, flat quality of someone who had long ago stopped being impressed by anything. "There's bread enough here for all of us, if you'd be willing to share the heap you've got."

Seraphina did not look at him. She swallowed a piece of crust with deliberate slowness and then preened a feather on her left wing, examining it as though she had discovered a flaw. "There is bread everywhere on this lake, Aldous," she said. "You need only look for it."

"The wind's pushed most of it to your side," said Aldous. "The young ones are hungry."

Seraphina turned her head at last, regarding him with one dark, perfectly round eye. "Then they may learn to be resourceful," she said. "I am not their mother." She returned to her meal.

Aldous did not argue. He simply turned and paddled away, and the younger ducks followed, and they spent the rest of the afternoon diving for bread scraps that had sunk beneath the surface, pulling up soft, waterlogged morsels from the muddy bottom. They were accustomed to this. Ducks, by nature and by necessity, are scavengers. They know how to find what others have lost or discarded. They know how to live on what the world does not willingly offer them.

Seraphina watched them go with a faint sense of satisfaction. The hierarchy of the lake was, in her view, natural and correct. Swans were made for the surface—for gliding, for being seen, for receiving what was freely given. Ducks were made for the bottom, for grubbing, for making do. It was not cruelty. It was simply the order of things, as fixed and unarguable as the turning of the seasons.

And the seasons did turn. October bled into November, and November hardened into December, and the cold came down on Harrowmere like a hand pressed flat against the earth. The oaks lost their leaves and stood bare and black against a sky the colour of wet slate. The groundskeeper wore a greatcoat now and threw his bread with red, chapped hands, then retreated quickly to his cottage by the gate. The Canada geese departed in a clamour of wings on a morning in late November, their calls echoing across the parkland like a fanfare played on broken instruments. The heron disappeared to whatever secret place herons go. The tufted ducks vanished into the reeds and were not seen again.

It was the mallards who remained, as they always did, and Seraphina, who had never wintered at Harrowmere before and did not fully understand what was coming.

The freeze began on a still night in mid-December. Seraphina felt it before she saw it—a strange, spreading numbness in the water beneath her, as though the lake were slowly losing its memory of being liquid. By morning, a skin of ice had formed along the margins, white and fragile, and by the following morning it had thickened and spread until only a patch of open water remained near the centre, dark and small and very cold. Seraphina sat upon it, her feathers fluffed against the chill, and told herself it would pass.

It did not pass. Each day the circle of open water shrank. Each night the cold deepened, and the ice crept inward, pale and patient and inexorable. Seraphina could no longer reach the shore, could no longer walk the banks where the groundskeeper threw his bread. She was confined to a diminishing patch of black water, surrounded by a widening field of white, and the bread no longer reached her. The wind carried the scraps to the ice, where they lay scattered and unreachable, like coins on a counter beyond a gate.

She was hungry. She was, for the first time in her life, truly hungry, and the sensation appalled her. It was not merely the emptiness in her belly but the knowledge that accompanied it—the knowledge that she did not know what to do. She had never needed to know. The surface of the lake had always been hers, and the bread had always come to her, and the world had always arranged itself around her beauty like a room arranged around a chandelier.

On the third day of the deep freeze, she heard wings and looked up. Aldous was flying low across the ice, the three younger mallards behind him, their small bodies quick and purposeful against the grey sky. They were not heading toward her. They were heading south, toward the far end of the lake, where the groundskeeper's cottage sat beside a stone outflow where a stream fed into Harrowmere. The moving water there, Seraphina knew, would not freeze. She had seen it from a distance but had never thought to go there. It was a cramped, undignified spot, hemmed in by rocks and overhung with brambles. No swan would choose it.

Aldous did not choose it either. He simply went there, because it was what the situation required, and ducks, unlike swans, are not governed by aesthetics. They are governed by survival, which is a humbler and more reliable compass.

Seraphina watched them disappear over the stand of oaks. She sat on her shrinking circle of water. The ice clicked and groaned beneath her, and the cold pressed against her feet like a tightening fist. She thought of calling out to them, of asking to be shown the way, and the thought filled her with a shame so sharp it was almost physical. She had refused them bread. She had called them beneath her. And now she needed them, and the needing itself felt like a kind of death—the death of the bird she had believed herself to be.

But the ice did not care about her pride, and the cold did not negotiate, and by evening her circle of water was no larger than a washbasin, and she could feel the freeze climbing her legs, and she understood with perfect, terrible clarity that the bird she had believed herself to be would die here, on this lake, in this small prison of her own choosing, unless she became a different bird entirely.

She called out. Her voice broke the winter silence—a thin, reedy cry, nothing like the elegant trumpet she had imagined it to be. It sounded, she thought with horror, rather like a duck.

And from somewhere beyond the oaks, she heard an answer.

They came at first light, Aldous and the three younger ones, skimming low across the ice. They landed on the frozen surface near her and walked toward her on their flat, splayed feet—those ungraceful feet she had always found so vulgar—and Aldous looked at her with his plain, steady eye and said nothing at all for a long moment.

Then he said, simply, "This way. Follow us. The water's open by the outflow. There's bread there too—the groundskeeper scatters it on the bank."

She did not thank him. She could not, not yet. But she followed. She spread her wings and rose from the last dark patch of water and flew, low and heavy and ungraceful, across the ice, behind a one-footed drake and three young mallards who had never once been told they were beautiful and who had survived, and would continue to survive, because they had learned long ago that beauty is not a currency the winter accepts.

At the outflow, the water ran dark and free, and the bread lay on the bank in a scatter of brown and gold, and Seraphina ate beside the ducks, in the mud, among the brambles, and said nothing, and was warm.
