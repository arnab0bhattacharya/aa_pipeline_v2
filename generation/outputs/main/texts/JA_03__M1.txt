It is a truth very little acknowledged, that a young woman of sense and some consequence in her neighbourhood, when she has once established a reputation for judgement, will find that reputation the most troublesome of all her possessions. Miss Eleanor Ashby had arrived at the age of three-and-twenty with a character for penetration so well settled, that her opinion was sought upon every subject from the price of wool to the propriety of a new bonnet, and she had never been known to give it without a degree of deliberation which satisfied the world, and, what was of more consequence, satisfied herself.

Her godmother, the excellent Mrs. Ferrars of Ashbourne Park, was a lady who thought a good marriage the only rational end of a young woman’s education, and who, having no daughter of her own, had taken her niece, Miss Lydia Pembroke, under her immediate protection. It was therefore with no small degree of importance that she summoned Eleanor to her side one morning, and, after some preliminary talk of the weather and the late Mrs. Anstey’s rheumatism, came to the point.

“My dear Eleanor,” she began, “I know your judgement is better than most people’s, and better than my own, for I am all impulse and feeling, and you are all sense. I have three gentlemen in my head for my niece, and I cannot for the life of me decide which is the fittest man. I must beg you to consider them for me.”

Eleanor, who was not unused to being consulted upon matters of the heart, received this commission with a calm pleasure. She felt the weight of the trust, and was sensible of the compliment it paid to her understanding. “I shall be very happy to assist you, ma’am,” she replied. “Pray, who are the gentlemen?”

“There is Mr. Cadwallader, of course,” said Mrs. Ferrars. “He is respectable, has a good living at St. Jude’s, and his manners are unexceptionable. Then there is young Captain Fortescue, who is here on leave. He is very handsome, to be sure, and has a fine air about him; but he is a soldier, and I do not know what that may mean for a wife’s peace of mind. And then there is Mr. Greville, who has the estate next to ours. He is a man of very good property, and not at all disagreeable, though he does talk a great deal about his drainage.”

Eleanor listened with attention. “And what is Miss Pembroke’s own opinion on the subject?”

“Oh, Lydia!” cried Mrs. Ferrars, with a wave of her hand. “She is a good girl, but she has no thoughts of her own in these matters. She will be guided by us. She is all sensibility, and would be quite lost without a firmer mind to direct her. That is why I have applied to you.”

For the next week, Eleanor applied herself to the task with a seriousness worthy of the occasion. She observed the three candidates at church, at a card party at the vicarage, and during a walk about the village green. She weighed their merits with a nicety that would have done credit to a philosopher. Mr. Cadwallader’s piety was of a quiet, enduring sort, which she thought a very good foundation for domestic life; but his conversation, being chiefly of sermons and tithes, was unlikely to afford much entertainment. Captain Fortescue was all that was charming in society; he had a ready wit and a gallant manner, and Eleanor could not deny the pleasure of his company. Yet she saw in him a certain restlessness, a fondness for admiration, which she feared might prove a poor companion for a quiet wife. Mr. Greville, she concluded, possessed the most solid advantages. His property was secure, his character was steady, and if his talk was of turnips and tenants, it at least spoke of a mind engaged with the practical duties of life. A wife, she reasoned, could learn to endure a discourse on drainage, but she could not so easily learn to endure poverty or a fickle temper.

Having settled the matter in her own mind, she felt a serene confidence. She had performed her duty with diligence and impartiality, and the conclusion was as clear as daylight. She repaired to Ashbourne Park to deliver her verdict.

She found Mrs. Ferrars and her niece in the morning-room, the one engaged in some needlework, the other gazing pensively out of the window. Eleanor, with a sense of importance she could not entirely suppress, stated her case. She spoke of the Captain’s charm and the Vicar’s goodness, but dwelt with most force on the quiet virtues and substantial comforts offered by Mr. Greville. “He is a man,” she concluded, “on whom a woman may depend for a life of security and respect. His understanding is good, his situation is unexceptionable, and his temper, though not lively, is even. I am confident he is the most suitable choice.”

Mrs. Ferrars listened with great satisfaction. “I knew I was right to apply to you, Eleanor! You have seen it all so clearly. What do you say, my dear Lydia? Is not Mr. Greville an excellent match?”

A silence, which Eleanor at first attributed to maidenly reflection, fell upon the room. Miss Pembroke, who had turned from the window, looked not at her aunt, but at Eleanor. Her face was pale, but her eyes held a strange, determined light.

“I am very much obliged to you, cousin,” she said, in a voice barely above a whisper. “You have been most kind.”

“Well, speak up, child!” said Mrs. Ferrars. “Have you nothing to say to such a well-reasoned argument?”

“I am afraid,” said Miss Pembroke, “that I cannot accept Mr. Greville.”

The words fell into the quiet room with the force of a stone thrown into a still pond. Eleanor felt a shock of surprise, quickly followed by a flush of irritation. It was not merely that her advice was rejected, but that it was rejected without any apparent reason. “May I ask, Lydia,” she said, with a coolness that surprised herself, “what objection you can possibly have to a gentleman so eminently qualified to make you happy?”

Miss Pembroke’s colour rose. “My objection is not to his qualifications, but to my own feelings.”

“Feelings!” cried Mrs. Ferrars. “What have feelings to do with it? Eleanor has laid the whole thing before you. She has considered it for you.”

“But I have considered it for myself,” Lydia replied, and for the first time, her voice gained strength. “And I find I cannot accept him. I have already given my word to another.”

The two older women stared at her. Eleanor, whose pride was now thoroughly wounded, felt a cold dread creeping over her. “To whom?” she demanded.

Lydia’s gaze did not waver. “To Mr. Harding.”

The name meant nothing to Eleanor at first. She searched her memory. Mr. Harding? It was the curate. A man of no fortune, no particular family, and so quiet and unassuming as to be almost invisible in society. He was a person one passed in the lane and scarcely noticed. The idea was so absurd, so utterly at odds with every principle of sense and propriety, that Eleanor could only suppose the girl was labouring under some strange delusion.

“Mr. Harding?” she repeated, her voice sharp with disbelief. “The curate? Lydia, you cannot be serious. What can you mean?”

“I mean that I have promised to marry him,” said Lydia, with a simple dignity that was more disconcerting than any passionate declaration. “We have known each other these six months. He is not rich, it is true, but he is good and kind, and I love him. I have accepted him.”

Eleanor felt the ground shift beneath her. All her careful reasoning, her elegant analysis of character and circumstance, seemed to dissolve into air. She had judged the three gentlemen on their merits, but she had omitted from her calculation the one element which, it now appeared, was the only one of any real consequence. She had considered the men, but she had not truly considered the woman. In her pride of judgement, she had treated her cousin as a problem to be solved, not as a person with a heart of her own. The silence that followed was filled not with the quiet satisfaction of a question answered, but with the humiliating echo of a question not properly asked.
