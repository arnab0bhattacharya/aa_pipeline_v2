The rain, which had begun in the forenoon as a thin, apologetic drizzle, now beat against the windows of the library at Marchmont Hall with a steady, insistent petulance, as though the heavens themselves were out of humour with the business of the afternoon. Arthur Pemberton stood with his back to the fire, his hands clasped behind him, and regarded his cousin with an expression in which the natural kindliness of his disposition was contending with a certain diplomatic habit of reserve—a habit acquired in those Eastern courts where he had spent the last seven years of his life, and where the open countenance was a luxury few men could afford. He had come home to inherit the estate, to take up the duties of a country gentleman, and to marry Miss Eleanor Vance; and he had found, on his arrival, that the first two of these expectations were still within his grasp, while the third had been quietly and, as it seemed to him, perversely, withdrawn.

“I confess I do not understand it,” he said at last, in a tone which he strove to make judicial rather than wounded. “You write to me—or rather, your mother writes to me—that the engagement is at an end; that you have formed a resolution from which nothing can move you; and that I am to seek no explanation beyond your own conscience. I come to London, I present myself at your door, and I am told that you are gone to St. Bartholomew’s Hospital, where you spend your days among the sick, and that you will see me at six o’clock, if I care to wait. I have waited. And now you tell me—what? That you have discovered a vocation. Eleanor, I have seen a great many things in my time, but I have never seen a woman of your position and your prospects throw away a settlement—a very handsome settlement, I may say, for I am not a poor man—for the sake of nursing strangers in a hospital ward.”

Eleanor Vance was sitting opposite to him, in a low chair which the firelight touched with a ruddy glow, while the shadows gathered in the corners of the room. She was not a woman of many words, and her face, which was rather plain than handsome, had the peculiar stillness of one who has learned to listen to the complaints of others without being disturbed by them. She had been a girl of nineteen when Arthur went abroad; she was now twenty-six, and the seven years had done more than mature her features—they had given her a certain quiet authority, as of one who had looked upon suffering and had not been vanquished by it.

“I do not wonder that you are angry,” she said, and her voice was low and even. “I should have wondered more if you had accepted it as a matter of course. But you have been away, Arthur. You have been in countries where, I dare say, the sick are carried to the doors of the hospitals and left there, and the women of the house go on with their embroidery. Here, in London, in the year 1864, there are women—not many, but a few—who have begun to think that the care of the sick is not a thing to be left entirely to the ignorance of hired nurses and the charity of the parish. I am one of them. That is all.”

“That is all!” he repeated, with a short laugh. “You make it sound as if you had taken up a new fancy for water-colours. Eleanor, I have known you since you were a child. You were never a woman to be carried away by a fashion. If you had told me you had lost your fortune, I could have understood it; if you had told me you had lost your faith, I could have understood that too—men lose their faith in the East, I am told, as they lose their health. But this—this nursing, this hospital, this living in a sick-room when you might be living at Marchmont—it is not like you. It is a whim, and I cannot believe that a whim will hold you.”

She looked at him with a steady gravity which had in it something of pity.

“You think it a whim because you have not seen what I have seen,” she said. “You have been busy with treaties and protocols, and I have been busy with something else. Do you know what a hospital ward is like at six o’clock in the morning, Arthur? Do you know what it is to see a man—a labouring man, perhaps, who has never had a day’s illness in his life—brought in with his hand crushed by a machine, and to know that if there is no one to dress it, and watch it, and keep the fever down, he will die, and his children will go to the workhouse? I have seen that. I have seen a woman die in childbirth because there was no one to attend her but a drunken midwife. I have seen children—little children, Arthur—die of a fever that might have been checked if there had been one person in the house who knew enough to keep them clean. And I have come to think that a woman who has health and strength and a little knowledge, and who does not use them, is as much a traitor to her kind as a soldier who runs away from his post.”

Arthur moved uneasily. He was not a hard man, and there was that in her voice which stirred him in spite of himself; but he was also a practical man, and he had a practical man’s distrust of enthusiasm.

“That is all very well,” he said, “as a theory. But you are not a soldier, Eleanor; you are a woman, and a woman with a position to maintain. You have duties to your family, to your name, to the estate which is to be your home. Do you think I would not do my duty by the poor? I would subscribe to a hospital; I would build a wing, if it were wanted; I would send a doctor to the village. But to give up your life—to give up me—for the sake of nursing strangers—it is not reasonable. It is not—forgive me—it is not quite sane.”

She rose from her chair, and going to the window, stood looking out at the rain, which was now falling more heavily than ever. When she turned again, there was a faint colour in her cheeks, and her eyes had a light in them which he had not seen before.

“You are going to judge me,” she said, “and you have not seen. You have not seen the ward at St. Bartholomew’s; you have not seen the women who come there—women who have never had a day’s happiness in their lives, and who yet bear their pain with a patience that would put a saint to shame. You have not seen the look in a man’s eyes when he is told that he will live, and knows that his wife and children will not starve. You have not seen the work that is being done there by women who have given up—as I have given up—the things that the world calls pleasures. Come and see it, Arthur. Come to-morrow morning, at six o’clock, and I will show you the ward. If, after you have seen it, you still think me mad, I will not ask you to think otherwise. But do not judge me until you have seen.”

He was silent for a moment. The rain beat against the panes, and the fire hissed and sputtered in the grate. He had come to London prepared to be injured, prepared to be magnanimous, prepared to forgive; and he found himself confronted with something which he did not understand, and which he was not quite sure that he wished to understand.

“You ask a great deal,” he said at last, “when you ask me to get up at six o’clock in the morning to visit a hospital. But I will come. I will come, Eleanor, not because I think it will alter my opinion, but because I would rather know the worst than be left in the dark. And if I come,” he added, with a return of his old manner, “you must not expect me to be converted. I am a diplomatist, you know, and we are not easily converted.”

She smiled—a faint, tired smile, which had in it more of sadness than of mirth.

“I do not expect you to be converted,” she said. “I only ask you to see. The rest must be as it may.”

And with that he took his leave, and went out into the rain, which had now settled into a steady downpour, and made his way back to his hotel, wondering, as he went, whether the woman he had left behind him was a saint or a madwoman, and whether, if he went to the hospital in the morning, he should find the answer to the riddle.
