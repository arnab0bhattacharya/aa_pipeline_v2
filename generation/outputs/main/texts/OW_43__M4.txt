The morning-room of the Marchmonts’ house in Belgrave Square was an essay in lilac and old gold, and Mrs. Marchmont, who had furnished it, was understood to have said that she would rather be misunderstood in a beautiful room than understood in a dull one. It was therefore with a certain sense of outrage—aesthetic quite as much as moral—that she received the news which the butler, in the manner of one who has rehearsed a disagreeable part, conveyed to her upon a silver salver.

“The *Morning Chronicle*, madam. His lordship desired that you should see it at once.”

Aurelia Marchmont took up the paper as she might have taken up a dead mouse. Her husband’s charities had always been to her a species of expensive draught, blowing eternally under the door of her comfort, and she had borne them with the graceful resignation of a woman who knows that the poor must be supported, since Providence, in its infinite unwisdom, has declined to remove them. But to read, in a column of poisonous print, that Sir Lucian Marchmont, Baronet, had given away half a million in order to buy himself a seat in Paradise at a discount, and had chosen his charities as a clever man chooses his neckties—for effect—this was to have her private grievances elevated into a public scandal, and her husband’s virtues turned into a speculation.

“The wretches!” she said. “The odious, ink-stained wretches! And they have spelled ‘benefactions’ with two f’s.”

It was at this point that Sir Lucian entered, wearing the expression of a man who has been rained upon and has decided not to mention it. He was a large, fair, kindly person, with the look of one who has spent his life in being surprised by nothing and disappointed by very little; and he stood in the doorway with his hands behind him, like a schoolboy who has been sent for.

“You have seen it, Aurelia.”

“I have seen it, and I have never seen anything so disgraceful in my life—not even the curtains at the Hanburys’.”

“It is not pleasant,” he admitted. “But it is not altogether untrue. One gives, and one likes to be thanked. I am not a saint; I am only a man with a cheque-book and a conscience, and I confess that when the hospital board named a ward after me I was pleased. Is that very dreadful?”

“It is very human,” she said, and was astonished at herself, for she had meant to say something quite different.

“The *Chronicle* says I bought my reputation. Well, my dear, reputation is the one thing that is never bought too dear, because it is never bought at all. It is given, like a cold, or a knighthood. One can only be accused of it.”

“Lucian,” said his wife, and here she rose from her lilac sofa, “you are talking like a leader-writer. Do not. It is not becoming in a man of your size. You have given away half your fortune, and I have been very tiresome about it, and I have said that the money would have been better spent on a season at Homburg, and I have meant it, and I have been a fool. There is no one in London who could have done it but you, and there is no one in London who would have. And now they say you did it for a paragraph.”

“They say it because it is the only way they can understand it,” said Sir Lucian. “A good deed that is not explained is a reproach to everybody who has not done it. So they explain it, and are comfortable. It is a sort of moral drainage.”

“It is a sort of moral sewage,” said Aurelia with decision. “And I shall go to the office of the *Chronicle* this afternoon and tell them so.”

“My dear, you will do nothing of the kind.”

“I shall go,” she repeated, “and I shall take the brougham, and I shall wear the violet velvet, because it is the colour of a woman who is in the right. And if the editor is rude to me I shall be ruder to him, for I have had a great deal of practice.”

Sir Lucian looked at her with the helpless admiration of a man who has lived for twenty years with a woman and has never quite got over it.

“Aurelia,” he said, “I believe you are defending me.”

“I am defending,” she said, “the only man who ever gave away a fortune and never once mentioned it at dinner. Do you know what I have had to endure? I have had to sit at my own table and hear you talk about the drainage of Poplar while I wished to talk about the new piece at the Haymarket. And I have called you a bore, Lucian, and I have meant it, and I was wrong. You are not a bore. You are a public benefactor, and I have been a very great snob, and I intend to be one no longer.”

“You will find it a great sacrifice,” he murmured.

“Everything agreeable is a sacrifice,” she returned. “That is the whole philosophy of the world, and I have never heard it better put.”

At this moment there was announced the one person in London whom Mrs. Marchmont could not have wished to see—Mr. Claude Vavasour, of the *Morning Chronicle*, a young man of elegant appearance and exhausted principles, who wrote his paragraphs as other men write their sins, with a fine air of not being found out.

“I have come,” he said, with the composure of one who has brought a small bomb and intends to leave it on the table, “to offer Sir Lucian an opportunity of reply.”

“How good of you!” cried Aurelia. “And how exactly like the *Chronicle*! You attack a man in the morning, and in the afternoon you offer him the use of the very column in which you attacked him. It is like giving a man a handkerchief after you have blacked his eye.”

“My dear lady, the *Chronicle* is not a person; it is a principle.”

“Then it is a principle without manners, and I shall not offer it a cup of tea.”

Mr. Vavasour smiled the smile of a man who has been in many drawing-rooms and has left a little of his soul in each.

“Sir Lucian,” he said, turning from her, “the public is not unreasonable. It merely asks why a man of your position should suddenly distribute half his income among the undeserving.”

“The undeserving!” said Aurelia. “Do you know, Mr. Vavasour, that my husband has fed a thousand people who never said thank you, and educated a hundred boys who grew up to vote against him? And you call that a speculation! If he had wanted a reputation he would have bought a racehorse. It would have been cheaper, and infinitely more respectable.”

“Reputation,” said Mr. Vavasour, with the air of one producing an epigram he had kept in cotton-wool, “is the shadow of virtue.”

“Then virtue,” said Aurelia, “must be very tired of standing in the sun. And now, Mr. Vavasour, I will tell you something for your paper, and you may print it or not, as you please. My husband gave away half his fortune because he is a good man, and I have been a bad wife, and I have scolded him for it, and I am ashamed. There is no speculation in that, and there is no paragraph in it either, and I dare say you will not understand it. But it is the truth, and the truth is the one thing that is never fashionable.”

There was a silence, in which the clocks of Belgrave Square seemed to tick with a certain embarrassment. Mr. Vavasour looked at her, and for a moment the weariness went out of his face, and something like a boy looked out of it—a boy who had once believed in things before he learned to spell them correctly.

“I shall not print it, Mrs. Marchmont,” he said. “It is too good for the *Chronicle*.”

“Then print the other thing,” she said, “and be damned to you. I beg your pardon. That is not the sort of thing one says in a lilac room.”

“It is the sort of thing,” said Sir Lucian, “that I have wanted to say for twenty years, and have never had the courage.”

Mr. Vavasour bowed himself out with the grace of a man who has been defeated in a drawing-room and intends to be defeated in a better one. When the door closed, Aurelia stood for a moment looking at her husband, and then she laughed—the little laugh of a woman who has discovered that she has been in love for years without knowing it.

“Lucian,” she said, “I have been defending you all the afternoon, and I find I have been defending my own taste. I married you because you were the only man in London who did not talk about himself, and I have been punishing you for it ever since.”

“And now?”

“And now,” she said, “I shall give a dinner-party, and I shall ask all the people who have been unkind about you, and I shall put you at the head of the table and be proud of you, and it will be the most disagreeable evening of my life. But I shall enjoy it.”

“My dear,” said Sir Lucian, “you are a great deal better than you pretend to be.”

“That,” she said, “is the only thing about me that is not original. And now, go and write a cheque for something, or I shall begin to cry, and I have on the violet velvet, and it is not made for tears.”
