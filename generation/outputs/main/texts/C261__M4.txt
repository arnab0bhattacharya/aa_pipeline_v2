The attic smelled of dust and linseed oil, and those two scents had become, for Clara Wetherby, the smell of freedom. She had cleared the space herself over a fortnight in the autumn of 1912, dragging out broken chairs and trunks of forgotten mourning clothes, scrubbing the floorboards until her knuckles were raw, and carrying up her easel piece by piece so that no one in the house below would think to ask what she was doing. The window at the gable end was small and grudging, but it faced north, and on clear mornings the light that fell across her canvas was cool and honest and hers alone.

Her mother believed the attic was where Clara went to sulk. Her brother Arthur believed it was where she went to read novels. Only Mrs. Pell, the cook, who had been in the house since before Clara was born, suspected the truth, and Mrs. Pell said nothing, though she sometimes left a second helping of pudding on the stairs with a note that read, *For the young lady what climbs.*

The marriage had been settled upon her the way a stain is settled into linen — gradually, and then all at once. Mr. Edgar Vane was a solicitor of forty-two with a house in Highgate and a manner of speaking that made every sentence sound like the reading of a will. He had been to dinner twice. On the second occasion he had complimented the soup and inquired whether Clara was fond of children, and her father had laughed heartily and said she would learn to be. After he had gone, Clara stood in the hallway with her hands pressed flat against the wallpaper and heard her mother say, through the parlour door, *She'll have a comfortable life, and we'll not have to worry.*

It was not cruelty. That was the thing she could never make anyone understand. Her family loved her in the way one loves a clock that keeps good time, and they could not see that a clock might wish to be a bird.

She had shown her work to Mr. Hollis in the spring, in the little studio behind the art school on Flask Walk where he taught the Saturday classes for young ladies. She had carried three canvases wrapped in brown paper and waited an hour while he finished with the others. He was a thin, tired man with paint beneath his fingernails and a reputation, in certain circles, for honesty. He looked at her paintings for a long time, which she had taken for a good sign, and then he had set them down and said, not unkindly, that she had a pleasant feeling for colour but no grasp of form, that her figures were like dolls stuffed with sawdust, and that the world was full of women who painted pleasantly, and most of them were happier for having stopped. He had patted her hand. He had said she should not take it to heart.

She had taken it to heart. She had taken it up to the attic with her and set it on the shelf beside the turpentine, and she had looked at it every day since, and she had painted anyway.

The attic paintings were not pleasant. That was the private defiance of them. If she had no grasp of form, then she would grasp at something else — at the way the light came through the window at four o'clock in November, thin as watered milk; at the huddled shape of the chimney pots against a bruised sky; at Mrs. Pell's hands, which she had sketched one afternoon when the cook had come up to see the room and stood a moment too long in the doorway, wiping her palms on her apron. She painted her own face from a shaving mirror propped on a stack of hymnals, and she did not make it pretty. She painted the feeling of being told no, which was a grey-green colour with a hard edge to it, and the feeling of being told yes by no one, which was the same colour but with more air in it.

She kept a rag over the canvases when she was not working, though no one ever came up. The door at the bottom of the attic stairs had swollen in the damp and stuck in its frame, and it made a sound like a cough when it opened, which gave her warning. In three years only two people had climbed those stairs: Mrs. Pell, twice, and once Arthur, who had poked his head in, sneezed, and gone away again, and she had heard him tell their mother that Clara was doing something dusty up there and it was probably best left alone.

The wedding was set for the following June. In January she began a painting that she did not understand while she was making it. It was larger than the others — she had to turn the easel sideways to get it through the door — and it showed the attic window from the inside, but the window was not a window. It was a mouth, open, with the grey London light pouring out of it like breath. She worked on it for eleven weeks. She stopped going down to meals. Her mother sent up trays and Clara ate them standing, with a brush in her hand, and the paint got into the food and the food got into the paint, and she did not care. She had never in her life been so certain of anything, and she could not have said what it was she was certain of.

She finished it on a Tuesday in April, and she stood back from it in the failing light and felt nothing at all, which she had learned was the way of it when a thing was truly done. Then she heard the cough of the door in its frame.

It was her mother, out of breath, holding a lamp, her hair coming down. She had come up because Clara had missed dinner again and because there was a letter from Mr. Vane about the arrangements, and because, Clara understood later, some small accumulated worry had finally outweighed the habit of leaving her alone. She stood in the doorway with the lamp lifted and looked at the painting, and Clara watched her face and saw nothing in it that she could name.

Her mother was silent for a long moment. The lamp hissed. Below them, muffled by the floorboards, Arthur was playing the piano badly.

*What is it,* her mother said at last, and it was not a question about the subject, or about the mouth, or about the light. It was the question of a woman standing in a room she had never entered, in her own house, looking at something that had been made there without her knowledge.

Clara wiped her hands on her rag. She thought of Mr. Hollis and his sawdust dolls, and of Mr. Vane and the soup, and of the three years of quiet and turpentine and the north light falling on her like a promise.

*It's mine,* she said.

Her mother looked at her for a while longer. Then she set the lamp down on the trunk by the door, and she came further into the room, and she looked at the painting again, closer, the way one looks at a face to see whether it is telling the truth.

*There's more of them,* Clara said, and drew the rag off the others, one by one, and the attic filled up with the grey-green light of them, and neither of them said anything for a long time, and the piano went on playing, badly, below.
