The fair at Millwall Marshes was a mud-slicked riot of noise and colour, a place where the stench of tallow and fried onions warred with the damp, riverine air. Silas Croft stood before his small, striped booth, his voice a practiced rasp that cut through the din. He was a man made of sharp angles and frayed edges, but his eyes, when they fell upon his prize, held a soft, proprietary gleam. There, in a pen of clean straw, sat Barnaby. Barnaby was a pig, a Berkshire of considerable girth and intelligence, and he was Silas’s entire world. With a delicate touch of a switch, the pig would nod, shake his head, and prod a numbered card with his snout to solve simple sums, a feat that drew gasps and pennies from the gaping crowd.

“Observe, ladies and gentlemen, the porcine prodigy! The thinking swine! A marvel of nature and nurture, a scholar in the sty!” Silas would boom, his patter as worn and reliable as his boots. But today, a new sound intruded upon his rhythm. It was a loud, boastful laughter from a larger, gaudier tent opposite his own, where a man named Casimir Vane was performing with a troupe of acrobats. Vane was all flash and swagger, with a waistcoat the colour of a sunset and a voice that promised miracles he rarely delivered. He caught Silas’s eye and winked, a gesture of pure, unadulterated contempt.

After the show, as Silas counted his meagre earnings, Vane sauntered over, his shadow falling long and dark across the straw. “A charming little trick, Silas,” he said, his voice smooth as oil. “But the beast is wasted on you. A mind like that needs a proper stage, a proper master.” Silas bristled, clutching his canvas bag of coins. “He’s not a trick. He’s my partner.” Vane laughed. “Everything has a price. Name one for the pig.” Silas’s pride, a brittle and precious thing, flared. “He’s not for sale.” Vane’s eyes glittered. “Perhaps not for coin. But for a chance to win something grander? A game of cards. My prize against yours. Your pig against my ‘Celestial Cabinet of Curiosities’.”

The cabinet was a magnificent thing of polished mahogany and inlaid brass, filled with strange and wonderful objects: a stuffed two-headed calf, a jar of mermaids’ tears (which looked suspiciously like salt water), and a music box that played a hauntingly beautiful tune. Silas, who had always coveted the cabinet, felt his resolve weaken. It was a foolish, desperate gamble, but the promise of such a prize, the chance to elevate his entire show, was a siren’s call. They played in the corner of a nearby ale tent, the air thick with smoke and the smell of stale beer. The game was a simple one, ‘Put and Take,’ a matter of pure chance. With each turn of the spinner, Silas’s heart hammered against his ribs. For a time, he was ahead. But luck is a fickle mistress. The final spin was slow, deliberate. The needle clicked past Silas’s mark and landed firmly on Vane’s. The silence that followed was heavier than any sound. Vane smiled, a thin, triumphant slash across his face. “I’ll collect my property in the morning, Silas.”

Silas stumbled out into the twilight, the world a blur of coloured lights and mocking faces. The Celestial Cabinet, once a beacon of hope, now seemed a gaudy tombstone. He had lost Barnaby. He sank onto an upturned crate behind the pie-seller’s stall, the day’s triumphs turning to ash in his mouth. It was there that old Finnian found him. Finnian was a mountain of a man, his apron perpetually dusted with flour, his face a roadmap of good humour and grease. He sold hot pies from a cart that steamed like a dragon’s nostril, and his booming laugh was as much a part of the fair as the carousel music.

“Lost your best customer, have you, Silas?” Finnian asked, his voice gentle for all its volume. Silas could only shake his head, the words caught in his throat. He explained the story in a low, broken monotone. Finnian listened, his jolly expression hardening into one of grim resolve. “A thief and a cheat, that Vane,” he grumbled, wiping his hands on his apron. “Thinks he can just take a man’s livelihood? Not while there’s hot pie in England.” He clapped a heavy hand on Silas’s shoulder. “We’ll get him back. But we can’t do it with force. Vane’s got two big lads working for him. We’ll have to be cleverer.”

An hour later, a council of war was convened behind Finnian’s cart. With them were two others: Penny, a waif of a girl who sold bunches of watercress from a wicker basket, her eyes wide and watchful, and Elias, a man with a melancholic face who sold broadside ballads from a sheaf of papers, his voice a reedy tenor that sang of lost love and highwaymen. Elias was a master of observation, his trade requiring him to watch the crowd for a likely mark, to gauge their mood and interests. Penny, small and easily overlooked, could slip through any crowd, her sharp eyes missing nothing. Together, they formed an unlikely alliance: the pie-man, the ballad-seller, the watercress girl, and the heartbroken showman.

Their plan was born of desperation and ingenuity. They would not try to steal Barnaby back. They would win him back, using Vane’s own arrogance against him. Elias would create the distraction. He had a new ballad, a sensational piece about a dastardly cheating gambler who lost his fortune, and he would sing it with theatrical gusto right outside Vane’s tent, drawing a crowd and, hopefully, Vane’s attention. Finnian would use the commotion to approach Vane’s two hired hands, offering them free pies and a story about a rival showman trying to poison their employer’s livestock, sending them on a wild goose chase to the far end of the fair. Penny’s role was the most crucial. She would use the chaos to get close to Vane’s tent, not to steal, but to observe. She was to find out where Vane kept his valuables, specifically, the playing cards he had used to win Barnaby.

The next morning, as the fair awoke in a misty haze, the plan went into motion. Elias’s voice, sharp and clear, sliced through the morning air. “Come and hear the tragic tale of ‘Villainous Vance, the Cardsharp’s Dance!’” The name was just close enough to Vane’s to be insulting. A crowd gathered, laughing and jeering. Vane emerged from his tent, his face purple with rage. As he stormed towards Elias, Finnian lumbered over to the two bouncers, his pies held aloft like a peace offering. “Gents, a moment! Terrible news! A man in a blue coat was asking about your prize calf, said he had a vial of poison…”

While Vane was occupied, arguing with the ballad-seller and his amused audience, Penny, her basket of watercress held before her like a shield, slipped into the shadows behind his tent. The canvas was loose, and she could see inside. She saw the Celestial Cabinet, and next to it, a small, locked strongbox. But it wasn't the strongbox she was looking for. She scanned the cluttered space: a trunk of costumes, a makeup table. And there, tucked into the corner of a grimy mirror frame, was the deck of cards. They were not ordinary cards. She had seen them from a distance during the game; the backs had a tiny, almost invisible nick in the corner of the Ace of Spades. A marked deck.

She didn't take them. Instead, she scurried back to Silas, her heart thumping. “The cards, Mr. Croft! They’re marked! He cheated you!” That was all they needed. Silas, armed with this knowledge and a sliver of his old confidence, strode out to meet Vane, who was now returning to his tent, flustered and furious. “I want a rematch, Vane,” Silas declared, his voice ringing with newfound authority. Vane sneered. “On what grounds? You have nothing left to wager.” “On the grounds that you are a cheat,” Silas said, loud enough for the lingering crowd to hear. “Your cards are marked. We all saw it. If you are so confident in your luck, play me again. Fairly. Barnaby against the cabinet. Winner takes all.”

Cornered by the public challenge, Vane’s pride would not let him back down. “Fine,” he spat. “A new deck. But the stakes are the same.” They played again, this time in the open, with Finnian, Elias, and Penny forming a silent, watchful circle. The new deck was clean. The game was pure chance once more. The spinner clicked and whirred, a frantic heartbeat in the centre of the gathering. Silas watched the little wooden arrow, his knuckles white. It spun past his mark, past Vane’s, and then, with a final, shuddering click, it landed squarely on Silas’s side.

The cheer that went up from their small group was ragged but joyous. Vane’s face was a mask of disbelief and fury. He had no choice but to concede. Silas walked to the pen, and Barnaby, as if sensing his freedom, trotted out to meet him, nuzzling his hand with a soft, warm snout. The Celestial Cabinet stood on the other side of the clearing, a beautiful, empty promise. Silas looked at it, then at the pig, then at the three faces watching him—Finnian’s broad grin, Elias’s quiet smile, and Penny’s shining eyes. He had won back more than just his partner. He had found a family in the mud and noise of Millwall Marshes. He put a hand on Barnaby’s back, the coarse bristles a familiar comfort. “Come on, old friend,” he said softly. “Let’s go home.” And together, the four of them and the learned pig walked away from the fair, leaving Vane and his gaudy, hollow curiosities behind them in the morning mist.
