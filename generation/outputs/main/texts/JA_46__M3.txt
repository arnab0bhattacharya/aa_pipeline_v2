The town of Chelmsbury, in the county of Essex, had seldom experienced a winter of such animation as that which followed the arrival of the —th regiment of militia. The officers were in general reported to be a very creditable set of young men, but among them all, Captain Lennox was universally allowed to hold the first place in point of consequence and attraction. He possessed a clear estate of two thousand a-year, a person extremely agreeable, and a uniform which was thought to set off his figure to great advantage; circumstances which, united to a certain easy assurance of address, were sufficient to secure him the good opinion of every mother in the neighbourhood, and to make him the object of very decided attention from every daughter.

To the matrons of Chelmsbury, the quartering of the militia in their town was an event of the highest importance, opening as it did a new and promising field for the exercise of their domestic diplomacy. The assembly rooms, which had before echoed only to the measured steps of a few country dances, now became the theatre of nightly contention, where the mothers manoeuvred for precedence, and the daughters displayed their several accomplishments with a zeal which the occasion well deserved. Mrs. Whitmore, a lady of more ambition than prudence, and a widow with four daughters to settle, was among the most active in the field. She had singled out Captain Lennox from the very first evening of his appearance, and had devoted her energies to bringing him forward as a suitor for her eldest girl, a young woman of some personal pretension and less understanding, whose smiles were dispensed with a liberality that argued a total want of that delicacy which is the true ornament of her sex.

Captain Lennox, however, was not wholly without discernment, and the very eagerness with which he was pursued by Mrs. Whitmore and her associates served rather to amuse than to attach him. He was a young man who had always been accustomed to please, and to be pleased with the consciousness of pleasing; and he was not a little surprised to find that there was one young woman in Chelmsbury upon whom his address, his attentions, and his uniform, appeared to be entirely thrown away. This was Miss Elliot, the eldest daughter of Mrs. Elliot, a widow lady of small fortune and considerable good sense, who, with two daughters, occupied a modest house on the outskirts of the town.

Miss Elliot was now three-and-twenty, an age at which a young woman, without the advantage of beauty or fortune, might be thought to have outlived the expectations of romance. She was not handsome; her complexion was without brilliancy, and her features, though regular, wanted that softness which is generally more captivating than mere symmetry. But there was in her countenance an expression of good sense and quiet observation, and in her manner a composed and unaffected civility, which, if it did not immediately command admiration, at least secured respect. She had been brought up by her mother with a due regard to propriety, and without that false indulgence which teaches a young woman to consider every man as a possible lover, and every ball-room as a market for the disposal of her person.

Captain Lennox was at first merely struck by the novelty of finding himself unnoticed. He had been accustomed to receive, as a matter of course, those small tokens of preference which young women are but too ready to bestow upon a man of pleasing exterior and independent fortune; and the discovery that Miss Elliot could listen to his conversation without visible emotion, and could meet his gallantry with nothing more than civil indifference, was a species of mortification to which he was wholly a stranger. He resolved, with the natural perverseness of a mind that has always been gratified, to make himself agreeable in spite of her, and to compel, by the force of his own attractions, that acknowledgment which she seemed disposed to withhold.

He accordingly sought her out at the next assembly, placed himself by her side, and entered into conversation with all the ease and confidence which a consciousness of his own powers could bestow. He spoke of the neighbourhood, of the regiment, of the usual topics of the day, and flattered himself that he was succeeding, when Miss Elliot, with a look of civil curiosity, asked him whether he was fond of reading. The question, so different from the flattering inquiries to which he was accustomed, discomposed him for a moment; he replied that he had not much time for books, but that he was fond of a novel now and then. Miss Elliot smiled slightly, and observed that she had always understood novels to be a very agreeable relaxation, but that she had been more accustomed to hear them praised for their entertainment than recommended for their instruction. She then turned to speak to an elderly gentleman who sat near her, and Captain Lennox found himself dismissed with a politeness which left no opening for reply.

He was vexed, but he was not discouraged. He persuaded himself that Miss Elliot's indifference was merely the result of a studied reserve, assumed for the purpose of increasing his attention; a species of coquetry which, however it might disguise itself under the appearance of coldness, was in reality but a more artful mode of encouragement. He had known young women who had played the same game, and who had ended by yielding with as much readiness as they had at first shown reluctance; and he was not wanting in the confidence which such experience naturally produces.

He redoubled his assiduities. He rode past the Elliots' house every morning with a regularity that could scarcely be accidental; he inquired after their health with an interest that was meant to be affecting; and at the next party, he secured Miss Elliot's hand for two dances, an attention which, from a man of his supposed pretensions, was of no small consequence. Miss Elliot accepted his hand with the same composed civility which marked all her behaviour, danced with him without either evasion or rapture, and at the conclusion of the set, thanked him and withdrew to her mother's side with an air of perfect unconcern.

Captain Lennox was now seriously puzzled. He could not believe that any young woman, situated as Miss Elliot was, without fortune or beauty, could really be insensible to the honour of his preference. He began to suspect that she was acting a part, and that her indifference was assumed; yet he could discover no symptom of consciousness, no glance of secret triumph, no momentary relaxation of that steady composure which seemed to be her natural expression. He was reduced to the humiliating reflection that he had met with a young woman who was either too dull to perceive his merit, or too proud to acknowledge it; and he knew not which alternative was the more disagreeable.

The truth, however, was of a nature which his own vanity would not suffer him to suspect. Miss Elliot was neither dull nor proud, but she was possessed of a degree of good sense which enabled her to form a just estimate of Captain Lennox's character. She had observed him in company, had marked his easy assurance, his love of admiration, and his total unconsciousness that any person could be indifferent to his attentions; and she had privately resolved that a man who was so well satisfied with himself could have but little capacity for being satisfied with any one else. She saw that his preference for her arose not from any real admiration of her character, but from the pique which her indifference had excited; and she was too wise to mistake the irritation of wounded vanity for the warmth of genuine attachment. She had, besides, a sincere regard for a young man of her acquaintance, a Mr. Wallace, the curate of the parish, whose modest worth and steady good sense had long since gained that place in her esteem which Captain Lennox's pretensions were never likely to reach.

Mrs. Elliot, who watched the progress of the affair with the quiet sagacity of a sensible woman, was not sorry to see her daughter's composure. She had no objection to Captain Lennox's fortune, but she had a strong objection to his character, and she was well content that Miss Elliot should conduct herself in a manner which, while it gave no offence, afforded no encouragement. She knew that a young woman who is neither to be flattered by praise nor awed by rank, who receives attention without exultation and repels it without incivility, possesses a power of defence which is far more effectual than any open resistance; and she trusted to her daughter's prudence to preserve her from the danger which the Captain's address might have occasioned to a less guarded mind.

Captain Lennox continued at Chelmsbury until the spring, and during the whole of his stay, he persevered in his attentions to Miss Elliot with a constancy which did credit to his perseverance, if not to his judgment. He was uniformly civil, uniformly assured, and uniformly unsuccessful. When the regiment received orders to march, he took his leave with a regret which was perhaps sincere, though it was not of the deepest kind. He had been disappointed in his wishes, and his vanity was wounded; but his spirits were good, his fortune was unimpaired, and he had no doubt that in the next town where the regiment should be quartered, he should find plenty of young women who would be more sensible to his merits than the cold and unaccountable Miss Elliot.

That young lady, on hearing of his departure, expressed to her mother a hope that Captain Lennox would find himself happy wherever he went, and an opinion that he was a very agreeable young man. Mrs. Elliot smiled, and said she believed him to be so; and the subject was not again renewed. Miss Elliot's heart, as we have hinted, was already engaged to a worthier object; and if the history of her fate should ever be related, it will be found that the man who could distinguish merit where it was unaccompanied by display, and could prefer sense to flattery, was rewarded with that steady and rational affection which Captain Lennox's vanity would never have taught him to value, or his address to obtain.
