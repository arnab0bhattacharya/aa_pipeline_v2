The sea air at Calverley Bay was said to be particularly beneficial for the nerves, and it was this specific, medicinal quality that Mrs. Thorne had cited when selecting it as their summer residence. She had not, however, mentioned the primary object of her visit, which was the expected presence of Lord Ashcombe, a young nobleman of considerable estate and, more importantly, of sufficient age to be considered a husband. It was not that Mrs. Thorne lacked affection for her two daughters, Eliza and Mary; on the contrary, she loved them with a devotion that was entirely practical and strictly directed toward their advancement. She had long since determined that while a handsome face was a pleasant ornament, a title was a necessary foundation, and that the foundation must be laid before the summer season wore thin.

They arrived on a day of such bright, unclouded sky that the white cliffs seemed to glare with an almost offensive purity. Mrs. Thorne, dressed in a gown of pale blue muslin that suggested both economy and elegance, had already surveyed the assembly of visitors with a critical eye. The resort was small, intimate, and, to her satisfaction, devoid of the loud, vulgar families who often plagued the larger watering places. Here, the air was thinner, the conversation more restrained, and the potential for a quiet, advantageous match seemed significantly higher.

"The gentleman I am told is staying at the White Lion," Mrs. Thorne whispered to her eldest, Eliza, as they strolled along the shingle beach. "I am told he is alone, or at least, without any female relations who might claim his affections exclusively. It is a perfect situation."

Eliza, who possessed a disposition of such placid indifference that it bordered on the pathological, merely nodded. She had long ceased to struggle against her mother’s will, finding that resistance was a form of labor she could not afford. Mary, the younger, who had a sharper tongue and a quicker eye for the absurd, offered a slight, almost imperceptible shrug. She had read the accounts of Lord Ashcombe’s character in the London papers: he was described as a man of serious mind, somewhat reserved, and deeply devoted to his studies. To Mary, this sounded less like a romantic prospect and more like a heavy volume one was obliged to read in a dull room.

Their opportunity to observe the object of their ambition presented itself sooner than anticipated. As they were returning from a walk to the church, which stood on a gentle rise above the bay, they encountered a carriage coming down the steep lane. The horses were tired, and the coachman stopped to let them pass. Inside the carriage sat a young man of a strikingly awkward appearance, his face flushed with the heat of the journey, and beside him, a woman of a certain age, with spectacles perched on her nose and a demeanor of stern authority.

Mrs. Thorne halted abruptly, her fan closing with a sharp click. "There he is," she murmured, her voice trembling with a mixture of hope and anxiety. "That must be Lord Ashcombe."

Eliza looked closer. The young man was turning a page of a book with trembling hands, while the woman beside him was tapping him on the arm with a ruler.

"Is that... is that his sister?" Eliza asked, her voice barely audible.

"I cannot imagine," Mrs. Thorne replied, her eyes narrowing. "It is not like him to have a sister of that age and appearance. Unless it is a cousin. A very distant cousin, perhaps. Or a chaperone of the most unpretentious kind."

The carriage passed, and the young lord caught a glimpse of the three women on the path. He started, as if startled by the sight of such concentrated attention, and hid his face in his book. The woman beside him scolded him in a low, sharp voice, and he slumped further into the corner.

Mrs. Thorne stood frozen on the path, her mind racing. A chaperone? A governess? The implications were staggering. If Lord Ashcombe required a governess, it suggested a level of dependence that was, if not disqualifying, at least highly inconvenient. But she was not a woman to be deterred by such minor details. The title was what mattered. The rest could be managed.

"We must pay our respects," she announced, her voice regaining its usual composure, though a slight tremor remained in the tone. "It would be most improper to ignore him, given his station."

The three women walked up to the carriage just as it was stopping at the door of the White Lion. Lord Ashcombe, whose name, as it turned out, was actually Edward, and who was only seventeen years old, looked up from his book with an expression of profound distress. The woman beside him, Miss Hargrave, his governess, introduced him with a stiffness that suggested she had been paid to perform this duty, rather than feeling any natural inclination toward it.

"Lord Ashcombe," Miss Hargrave said, "allow me to present Mrs. Thorne and her daughters."

Mrs. Thorne curtsied with a grace that was entirely mechanical. "We are delighted to make your acquaintance, my lord. We are staying at the same inn. I trust the journey has not been too fatiguing?"

Edward, who was still clutching his book, managed a mumbled reply. "It was... very bumpy, ma'am. The coachman drives too fast."

Mrs. Thorne smiled, a smile that did not reach her eyes. "We shall be sure to recommend a gentler pace for your future travels, my lord. Your health is of such importance."

Eliza and Mary exchanged a glance. In that glance was communicated a volume of shared despair. The boy was not merely awkward; he was terrified. He looked at Mrs. Thorne as if she were a predator, and at his governess as if she were the only safety he knew.

"Shall we join you for tea, my lord?" Mrs. Thorne asked, her voice sweet and inviting. "The sea air is so refreshing, but one finds that a cup of tea in the parlor is a great comfort."

Edward looked at Miss Hargrave. Miss Hargrave looked at her watch. "I am afraid, ma'am, that Lord Ashcombe has his lessons to complete. He has a history essay due tomorrow."

Mrs. Thorne’s smile faltered for a fraction of a second, but she recovered it with a skill that was almost impressive. "Of course. We would not wish to interfere with your studies. We shall hope to see you at the assembly next week, then. I am sure the ladies will be most eager to hear your thoughts on the state of the nation."

Edward, who had no thoughts on the state of the nation beyond the fact that he was hungry, nodded quickly and ducked back into the carriage. Miss Hargrave bowed stiffly and closed the door.

As the carriage drove away, Mrs. Thorne turned to her daughters. Her face was a mask of serene confidence, but her eyes were sharp with calculation. "He is a young man of great promise," she said. "His shyness is merely a sign of his modesty. Many a great man has been reserved in his youth."

Eliza looked at the dust settling on the road. "He is seventeen, Mother."

"So much the better," Mrs. Thorne said. "There is time to shape him. And a governess! It shows that he is well-bred and accustomed to instruction. It is a very good sign."

Mary, who had been biting her lip to keep from laughing, finally spoke. "He was reading a book on botany, Mother. And he smelled of lavender water."

Mrs. Thorne stopped walking. She turned to Mary, her eyes cold. "You are being unkind, Mary. And uncharitable. We must not judge a man by his hobbies or his scent. We must look to his character, his prospects, and his title. And those, I assure you, are excellent."

The three women walked back to the inn in silence. The sea was crashing against the cliffs, a loud, rhythmic sound that seemed to mock their quiet desperation. Eliza felt a profound sense of fatigue, a weariness that went beyond the physical. She looked at her mother, who was already planning how to arrange a seating position at the assembly that would allow her to converse with the young lord, and she wondered if there was any escape from this life of performance.

There was, perhaps, in the fact that Lord Ashcombe had a governess. It meant that he was not, in any true sense, free. He was a boy, still under the control of another, and thus, in a way, they were all under the same yoke. Mrs. Thorne was under the yoke of her ambition; Eliza and Mary were under the yoke of their duty; and Edward was under the yoke of his education. They were all trapped in their respective roles, performing a part in a play that none of them had chosen.

As they entered the inn, Mrs. Thorne paused at the door and looked back toward the road. "We shall have him," she said, with a certainty that was more a command to herself than a prediction of the future. "He is a good boy. And good boys are easily managed."

Eliza followed her mother into the dining room, where the smell of salt and roasted meat filled the air. She sat down at the table, her face calm, her hands folded in her lap. She did not smile. She did not frown. She simply sat, waiting for the next act to begin, and for the sea to continue its endless, indifferent crashing against the rocks.
