It is a common error in the arithmetic of human happiness to suppose that a woman’s intellectual endowments, when they exceed the ordinary measure allotted to her station, are a simple addition to her domestic felicity. Rather, they are a disturbing element, like an excess of oxygen in a closed chamber, making the vital flame burn too fiercely for the encompassing vessels. Such was the case with Mrs. Letitia Fawley, the wife of the Reverend Augustus Fawley, curate of St. Cyprian’s in the provincial town of Larchford. Letitia was the daughter of a deceased classicist, a man who had imbued her with a knowledge of Greek and a refined contempt for the half-educated, but who had left her little else than a quantity of battered texts and a habit of exacting mental analysis. Augustus, a man of mild disposition and unimpeachable morals, but of no particular brilliance, had married her out of a profound admiration for her mind—an admiration which, in the course of three years of married life, had not unfrequently bordered on bewilderment.

Letitia found herself situated in Larchford as a foreign body in a living organism: present, active, yet resented by the surrounding tissue. The parish ladies, headed by Mrs. Vavasor, the ironmonger’s widow, and Miss Polehampton, the magistrate’s sister, maintained a tone of elaborate condescension toward the curate’s wife. They spoke slowly to her, as one might speak to an intelligent child, and commended her little household economies with a patronizing warmth that Letitia felt as a series of small, sharp incisions upon her pride. They could not forgive her for using words of three syllables with an accuracy that implied she knew their precise derivation, nor for the fact that her bonnets, though of cheaper straw than theirs, were shaped with an unmistakable elegance that suggested she possessed a standard of beauty independent of the Larchford modiste.

If the ladies of the parish were condescending, the poor were suspicious. They regarded Mrs. Fawley’s visits with a sullen reserve, interpreting her direct gaze and her habit of inquiring into the exact quantities of their diet as a species of inquisitorial harshness. When she spoke to them of economy and self-reliance, they heard only a desire to withhold the customary dole. Old Goody Pringle, who inhabited a damp cottage in Dapple Lane, had expressed the general sentiment with more force than grammar: "Her’s a fine lady, wi’ her hard words and her proud eye. I’d rather see the passon hisself, for he’ve got a soft heart, though he be but a weak head, and her’s got a hard heart, though she be a fine scholar." This judgment, Letitia was aware, was not entirely without foundation. She could not, like Augustus, look upon a filthy child or a rheumatic old man and feel only a tender pity; she saw also the preventable causes of the misery, the broken window, the wasted wage, the gin-shop, and her pity was transformed into a burning impatience with human stupidity.

It was this impatience, allied to a genuine desire for Augustus’s advancement, that had determined her to secure for him the vacant living of Tidmarsh, a benefice in the gift of Lord Blandford, which had recently fallen vacant upon the death of the incumbent. The income was four hundred pounds a year, with a commodious rectory and a village of manageable size. Letitia had calculated, with the precision of a general planning a campaign, that this competence would place them beyond the humiliating dependencies of curacy, and would furnish her with a sphere in which her abilities might find a more fitting exercise. She had written to Lord Blandford’s agent, a Mr. Thesiger, setting forth Augustus’s claims with a eloquence that was perhaps too forcible, and had even taken upon herself to call upon Lady Blandford during one of her visits to the county town, where she had conversed with a dignity and intellectual breadth that she fondly imagined must impress a woman of quality.

The result of these machinations was now known. The living of Tidmarsh had been bestowed upon the Reverend Nathaniel Groveler, a man of ponderous intellect and still more ponderous pulpit delivery, whose sole claim to ecclesiastical preferment lay in the fact that his maternal uncle had once been of service to Lord Blandford at Newmarket. Letitia sat in the small parlour of the curate’s house, the letter from Mr. Thesiger lying open upon the table before her, and felt within her breast the sickening heaviness of a hope extinguished. Augustus stood by the window, looking out upon the rain-slicked cobbles of the lane, his face wearing an expression of patient disappointment. He had said, with a gentleness that cut her more sharply than any reproach, that he was sorry she had taken so much trouble, and that perhaps it was all for the best, since Tidmarsh was a damp situation and he had heard the parsonage was subject to flooding.

"I am not thinking of the parsonage," Letitia said, her voice low and strained. "I am thinking of the manner in which this has been done. It is not that Mr. Groveler is unworthy—he is an honest man, I suppose, though a dull one. It is that the whole system of patronage is a corrupt and irrational thing, which places the highest offices of the Church at the disposal of men who regard a living as a debt of honour to be discharged among their sporting acquaintances."

"My dear," said Augustus, turning from the window with a faint, deprecating smile, "you must not say that. Lord Blandford has, no doubt, his obligations. And Mr. Groveler is, as you say, an honest man."

"An honest man!" Letitia repeated, with a scornful intonation that she instantly regretted, for she saw Augustus wince. She was silent, and in that silence a new and painful thought took shape within her mind. She had been active, unwearied, ingenious, in pushing Augustus’s claims; but had her activity, perhaps, been the very thing that had injured him? She recalled her visit to Lady Blandford, the composed, slightly amused expression on that lady’s face as Letitia had spoken of Augustus’s scholarly habits and his acquaintance with the German theologians. She recalled, too, the tone of her letter to Mr. Thesiger—a tone which, upon reflection, she perceived to have been more suited to a woman advocating her own cause than to a wife gently solicitous for her husband’s prospects. The conviction stole upon her, with the slow, chill certainty of a winter dawn, that she had made Augustus appear not as a man deserving advancement, but as a man governed by an ambitious wife; and that in the eyes of Lord Blandford and his agent, this was an offence more unpardonable than dullness or even dishonesty.

She looked at Augustus, who had taken down a volume of Jeremy Taylor and was turning the pages with an absent, melancholy attention, and she felt a sudden, sharp revulsion against her own self-assertion. He was a good man, she thought, a better man than she, in his patient, uncomplaining acceptance of whatever befell him; and she had done him a wrong, not by desiring his advancement, but by desiring it too vehemently, too visibly, with too much of her own pride and her own intellectual arrogance mingled in the motive. The ambition which had seemed to her a noble resolve to lift him to his proper place, now appeared as a subtle selfishness, a desire to vindicate her own superiority by proving that the man she had chosen was not, after all, a mere obscure curate. She had been fighting, not for Augustus, but against the humiliation of being Augustus’s wife in Larchford, and the distinction was one that she could not now ignore.

There are moments in the life of a thoughtful mind when the scales fall away and the whole structure of self-justification stands revealed in its nakedness. Letitia Fawley had such a moment now, standing by the table with the letter beneath her hand and her husband’s patient figure by the window. She did not confess to him, for confessions are often a luxury which the confessor cannot afford to bestow and the recipient is embarrassed to receive. But she registered the truth within her own consciousness, with that severe intellectual honesty which was, perhaps, the one virtue she possessed in excess. She had harmed the man she loved, and the harm was irreparable, for the living was gone, and no amount of self-knowledge could restore it. The only reparation lay in the future, in a long, slow discipline of self-abnegation, in learning to value Augustus not for what she could make of him, but for what he was.
