The letter from the Dean of St. Cuthbert’s arrived on a Tuesday, carried by a trembling verger who refused to step past my front gate. The old man, Mr. Phipps, muttered something about the devil’s breath in the pipes before thrusting the sealed envelope into my hand and fleeing down the lane. I read the Dean’s elegant script at my desk, learning that the cathedral at Thornbury required a discreet inquiry into two matters that the Dean feared were deeply connected. The first was a complaint from the organist, Mr. Edgar Vance, who swore the great organ played notes of its own accord in the dead of night. The second was a troubling discovery made by the sacristan: a valuable silver-gilt altar plate, a 17th-century chalice donated by a wealthy merchant family, had been replaced by a cunning copy.

I took the afternoon train to Thornbury, a quiet cathedral city nestled in a western valley where the damp seemed to seep into the very stone. The Dean, Dr. Hollis, met me in his parlour with the nervous energy of a man balancing faith with practicality. He was a tall, ascetic figure, his spectacles perpetually fogged by the cold interior of the cathedral buildings.

"I must impress upon you, Mr. Ainsworth, the need for absolute discretion," Dr. Hollis said, pouring a cup of tea that tasted faintly of rust. "The choir has already seized upon Vance’s claims as a ghost story. They whisper about the spirit of old Canon Devereux, who they say was walled up in the triforium for simony. If word of the stolen chalice reaches the press, the Diocese will be humiliated. We are a poor foundation, sir. That chalice was our most valuable asset."

I asked to see the copy first. The Dean led me through a labyrinthine corridor into the sacristy, where the sacristan, a wizened fellow named Toomey, unlocked a heavy oak cabinet. The false chalice sat on a velvet cloth. I turned it over in my hands, feeling the weight. It was lighter than it should have been, the silver worn thin in places where true age would have left a heavier patina. The gilt was bright, almost garish, clearly recent work. Beneath the base, the original hallmarks had been reproduced with skill, but the stamp was slightly off, the letter forms too modern in their spacing.

"A clever forgery," I said. "Made within the last year, I should think. Who has access to this cabinet?"

"Only Toomey, myself, and the Bishop when he visits," the Dean replied. "But the vestry is unlocked during hours when the choir practices, and the nave is open to the public during the day."

I then asked to meet the organist. We found Vance in the organ loft, a narrow gallery high above the choir stalls, where the pipes of the great instrument rose like a metal forest. Vance was a slight man with ink-stained fingers and a pallor that suggested he spent very little time out of doors. He sat on the organ bench, wringing his hands.

"It began in October," Vance said, his voice thin and reedy. "I lock the console each evening, Mr. Ainsworth. I cover the manuals and draw the stops. But three times now, I have come down in the morning to find the cover removed and the organ sounding a low chord. Not a melody, mind you. A chord. As if someone were leaning on the keys with their whole weight. The bellows are pumped by a hydraulic engine now, so there is no boy to blame. The engine should be off overnight, yet when I test it in the morning, the wind chest is full."

I examined the console. The keys showed no marks of forced entry, but I noticed that the lock on the cover was a simple ward lock, easily picked by anyone with a length of wire. I asked Vance if he had enemies among the choir.

"They think me mad," he said bitterly. "Particularly Mr. Lacey, the head chorister. He has wanted my position for years, though he hasn’t the skill for it. He leads them in their ghost talk. They say the chord I hear is the note Canon Devereux heard before he was entombed."

I dismissed Vance and descended into the nave. The choir was rehearsing for evensong, their voices rising in a Palestrina motet that seemed to make the very air vibrate. I studied the architecture, tracing the line of the triforium gallery that ran around the upper level of the church. Behind the organ loft, the gallery connected to a narrow passage that led to the cathedral library and, beyond that, to the canon’s vestry. It would be possible, I thought, for someone to enter the organ loft from behind, through the pipe chambers, without using the main stair.

That night, I returned to the cathedral after dark. Dr. Hollis had left the west door unlocked for me, and I let myself in, my lantern casting long shadows across the flagstones. The nave was utterly silent, the kind of silence that exists only in large stone buildings, a silence that seems to press against the ears.

I climbed the winding stair to the triforium and made my way along the gallery. The passage behind the organ loft was cramped, filled with dust and the smell of old leather. I squeezed between the wooden casing and the wall until I reached the pipe chambers. Here, the great metal pipes disappeared into the darkness above, and I could see the mechanical action, the thin wooden trackers that connected the keys to the valves.

I settled into a recess behind the swell box and waited.

It was well past midnight when I heard footsteps. Not the bold tread of someone walking in the open, but the careful, sliding step of a man moving in darkness. I doused my lantern and held my breath. A figure emerged from the direction of the library passage, moving with familiarity through the cramped space. I could hear him fumbling with something metallic.

Then came a sound that made my heart seize, a low, groaning note from the organ, followed by another, forming a chord that resonated through the whole instrument. It was not a ghost. The figure had leaned against the trackers, pressing several of them inward, and the hydraulic engine, which I now realized had been left running, supplied the wind.

But the figure was not playing music. He was using the chord as a mask. While the organ sounded, he could move through the loft and down into the choir stalls without fear of being heard. I watched as he descended from the loft by a narrow ladder I had not previously noticed, hidden behind the pedal board. He crossed to the south aisle and disappeared into the sacristy.

I followed, keeping to the shadows along the choir stalls. The organ chord still hummed, a discordant drone that set my teeth on edge. When I reached the sacristy door, I found it ajar. Inside, the figure had lit a small candle and was prying at the back panel of the oak cabinet with a thin chisel. Beside him on the floor lay a cloth bag.

"Mr. Lacey," I said.

The head chorister spun around, his face a mask of terror. In the candlelight I could see he was holding not the false chalice but a small silver ciborium, another piece of plate that I suspected was also destined for replacement.

"It is not what you think," Lacey whispered, his voice cracking.

"I think," I said, stepping into the room, "that you have been entering the organ loft by night, sounding a chord to cover your movements, and removing the cathedral’s silver piece by piece, replacing each with copies made by a silversmith in Bristol or Bath. I think you have been doing this for months, perhaps longer, and that Mr. Vance heard the result of your method rather than any supernatural occurrence."

Lacey sank into a chair. The candle guttered. The organ chord faded as the wind chest emptied, leaving a silence deeper than before.

"I have debts," he said. "Gaming debts. I owe a man in Bristol a sum I cannot repay. He suggested the plate. He knew a smith who could make copies. The organ was my idea. I knew the sound would carry through the stone and that the choir would make a ghost story of it. No one questions a ghost, Mr. Ainsworth. No one investigates what they fear."

I took the ciborium from his hands and examined it. It was genuine, heavy with age. The cloth bag contained two more small items, a paten and a spoon, both original.

"How much have you taken?" I asked.

"Only the chalice, so far. The others I was to collect this week. The smith is waiting."

I told Lacey to remain where he was. I went to fetch the Dean, finding him in his parlour, still awake, reading by the fire. When I explained what I had discovered, he did not speak for a long moment. He removed his spectacles and polished them on his sleeve.

"The choir will be disappointed to lose their ghost," he said finally. "But I suppose the truth must serve. I will send for the police in the morning. Lacey will be removed quietly. We will say he has resigned for reasons of health."

"And the organ?" I asked.

"Mr. Vance will be relieved to learn it is not haunted. Though I suspect he may be somewhat disappointed as well."

I returned to the cathedral and sat in the nave until dawn, watching the light creep through the clerestory windows and fill the ancient space with colour. The organ stood silent, its pipes catching the first gold of the morning sun, and I thought how strange it was that a man could use so beautiful an instrument to mask so ugly a crime, and how eagerly others would believe in ghosts rather than face the ordinary wickedness of their fellow men.
