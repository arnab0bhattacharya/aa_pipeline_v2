The harbour of Lisbon lay before me like a painted scene of some elder master—a tranquil composition of pale terraces and white domes descending to a sea of glass, wherein the evening sun dissolved in ruddy gold. So calm, so luminously serene it appeared, that I could scarcely credit the intelligence which had drawn me hither across the waste of waters; for I had sailed from the ruins of my own country, bearing in my breast a hope that was near to madness, and the fair aspect of this city seemed rather to mock my feverish impatience than to promise its fulfilment.

I had left England a shadow. The great sickness which had swept the nations had not spared her; the cities of my youth stood silent, and the green fields were returning to the wild. In the hospital of my native town I had watched the plague take all that was dear to me—my wife, my little son, my dearest friend—and I had come forth from that long vigil with a heart burnt hollow, and with one resolve alone remaining: to seek the remedy of which rumour spoke, the sovereign specific which the physicians of Lisbon were said to have discovered, and which, if it truly existed, might yet redeem the remnant of mankind. I was a physician; I had spent my life in the study of the healing art; and I confess that as my ship drew near the Tagus, and I beheld the unbroken peace of that city, a strange and bitter envy mingled with my hope. Here was life, and order, and the kindly commerce of men, while my own land was a graveyard.

I went on shore as soon as the customs would permit, and found the streets clean and quiet, and the people moving with a composed cheerfulness that smote upon my spirit like a reproach. There were no signs of the pestilence; no mourners, no shuttered houses, no fires of fumigation. I addressed myself to a physician of repute, one Senhor Almeida, to whom I had letters, and was received with a courtesy so grave and so measured that it chilled while it honoured me. I told him my errand with all the ardour of a man who has no other hope; I spoke of my country, of the desolation I had witnessed, of the remedy I sought; and I besought him, by every tie of our common humanity, to aid me.

He heard me to the end without interruption, and then, with a countenance that betrayed no emotion, he said: “You have been misinformed, sir. We possess no remedy. The health of this city is the gift of Providence, and of a wise administration. We have been fortunate; we have guarded our shores; we have been sparing of intercourse with infected lands. But of a specific, a cure, a sovereign medicine—of such a thing I know nothing.”

I was struck dumb. I had crossed the ocean, I had spent my last substance, I had nerved myself against every peril, and I was answered with a smooth denial. I pressed him; I conjured him; I described to him the agonies I had seen, the faces of my dead, the silence of my deserted streets. He listened with the same grave composure, and repeated his denial in other words, and at length, with a politeness that was like a wall of ice, he dismissed me.

Yet I could not believe him. There was that in the air of the city which spoke of a secret carefully kept; in the very order of the streets, the very health upon the cheeks of the people, I read the signs of a knowledge that was withheld. I resolved to remain, and to observe. I lodged in a modest house near the great square, kept by a widow, one Dona Isabel, whose tongue was loosened by kindness and by the small coin I could afford; and from her, and from the idle talk of the market, I gathered fragments that confirmed my suspicion. There had been, it was said, in the time of the great sickness, a physician of the old hospital of São Marcos, a man of singular genius, who had devoted himself to the study of the contagion, and who had died—some said of the plague itself, some said by the hand of the authorities, for that his discovery was judged too dangerous to be made known. His name was Fonseca. His papers, it was whispered, were sealed up in the archive of the College of Physicians, and no man might see them.

The thought of those papers took possession of me. I walked by night beneath the walls of the College, a low building of yellow stone in a quiet street, and I watched the dim light in the window of the librarian, and I reasoned with myself in the passionate manner of a man whom grief has made reckless. Was it not my right, I demanded, to save my fellow-creatures? Was not the withholding of such a remedy a crime against humanity, more dreadful than any theft or trespass I could commit? I had no country left, no family, no future; I had only this one hope, and I was resolved to hazard all upon it.

I made the acquaintance of a young man, a student of medicine, one Vicente, whose poverty made him accessible, and whose enthusiasm I could touch with the language of a common cause. To him I spoke of the millions who had perished, of the duty we owed to the dead and to the living; and I saw his eyes kindle, and his scruples melt before my ardour. He procured for me, at length, a key to the lesser door of the archive; and upon a night of wind and rain, when the streets were empty and the watch was housed, I entered that solemn chamber, and by the light of a dark lantern I searched the shelves.

I found the packet at last, bound in faded tape, inscribed with the name of Fonseca. My hands trembled as I broke the seal. I read by the flickering light, with the rain beating upon the casement, and my heart beat so loud that I could scarce hear my own thoughts. And as I read, a cold horror crept over me, and the hope that had sustained me through so many months fell from me like a garment. For the papers of Fonseca were not the record of a cure. They were the confession of a man who had sought a remedy and had found only a terrible expedient: the inoculation of the healthy with a mitigated form of the contagion, a practice which, in his own words, “doth preserve the body from the greater malady, but at a price which no honest physician may conceal.” The price was a lingering corruption of the blood, a slow and secret poisoning, which saved the life and sapped the vigour, and which, transmitted to the children of the inoculated, had already, he wrote, “filled our city with a race of men who live, and yet are dead.”

I sat in that dark archive until the dawn, and I understood at last the silence of Senhor Almeida, and the health upon the cheeks of the people, and the terrible order of the streets. This was the secret of Lisbon: not a remedy, but a compact with death. And I, who had come to save mankind, had come only to learn that the salvation offered was a lie, and that the price of it was the ruin of the race.

I replaced the papers, and I went out into the grey morning, and I walked by the river, and I wept—not for myself, but for the world, and for the vanity of all our hopes. The sun rose upon the calm water, and the city awoke to its orderly and cheerful life; and I turned my face once more to the sea, and to the desolate land from which I came, bearing with me a knowledge that was heavier than the plague.
