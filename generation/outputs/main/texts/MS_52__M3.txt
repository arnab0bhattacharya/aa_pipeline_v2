It was in the sweet, melancholy summer of my eighteenth year that the pestilence of grief took its most absolute hold upon my spirit, transforming the verdant world around me into a theatre of phantom echoes and unavailing regrets. We were dwelling then at Thornhaven, a village nestled in a deep, sequestered valley of Devonshire, where the high moors sweep downward to meet the murmuring sea, and where the ancient, moss-grown stones of the ruined abbey stand as melancholy witnesses to the fleeting nature of all earthly attachments. It was there, amidst those sublime and awaking landscapes, that my Clara had drawn her last, pain-fraught breath; and it was there, in the desperate, unreasoning anguish of my soul, that I conceived the fatal, yet deeply soothing, design of addressing her as though the grave had not yet claimed her.

Clara had been the sun of my existence, the gentle, exalted companion whose understanding was so finely attuned to my own that we seemed but two halves of a single, thinking spirit. Her disposition was all grace and elevated sensibility; she felt the beauties of nature with an intensity that bordered upon pain, and when the consumption had planted its first, fatal rose upon her cheek, she bore the gradual decay of her earthly tenement with a heroism that wrung my heart even more deeply than could the contemplation of her inevitable loss. When she was taken from me, the light of the universe was extinguished, and I was left to wander amidst the ruins of my shattered hopes, a solitary and despairing survivor in a world that had suddenly become a vast and unpeopled wilderness.

For a time, I was wholly given over to the voiceless agony of my bereavement, wandering alone upon the craggy heights of the moor, or sitting for hours beside the grey and restless waves, seeking in the tumult of the elements a corresponding tumult to my own internal suffering. But the human heart, even when most crushed and desolate, cannot forever endure the absolute silence of its own despair; it must find an outlet, a voice, a language for its overflowing passion. And thus it was that I began, in the secrecy of my solitary chamber, to write letters to my departed friend.

I would sit at my casement, looking out upon the shadowed glades of Thornhaven, and, taking up my pen, I would address her in the very language of our former intercourse. I would tell her of the rising of the sun, of the mists that crept like spectres along the valley, of the nightingale that sang in the thicket, and of the deep, unutterable yearnings that possessed my soul. I wrote as though she were but journeyed to a distant country, from which, though I might not soon behold her, she could yet look down upon me, and feel the outpourings of my undying affection. I would end my letters with the same tender appellations I had used in her life, sealing them with wax, and, in the solemn, dreamlike abstraction of my mind, I would address them to her as though she still dwelt in the old cottage beneath the elm-trees. It was a gentle, beautiful madness, if madness it must be called; for in that communion with the unseen, I found a solace so profound, so exquisitely touching, that I seemed to be lifted above the vulgar realities of the world, and brought into nearer communion with the spiritual and the divine.

But this secret and cherished illusion was not long to remain undiscovered. My mother, a woman of strong affections but of a practical and unromantic turn of mind, had long viewed my solitary habits and my deep, unyielding grief with a mixture of pity and apprehension. She could not understand the intense, all-absorbing nature of my attachment to Clara, nor the profound and overwhelming despair that had followed upon her loss. She sought, by every means in her power, to rouse me from my melancholy, to engage me in the ordinary occupations of life, and to wean my thoughts from the contemplation of the dead; but her efforts were unavailing, and my abstracted manner only served to increase her fears.

It was upon a warm evening in the midst of July, when the air was filled with the scent of the hayfields and the distant murmur of the sea, that the fatal discovery was made. I had left my chamber for a brief stroll in the garden, and, in my absence, my mother entered the room to lay out some fresh attire. Upon my desk, carelessly left open, lay a letter, unfinished, the ink still glistening upon the page. Her eye fell upon it, and, moved by a curiosity that was perhaps as much the prompting of anxiety as of any lesser motive, she took it up and read.

I know not what were her precise emotions as she perused those pages; but when I returned, I found her sitting by my desk, the letter crushed in her trembling hand, her face pale, and her eyes filled with a terror that struck a chill to my very soul. She gazed upon me for a moment in silence, as if she beheld a stranger, a being alien to her understanding, and then, with a voice broken by emotion, she exclaimed—"Oh, my child, my unhappy child! What is this I have read? Do you not know that she is dead—that Clara is dead, and that these letters are but the offspring of a distempered brain? Oh, heaven! have I then lived to see my daughter's reason overthrown, and her mind given over to the delusions of a morbid grief?"

I stood motionless, overwhelmed by the suddenness and the violence of her accusation. The beautiful, consoling illusion that I had reared up in the solitude of my own heart was thus rudely shattered, and I felt as one who, walking in a pleasant dream, is suddenly awakened to find himself upon the edge of a precipice. I attempted to speak, to explain, to defend the purity and the innocence of my design; but my words were incoherent, and my voice failed me. I saw, by the expression of my mother's countenance, that she looked upon me as one bereft of reason, and the consciousness of her unbelief, the sense that she could not enter into the sanctuary of my grief, filled me with a profound and unutterable despair.

"Why do you seek to tear her from me?" I cried, at length, in a transport of passionate indignation. "She is not dead to me! She lives in every thought of my heart, in every feeling of my soul! When I write to her, I feel her presence; I hear her voice in the wind; I see her smile in the opening flowers! Oh, do not, do not, by your unkind and worldly doubts, sever the thin and trembling thread that binds me to her memory, for if that be broken, I am indeed undone, and the darkness of the grave will close upon me forever!"

My mother wept, and strove to soothe me; but her tears and her soothing words were mingled with renewed entreaties that I would abandon this fatal practice, that I would strive to overcome my grief, and return to the duties and the affections of life. But I was deaf to her appeals; I felt that she had invaded the most sacred recesses of my being, and that the bond of sympathy between us was, for the time, broken. I turned from her, and, seating myself by the casement, looked out upon the evening landscape, where the shadows were already beginning to gather, and the grey turrets of the ruined abbey were fading into the obscurity of the twilight. The nightingale had ceased its song; the wind had sunk to rest; and a deep, unbroken silence, fraught with melancholy and awe, brooded over the face of nature. It seemed to me as though the spirit of my departed friend had fled from that scene, scared by the intrusion of a worldly and unfeeling grief; and I was left alone, in the darkness of my own thoughts, to mourn, not only for the loss of her whom I had loved, but for the shattering of that gentle and consoling illusion which had been, to my desolate heart, the last refuge of its undying affection.
