The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which was, by the established rhythm of Ashworth Manor, the dullest day of the week. It was brought in with the tea by Mrs. Gable, the housekeeper, her face a mask of disapproval that did little to soften the news. "A letter for Miss Vance, from abroad," she announced, placing the single, crisp sheet upon the silver salver as if it were a contaminant. "From a Mr. Alistair Vance. Of Calcutta."

The name fell into the quiet afternoon like a stone into a placid pond. Cecily, the eldest, looked up from her embroidery, her needle hovering mid-stitch. Beatrice, curled in the window seat with a volume of Mr. Radcliffe, lowered it slowly. Only Elspeth, the youngest, seemed untroubled, continuing to stir a lump of sugar into her tea until it dissolved into a sweet, milky cloud.

"Alistair," Cecily repeated, the name feeling alien on her tongue. It had been years—twenty at least—since she had last heard it spoken aloud. He was a ghost, a family story told in fragments and hushed tones. The brother who had quarrelled with their father, the specifics of the dispute lost to time like a key dropped in a river. All that remained was the result: a stormy departure, a slammed door, and a silence that had endured until this very moment.

"Read it, Cecily," Beatrice urged, her eyes wide with a romantic fervour. "Perhaps he is an old man, full of regret, seeking to make amends before he dies. Or perhaps he has been shipwrecked and is writing from the shore of some strange island, his only hope our remembrance."

Elspeth giggled. "More likely he is a pirate, Bea. And he needs a respectable address to hide his treasure. We shall be rich, and he shall be hanged."

Cecily ignored them both. She took the letter, her movements precise and deliberate. The paper was thick and expensive, the ink a sharp, black script. She unfolded it and read aloud, her voice steady and devoid of inflection, as if delivering a sermon.

"My dear nieces," it began, and the informal address was its own small shock. "I trust this letter finds you and the memory of my dear brother, God rest his soul, in good health. It has been too long since I have had the pleasure of your acquaintance, a fact I intend to rectify with all due haste. My affairs in the East have concluded most favourably, and I find myself with both the means and the inclination to return to the home of my youth. I shall arrive at Ashworth in a fortnight, and look forward to renewing our acquaintance. I remain your affectionate uncle, Alistair Vance."

The final word hung in the air. *Affectionate*. Cecily placed the letter on the table, the silence in the room now thick with unspoken possibilities.

"Well," Beatrice breathed, a blush rising to her cheeks. "It is just as I thought. A prodigal uncle, returning to the fold. He will wish to see the orchard where he played as a boy, to walk the grounds and speak of his dear, lost brother. He will want nothing but peace and family."

Cecily’s lips thinned. "He says he has concluded his affairs *most favourably*. That is not the language of a man seeking peace, Beatrice. It is the language of a man of business. Father left this place in… delicate circumstances. The roof leaks in the west wing, the tenants are a season behind on their rents. This uncle, who made his fortune in Calcutta, will see Ashworth not as a home, but as an investment. He will want to audit the ledgers, to demand an accounting. He will see three unmarried women as a liability to be managed."

The practicality of her words chilled the air. Beatrice looked crestfallen, her romantic vision crumbling into dust.

"Oh, don't be so grim, Cecily," Elspeth said, hopping up from her chair. "You are both forgetting the most important thing. He is *rich*. Calcutta rich! He will not care about ledgers or orchards. He will want to dazzle us. He will have brought silks and jewels and spices. He will want to see our faces when he presents us with gifts. He will want to be adored."

The three theories hung in the space between them, each a plausible ghost of the man they did not know. The Penitent, The Auditor, The Benefactor.

For the next fortnight, the house was thrown into a state of nervous agitation. Cecily spent hours with the estate books, her brow furrowed in concentration, as if by sheer force of will she could make the numbers appear more robust. Beatrice took to wandering the gardens, composing in her mind the poetic speeches she would deliver to her uncle upon his arrival, speaking of memory and the enduring bonds of blood. Elspeth, meanwhile, began a frantic inventory of her wardrobe, lamenting its provincial inadequacy and fantasising about the transformative power of a sari or a string of pearls.

The quarrel, the source of it all, became the subject of much debate. Beatrice was convinced it was a matter of the heart—perhaps Alistair had loved a local girl their father had disapproved of. Cecily insisted it must have been a question of inheritance, some unfair division of their grandfather's property. Elspeth, with a wicked glint in her eye, suggested their father had caught his brother cheating at cards. Mrs. Gable, when pressed, would only say, "It was a proud man's quarrel, and the price of pride is a lonely house." She offered no further elaboration.

The day of his arrival was overcast, a low grey sky pressing down upon the green hills of Somerset. The carriage, a dark, imposing vehicle that seemed far too grand for their quiet lane, rolled up the gravel drive at precisely four o'clock. The sisters stood in the drawing-room window, a trio of apprehension.

He was not what any of them had expected. He was neither the frail old man of Beatrice's imagining, nor the coarse merchant of Cecily's fears. Alistair Vance was a man of perhaps fifty, tall and broad-shouldered, with a shock of iron-grey hair and a face tanned by a foreign sun. His eyes, a startlingly clear blue, took in the faded grandeur of the hall, the worn carpet, the chipped plasterwork of the ceiling, with a single, sweeping glance. He did not look like a penitent, an auditor, or a benefactor. He looked like a conqueror surveying new territory.

He greeted them with a firm handshake and a brief, assessing smile. He complimented Beatrice’s looks, asked Cecily a pointed question about the harvest, and presented Elspeth with a small, carved box from his coat pocket. Inside, nestled on a bed of velvet, was not a jewel, but a tiny, perfect model of a ship, its sails carved from ivory. "A reminder that all journeys must have an end," he said, his voice a low rumble.

That evening, as they sat down to a dinner that Mrs. Gable had outdone herself on, the real purpose of his visit began to unfold. It was not a single thing, but all three, and none of them. He spoke of Calcutta with a strange mix of fondness and weariness, of fortunes made and lost, of a life lived at a fever pitch. He did not speak of their father, not directly.

Instead, as the port was poured, he leaned back in his chair and looked at the three of them. "This house," he said, his voice quiet but carrying immense weight. "It has good bones. But it is tired. It has been left to fend for itself for too long." He looked at Cecily. "You have the head for it, I see. But you are managing a decline, not building a future." He turned to Beatrice. "And you have the heart for it. But a heart cannot mend a leaking roof." Finally, his gaze settled on Elspeth. "And you… you see only what it lacks."

He took a sip of his wine. "My quarrel with your father was over this place. I wanted to modernise it, to turn the west pasture into a tenant farm, to build a new stable block. He called it desecration. He called it greed. I called it survival. He chose to preserve the past. I chose to build a future. And in the end," he said, a flicker of something—regret? triumph?—in his eyes, "I built mine elsewhere."

He set his glass down. The theories of the three sisters lay in ruins around them. He was not the Penitent, for he felt no guilt. He was not the Auditor, for he had already seen the books in his mind's eye long ago. He was not the Benefactor, for his gifts were not given from simple generosity.

He was the victor. He had come home to see if his brother's stubbornness had been justified. He had come to claim the territory he had lost two decades before, not with anger, but with the quiet, devastating power of his success. He was here to offer them a future, but it would be on his terms. The consternation in the drawing-room had been a simple fear of the unknown. The dread that settled over the dinner table was the far more complex fear of being known, and found wanting.
