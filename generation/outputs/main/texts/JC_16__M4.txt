The telegram had come up from the coast in the grey of the morning, carried by a half-breed runner whose breath still laboured from the ascent, and it lay now upon the desk in the consular office—a flimsy, soiled slip of paper that seemed to weigh upon the whole room with the gravity of an ultimatum. His Excellency’s ship, the *Vigilant*, would arrive within the week. She was coming, the message said, to protect British interests. The words were perfectly legible, inscribed in a clerk’s careful hand, yet Mr. Herbert Ellery found himself reading them over and over, as though repetition might at last extract from them some meaning that had hitherto eluded him. The morning light, filtering through the slatted blinds, fell in dusty bars across the floor and touched the edges of the papers upon his desk—papers that represented, he was suddenly and uncomfortably aware, the accumulated labour of twenty years.

He rose and went to the window. Below, the town of Puerto Marea lay in the hollow of the hills like something spilled and left to dry—a confusion of red-tiled roofs and whitewashed walls, of narrow streets that wound without purpose toward the harbour, where the water lay flat and discoloured, a sheet of hammered brass under the mounting sun. A few fishing boats, their sails furled, rocked at anchor. Farther out, a steamer of some foreign line—German, he believed—traced a smudge of smoke across the horizon. Nothing, in all that prospect, suggested the imminence of crisis. Nothing suggested that the peace of this forgotten corner of the republic was about to be disturbed by the arrival of a warship flying the flag he had served, without enthusiasm, for the better part of a lifetime.

He turned back to the room. It was a high, bare apartment, its walls stained by years of damp, its furniture of that heavy, dark, joyless sort that seemed to have been designed for the express purpose of discouraging any prolonged occupation. Upon the shelves, the bound volumes of his annual reports stood in unbroken ranks—each one a monument to obscurity, each one consigned, he had long since concluded, to the dusty silence of some departmental archive in Whitehall where no living eye would ever peruse them. He had written of trade, of shipping, of the condition of the roads and the disposition of the local authorities. He had chronicled the small doings of a small place with a diligence that now struck him as almost pathetic. And in all those pages, he reflected, there was not a single sentence that could tell him what he was supposed to protect.

The British interests. He repeated the phrase to himself, silently, as though it were a proposition in some unfamiliar language. There were British interests, certainly—there was the railway, or what passed for it, a single line of rusted track that connected the port with the interior and had not, to his knowledge, returned a dividend in fifteen years. There was the mining concession, held by a company whose directors in London had long since ceased to correspond with him. There were the merchants—two of them, old Mr. Pemberton and his partner, who dealt in hides and coffee and whose chief concern, Ellery knew well, was to be left undisturbed in their slow accumulation of a modest competence. These were the interests. He could enumerate them. But could he say that he shared them? Could he say that he believed in them?

The question was not one he had ever been compelled to ask himself before. In the early years, the work had seemed sufficient in itself—the daily round of duties, the small ceremonies of office, the satisfaction of being, however obscurely, the representative of something larger than the town, larger than the republic, larger than himself. But the years had passed, and the larger thing had receded, had grown dim and doubtful, until at last he could no longer be certain whether it existed at all, or whether it was merely a name, a word, a convenient fiction employed to justify the presence of men like himself in places like Puerto Marea.

He sat down again at his desk and drew toward him a sheet of paper. It was his habit, when perplexed, to write—to set down his thoughts in the form of a despatch, as though the act of composition might clarify what reflection could not. But his pen hovered above the page, and no words came. He thought of the *Vigilant*—of her commander, some brisk and confident officer who would step ashore with his sword at his side and his mind made up, who would expect to be met with deference and information, who would ask, in that tone of assured authority which the service instils, what measures had been taken for the protection of British interests, and who would receive, in answer, only the stammering of an old man who had forgotten why he was there.

A knock at the door roused him. It was his clerk, a young Creole named Alvarez, who had served him for three years with a patience that Ellery sometimes suspected was merely the patience of indifference.

“The mail, señor,” Alvarez said, placing a bundle of letters upon the desk. “And there is a gentleman below—Mr. Pemberton. He wishes to see you.”

Ellery nodded. “Show him up.”

Pemberton entered with the slow, deliberate tread of a man who had spent too many years in hot climates to be hurried. He was tall, spare, with a face like old leather and eyes that retained, despite everything, a certain shrewdness. He declined the chair that Ellery offered and stood instead by the window, looking out at the harbour.

“You have heard the news,” he said. It was not a question.

“The telegram came this morning.”

“And what do you make of it?”

Ellery was silent for a moment. “I make of it,” he said at last, “that we are about to be honoured with a visit.”

Pemberton turned. “Honoured,” he repeated, with a faint grimace. “That is one word for it. I have been here thirty years, Ellery. I have seen the Spanish come and go, and the French, and the Germans. I have seen revolutions and counter-revolutions, and I have learned that when a warship arrives, it is never for the reason stated. The stated reason is always the protection of interests. The real reason is something else entirely—something that is never written in the despatches, something that perhaps the men who send the ships do not even understand themselves.”

“And what do you suppose the real reason is, in this case?”

Pemberton shrugged. “Perhaps there is none. Perhaps it is merely the habit of power—the need to show the flag, to remind the world that we are still here, still watching, still”—he paused, searching for the word—“still something. But I will tell you this, Ellery. Whatever the reason, when that ship arrives, we shall be expected to know what it means. We shall be expected to have opinions, to offer advice, to speak with authority. And if we cannot—”

He did not finish the sentence. He did not need to.

When he had gone, Ellery sat for a long time in the gathering heat of the afternoon, the telegram before him on the desk, the blank sheet of paper still waiting for words that would not come. Through the open window drifted the sounds of the town—a dog barking, a woman calling to her child, the distant clangour of a bell from the church of Santa Maria. The ordinary sounds of an ordinary place, undisturbed by history, unconscious of empire. He envied them their oblivion. He envied Pemberton his certainty, even if that certainty was only the certainty of disillusion. For himself, he had nothing—no conviction, no purpose, no clear sense of what he was meant to defend or why. He was a consul without a country, a servant of interests he could no longer name.

The sun sank lower. The shadows lengthened across the floor. And still he sat, motionless, as though waiting for some revelation that would never come—waiting, in the silence of that forgotten office, for the warship that would demand of him an answer he did not have.
