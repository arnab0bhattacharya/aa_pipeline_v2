Captain Silas Thorne’s house in Greenwich smelled of beeswax and the sea, a scent trapped in the very timbers of the place, though he had not set foot on a deck in a decade. He sat behind a desk of polished mahogany, a man carved from old rope and salt-water, his face a roadmap of squints against a thousand suns. Opposite him, Arthur Penhaligon felt the worn leather of his chair creak under his stillness. He was a man whose trade was in observation, in the quiet accumulation of detail, and he noted the tremor in the Captain’s hand as it rested beside a small, silver-framed photograph.

“My son,” Thorne said, his voice a low rumble like a distant swell. “His name is Thomas. He’s a good boy, Mr. Penhaligon. A bit wild, perhaps. Too much of his mother’s fire and not enough of my sense.” He tapped the frame. “We had words. The kind of words that fester. He left. Said he’d make his own way.” Thorne’s gaze drifted to the window, towards the distant, hazy smear of the river. “His last letter was six months ago. Postmarked from the Pool of London. Said he’d found work on the docks. That’s all.”

Penhaligon took the photograph. A young man with his father’s strong jaw and eyes that held a restless, defiant light. “The docks are a world of their own, Captain. A city within a city. Finding one man is like finding a specific wave in the ocean.”

“I know it,” Thorne grunted. “But I have to try. I will pay whatever it takes. Just find him. Tell him… tell him the tide has turned.”

The search began in the labyrinthine alleys of Wapping and Rotherhithe, where the air was thick with the stench of coal smoke, brine, and roasting hops. Penhaligon spent days talking to stevedores, tally-men, and pub-keepers, his inquiries met with shrugs, suspicion, and the occasional, grudging admission that the name Thomas Thorne sounded vaguely familiar. The docks were a place of transient faces and forgotten names. But a pattern began to emerge from the squalor. Men who had been hired on for steady work at a place called Gable’s Yard had a way of looking over their shoulders. They were paid in crisp banknotes, not the greasy coin of the other yards, but they were also tight-lipped and skittish.

Gable’s Yard was a grimy, secluded wharf tucked away behind a row of derelict warehouses. Its proprietor was a Mr. Alistair Gable, a man whose placid, almost clerical demeanour was at odds with the raw power of his forearms. He wore a clean collar and a waistcoat, and he sat in a small, glass-fronted office that looked out over the yard, a ledger open before him, though Penhaligon noticed it was filled with neat columns of figures, not names. Gable employed no clerk; every man was paid in cash by Gable himself at the end of each shift.

“I’m looking for a young man,” Penhaligon said, leaning against the doorframe of the office. “Name of Thomas Thorne. Might have worked for you.”

Gable did not look up from his ledger. “We use many hands. They come and go. I don’t keep track of their histories, only their labour.”

“He’s about twenty, dark hair. A bit of a temper.”

“Sounds like most of the lads on the docks,” Gable said, his pen scratching softly. “If he’s not here, he’s not here.” His eyes, pale and colourless as a winter sky, finally lifted. “I have a business to run, sir. If you have no official business, I must ask you to leave.”

The peculiar detail that snagged in Penhaligon’s mind was the shipping manifests he’d managed to glimpse. Gable’s Yard specialised in a single type of cargo: heavy, iron-strapped crates bound for Hamburg on the weekly steamer, *The Northwind*. The manifests declared them to contain machine parts, weighing a substantial three hundred pounds each. But a conversation with a disgruntled clerk at the Hamburg end of the shipping line, bought with the price of several whiskies, revealed a strange discrepancy. The crates arrived light. Always lighter. By fifty, sometimes eighty pounds. The Hamburg line had complained, but Gable had airtight contracts and a lawyer who silenced them. The cargo was insured for its declared weight, and the difference was written off as a curious, ongoing loss.

Penhaligon knew then that he was no longer just looking for a missing boy. He was looking at a ghost in the ledger, a phantom weight that vanished somewhere between Gable’s wharf and the open sea. He waited until a Tuesday night, when the *Northwind* was due. Under the cloak of a fog that rolled in from the estuary, swallowing the gaslights, he slipped past the single guard Gable kept, a man more interested in his flask than his duties.

The warehouse was cavernous and cold. The air was thick with the smell of damp wood and something else, something sharp and metallic. The crates stood in neat rows, their iron straps gleaming dully. Penhaligon found a crowbar and, with a grunt of effort, pried at the lid of one. The nails shrieked in protest. Inside, nestled in a bed of straw, were not gears or pistons, but dozens of lead ingots, each stamped with a foreign mint mark. They were dense, heavy, and utterly silent.

He heard the sound then. A soft, rhythmic scraping from the far end of the warehouse. It was followed by a low, guttural cough. Penhaligon froze, his heart a frantic drum against his ribs. He crept towards the sound, past stacks of empty crates, to a small, partitioned-off area. In the dim light of a single, sputtering lantern, he saw the source.

A man was hunched over a brazier, a crude metal file in his hand. He was gaunt, his clothes torn and stained. But the jawline, the dark hair matted with grime… it was the boy from the photograph. Thomas Thorne. He was not filing the lead; he was working on a small, intricate mechanism of brass and steel, a piece of clockwork that seemed to have no purpose. He looked up, his eyes wide with a feral terror, and scrambled back.

“Who’s there?” he rasped.

“Your father sent me,” Penhaligon said softly, holding up his hands. “Captain Thorne.”

The name seemed to break something in the young man. A sob escaped his lips. “He can’t help me. No one can.”

As Penhaligon moved closer, he saw the full, horrific tableau. Thomas was not alone. In the shadows behind him lay another man, unmoving. And next to him, a pile of discarded clothing. It was Gable’s method. He brought on desperate men, men with no families to miss them. He paid them in cash for a week, building their trust. Then, on the night of a shipment, he made them one final payment. They would help him load the lead ingots into the crates, and then he would add one last piece of cargo. A man, shackled and gagged, would be wedged into a false bottom. The declared weight was for the lead. The lighter weight on arrival was the weight of the lead, minus the man, who would be sold into servitude in the German industrial towns, a ghost erased from the world.

Thomas had been the last one. He’d been slated for tonight’s crate. But he was wiry and desperate. He’d broken free, killed the man Gable sent to silence him, and had been hiding here ever since, trapped in the warehouse, waiting for the end.

“He’s a monster,” Thomas whispered, his gaze fixed on the dead man. “He calls it ‘shipping the light’.”

A heavy tread sounded at the warehouse entrance. Gable stood there, a lantern in one hand and a stout cudgel in the other. His placid face was gone, replaced by a mask of cold fury.

“Penhaligon,” he said, his voice a silken threat. “You should have stayed away.”

What followed was not a fight, but a brutal, clumsy scramble in the dark. Penhaligon used the crowbar, Gable the cudgel. They crashed through stacks of empty crates, the sounds of splintering wood and grunts of pain echoing in the vast space. It was Thomas who ended it. He lunged from the shadows with the heavy brass mechanism he’d been working on, bringing it down on Gable’s skull with a sickening crunch. The warehouse owner crumpled to the floor, the lantern rolling from his grasp and extinguishing, plunging them into absolute blackness.

Later, on the deck of a police launch chugging up the Thames, the sun began to rise, painting the polluted sky in hues of rose and grey. Thomas sat wrapped in a blanket, shivering, but the feral look was gone from his eyes. Penhaligon stood beside him, watching the familiar silhouette of Greenwich come into view. The case was closed. The police had their villain, a warehouse full of evidence, and a story that would scandalise the city for a week before being forgotten. But Penhaligon knew the real work was just beginning. He looked at the broken young man beside him and thought of the old Captain waiting in his house of ghosts. Finding Thomas Thorne had been the easy part. Bringing him home would be the true voyage.
