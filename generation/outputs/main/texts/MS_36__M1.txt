My mind is a tempestuous sea, and the events of this day have been the storm that has broken my last frail vessel. That I should sit here, in the quietude of my chamber, with the moonlight streaming upon the floor, and yet feel the very walls to be closing in upon me, is a condition of the spirit so dire, so utterly without remedy, that I can scarce command the hand that writes these lines. Yet write I must, lest the tumult of my thoughts burst my brain asunder, and leave me a creature of unreasoning anguish. To whom can I confide my sorrows, if not to this silent page, which has heard the sighs of my girlhood and the first, trembling aspirations of my soul?

My guardian, Mr. Finch, is the noblest of men. Of this there can be no doubt. He has been to me, for these ten years past, more than a father—a guide, a protector, a friend whose generosity has known no bounds. Since the cruel sea claimed my father and the fever my sainted mother, leaving me a penniless orphan of but eight years, it was Mr. Finch who took me to his home, Ashwood Hall, who bestowed upon me his name and his care. He who is a man of grave years and settled temperament has ever treated my girlish fancies with a tender indulgence, and has given me an education and a station in life far beyond my deserts. How, then, could I feel aught but the most fervent gratitude and affection for him? When, this very evening, he took my hand and, with a gravity that became him, spoke of a future shared between us, of a union that would secure my happiness and his own, I felt my heart swell with a warm, if tranquil, regard. It was a prospect of peace, of unbroken security. He spoke not of a passion that consumes, but of a companionship that endures. "Your welfare, my dear Emmeline," said he, "has been the study of my life; let me make it the happiness of my future." And I, in the innocence of my heart, believed that I could return this happiness; that the fondness I bore him was a sufficient foundation for the most solemn of all vows. I told him I must have one night to reflect, that my decision should be worthy of his goodness, and he, with his accustomed kindness, withdrew, leaving me to the sweet and serious contemplation of my fate.

It was in this mood of gentle resolve that I sought the solitude of the old library, meaning to lose myself for an hour in some familiar tale, and thus prepare my mind for the momentous choice before me. The air was thick with the scent of old leather and dried flowers, and the tall windows looked out upon the darkening Kentish woods, where the first stars were beginning to prick the twilight. I was running my hand along a shelf of my mother’s books, those precious volumes that were all I had left of her, when my fingers closed upon a small, carved casket of rosewood, which I had never before seen. It was not locked. My heart, I know not why, began to beat with a strange and fearful quickness. Within, upon a bed of faded velvet, lay a single letter, sealed with black wax, and a miniature portrait of my mother as a girl—younger, far younger than I had ever known her, with a look of wild, unclouded joy in her eyes that was altogether new to me. The letter was in her hand, but the address was not to my father. It was to Mr. Finch.

I will not describe the agony of indecision that tore my bosom as I held that letter. To read it seemed a sacrilege against my mother’s memory, a betrayal of my guardian’s trust. Yet a voice within me, a dreadful and irresistible impulse, compelled me on. My hands trembled so violently that I could scarce break the seal. The words that met my eyes were not of love, as I had feared, but of a deeper, more terrible thing—a final and irrevocable renunciation.

"My dearest, my ever-beloved Edmund," it began, and the very first words struck a coldness to my soul. "When you receive this, I shall be another's. Do not think me fickle, do not curse me as a creature of ingratitude. My heart is, and ever will be, your own. But my duty to my family, the desperate circumstances of my father's affairs, compel me to a course from which my spirit revolts. Mr. Ashworth has offered a hand that will save my father from ruin. It is a sacrifice I must make. Forgive me, Edmund. Forget the wretched girl who was not strong enough to defy the world for your sake. I have returned your letters, save this one, which I cannot bear to part with. Think of me as one dead to you. May you find a love more worthy, more constant, and more fortunate than your unhappy Clara."

My mother! My gentle, pious mother! The author of these words of passionate despair! And Mr. Finch—my grave, my benevolent, my ever-calm guardian—was the Edmund of her youthful love! The world seemed to reel before my eyes. The tranquil affection I had felt but an hour since was now a thing of mockery and horror. His proposal was not the simple offer of a man who had watched over an orphan; it was the culmination of a life's-long, secret, and unrequited passion. He had not sought to make me happy; he sought to possess a living memory of the woman he had lost. I was not Emmeline, his ward, to be cherished for my own sake; I was Clara’s daughter, a pale reflection, a second chance at a long-dead dream. The goodness, the generosity, the very kindness I had revered, now appeared in a new and ghastly light. It was not disinterested care, but the slow, patient, and possessive devotion of a man who had made an idol of the past.

Oh, the sublime misery of this discovery! The kindness that was to have been my refuge has become my prison. To accept him would be to become the ghost of my own mother, to live out a romance that ended in sorrow and sacrifice, to be the consolation prize for a grief he has nursed for a quarter of a century. To refuse him would be to wound the only protector I have ever known, to seem an ungrateful wretch, to cast myself out from this home that has been my world. The storm of my feelings is absolute. I look out at the ancient, stoic oaks that have weathered a hundred winters, and I envy their impassive strength. My own spirit is a reed, bent and broken by this sudden, terrible gale. What is my duty? What is my heart? They are at war, and the field of battle is my own desolate soul. He has built a life around a memory, and I have been the unwitting cornerstone. To say yes is a lie; to say no is a cruelty. And I, who have been taught to value truth and goodness above all things, am now caught in a labyrinth where every path leads to a precipice of despair. The moon has climbed high, and its cold light seems to mock the fever of my soul. I am alone, utterly alone, with a choice that is no choice at all, but only the selection of the manner of my own unhappiness.
