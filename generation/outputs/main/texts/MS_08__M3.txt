It was in the autumn of the year 1814 that the affliction fell upon my father, Arthur Vance, with the sudden and devastating finality of an avalanche, stripping from him the celestial light to which he had devoted the labours of a lifetime. From my earliest infancy, I had known him only in his solitary observatory upon the brow of Alder’s Hill in Surrey, a lonely edifice swept by the tempestuous winds that howled across the heath, where he had passed his nights in communion with the distant luminaries, reading the heavens as one reads the deeply familiar pages of a cherished volume. I, his only child, Clara, had been both his pupil and his humble assistant, tasked with the recording of those arduous calculations by which he mapped the wandering stars; yet, in the terrible pride of his intellectual masculine vigour, he had never deigned to perceive me as aught but an instrument of his own will, an amanuensis to execute his dictation, rather than a mind capable of independent comprehension. When the cruel shadow descended upon his sight, extinguishing the glories of the firmament and confining him to a darkness impenetrable, he mourned not with the quiet resignation of a Christian, but with the fierce, impassioned despair of a Prometheus bound, railing against the cruelty of fate with a bitterness that wrung my very soul.

"Daughter," he exclaimed, in a voice broken by the intensity of his anguish, as he sat in the gloomy chamber beneath the dome, his sightless eyes turned upward as though to pierce the veil that separated him from his beloved stars, "the universe is annihilated, and I am buried alive within the sepulchre of my own being! The labour of forty years is rendered void; the map of the heavens, which I had hoped to bequeath to posterity as a monument of human intellect, must now remain a fragment, a mockery of its intended grandeur. I am encompassed by eternal night, and the stars are extinguished for ever!"

I listened to his lamentations with a heart divided between the profoundest pity and a sorrowful consciousness of my own capabilities; for I knew that the vast knowledge he had accumulated, the intricate observations he had gathered, were deeply impressed upon my own mind, and that I, who had toiled beside him through the long, solitary vigils of a thousand nights, possessed the skill and the devotion to complete the work he had begun. I approached him with a timidity born of reverence, and, kneeling beside his chair, I ventured to implore him to permit me to continue his observations, to ascend into the dome and trace the courses of the stars he could no longer see, and to complete the catalogue that was the master-work of his existence. "My father," I said, with an earnestness that trembled into tears, "do not despair; the light is not wholly extinguished while it yet burns in the mind. Suffer me to be your eyes; let me ascend to the dome and record the positions of those orbs which you have taught me to distinguish; permit me to complete the work you have so nobly begun, and thus preserve your name from oblivion."

He started at my words, as though struck by a physical blow, and, withdrawing his hand from my grasp, he turned upon me a countenance wherein sorrow was mingled with a proud and wounded indignation. "You!" he cried, "you, a woman, presume to tread the paths of celestial science, to wrest from the heavens their hidden mysteries? It is not for you, Clara, to grasp the telescope or to calculate the orbits of the spheres; your mind, however dutiful, is unequal to the vastness of the conception, the rigour of the mathematical reasoning required. The task is mine alone, and if I cannot execute it, it must remain undone. I would not betray the dignity of science by committing it to hands so frail."

I felt the sting of his disbelief acutely, yet I did not remonstrate; for I knew that his scorn was born not of cruelty, but of that very love of his work which made him incapable of entrusting it to another, and that to argue would be to provoke his wrath and deepen his despair. I withdrew in silence, but with a resolution fixed within my breast, a calm and melancholy determination to prove my devotion by the only means that could avail—that of silent, unaided perseverance. I determined that I would continue the observations in secret, guided by the precepts he had instilled in me, and that I would present to him, when the work was complete, a catalogue so accurate and so flawless that even his prejudices must yield before the evidence of my capability.

Night after night, when the house was wrapped in slumber and my father lay in his darkened chamber, haunted by the phantoms of his lost luminaries, I ascended the spiral staircase to the dome, and, seating myself before the great telescope, I communed with the stars. The silence of the observatory was broken only by the soughing of the wind across the desolate heath, and the occasional creak of the dome as it revolved beneath my hand; the sublimity of the scene filled me with a mingled awe and melancholy, for I felt myself to be a solitary pilgrim in a vast and indifferent universe, bearing upon my shoulders the weight of my father’s shattered hopes. The heavens, which had been his joy and his consolation, were to me but a field of duty, a barren expanse wherein I must labour without recognition or reward, sustained only by the filial love that demanded I spare him the knowledge of his own obsolescence.

The work was arduous beyond description; for I was compelled to calculate not only the positions of the stars, but to verify and extend the computations my father had left unfinished, to trace the intricate dances of the distant worlds, and to commit to paper, with a hand that trembled between hope and dread, the results of my nightly vigils. I was often overcome by fatigue and by the cold that seeped through the joints of the dome, and there were moments when the magnitude of my self-imposed task appeared so vast, so hopeless, that I was tempted to abandon it and resign myself to the quiet grief of watching my father’s decline. But the memory of his despair, the recollection of that anguished cry with which he had lamented the ruin of his life’s work, sustained me; I felt that I was fighting not merely for the completion of a scientific catalogue, but for the preservation of a mind upon the brink of dissolution, and that if I failed, the darkness that encompassed him would claim his reason as it had claimed his sight.

Months passed in this secret labour, and as the catalogue grew, so too did my confidence; yet the moment of revelation approached with a terror that haunted my waking hours. I could not conceal the work from him indefinitely, and I knew that when I placed the completed catalogue before him, I must be prepared to witness the conflict of his emotions—the astonishment, the pride, and the bitter humiliation of a proud spirit forced to acknowledge its dependence upon one whom he had deemed inferior. I feared that the very proof of my capability would wound him more deeply than his blindness, by reminding him that the work he had believed could be executed by himself alone had been accomplished by another; and yet I could not refrain, for the alternative was to permit the work to perish, and that, I knew, would be a death-blow to his spirit.

It was upon a tempestuous evening, when the rain beat against the windows of the observatory and the wind howled through the surrounding trees, that I at last placed before him the completed catalogue, a monument of my solitary toil. He ran his fingers over the pages, tracing the familiar figures with a touch that was at once reverent and incredulous; and as he read, I observed the workings of his countenance, the gradual softening of his stern features, the tremour of his lip, and the gathering of tears within his sightless eyes. "Clara," he murmured, in a voice so altered that it seemed to issue from the very depths of his broken heart, "you have done this—you, my child—you have traced the heavens and completed my work; and I, in my blind arrogance, believed you incapable. Forgive me, my daughter, forgive the pride that scorned you, and accept a father’s gratitude, which, though it comes too late to atone for my injustice, is yet sincere and profound."

I threw my arms about his neck and wept; for in that moment of reconciliation, the bitterness of my long concealment, the anguish of his unkindness, and the joy of his acknowledgment were commingled in a flood of emotion too vast for words. We sat together in the darkness, the father and the daughter, bound by a love that had triumphed over prejudice and pride, while the storm raged without and the stars, invisible to us both, pursued their ancient courses across the boundless firmament.
