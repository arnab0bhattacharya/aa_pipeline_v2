The Hanbury Varieties was no Alhambra. Its air, thick with the fug of damp wool, cheap tobacco and the sweetness of spilled gin, pressed upon one from the moment of entry. The light of the gas-jets was sickly and yellow, catching the gilded plaster of the proscenium arch and the flushed, expectant faces of the crowd. It was here, amidst the boos and catcalls of a Saturday night audience, that the most polished jewel in the ducal family of Arden had vanished.

The man accused was one Signor Valeriano, a conjuror of some local renown, whose act of ‘Audacious Borrowing’ had been the jewel in the evening’s crown. The victim was the Duchess of Arden herself, a formidable woman in grey silk, who sat now in the manager’s office with an expression of glacial fury. The stolen article was a bracelet of considerable value: a band of yellow gold, from which hung a single, flawless sapphire the size of a thrush’s egg.

“It was in my hand,” the Duchess stated, her voice a low, cutting instrument. “The Signor requested it. He held it aloft, turning it so the light caught the stone. Then, with a flourish, he placed it in a small, black lacquered box upon his table. A moment later, he opened the box and returned it to me. But it is a worthless copy! The paste is poorly set, the gold a dull alloy. The man is a common thief.”

Signor Valeriano, a lean man with a theatrical moustache and eyes that held a permanent look of wounded pride, wrung his hands. “Your Grace, I swear upon my professional honour, upon the saints themselves, that the bracelet I returned was the very one you entrusted to me. It is impossible! My hands, you see, they are my livelihood. They do not make such a mistake.”

It was into this tense little tableau that my companion was shown. Mr. Alistair Finch was not a man to be impressed by titles or theatrical distress. His gaze, sharp and analytical, took in the scene with a single, sweeping glance: the duchess’s imperious posture, the conjuror’s agitated hands, the gaudy posters on the wall advertising ‘Signor Valeriano, Master of Prestidigitation!’

“A fascinating problem,” he murmured to me, his voice low. “A miracle trick that has become a magician’s crime. Let us examine the theatre of operations.”

We were led upon the stage. The Hanbury’s boards were sticky underfoot, and the air was chill. Finch ignored the manager’s chatter and made directly for the conjuror’s table, a heavy piece of furniture draped in worn velvet. Upon it stood the small, black lacquered box, its lid ajar.

“A simple affair,” Finch observed, lifting the box. He tapped its sides, peered inside, then placed it back upon the velvet. He ran a slender finger along the table’s surface, his eyes missing nothing. He then turned to the conjuror. “Signor, be so good as to walk me through your movements. Precisely.”

Valeriano, regaining a little of his stage presence, obliged. “I approach Her Grace in the front row. I request a trinket. She offers the bracelet. I show it to all, so they may see it is genuine. I return here, to my table. I place the bracelet in the box—so. I close the lid. I make the pass—thus—with my silk handkerchief. I open the box, and the bracelet is gone! It has travelled, you see? To a place beyond this world. Then, with a second pass, it returns! I take it from the box and restore it to Her Grace.”

“The box you opened the second time,” Finch said, his tone flat, “was it the same box you closed the first time?”

“But of course!” the conjuror cried, aghast at the very suggestion.

Finch did not answer. He was examining the velvet cloth upon the table. He lifted a corner, his brow furrowed. “The pile of this velvet is deep. It would conceal a multitude of sins.” He turned his attention to the wings of the stage, where a young woman was standing half in shadow. She was the assistant, a pale girl with dark hair and watchful eyes, dressed in a costume of black spangles that seemed to drink the meagre light. She held a tray of other props—a set of billiard balls, a length of rope.

“And you, miss…?”

“Marie,” she said, her voice barely a whisper. “Marie Dubois.”

“You were in the wings throughout the performance?”

“Oui, monsieur.”

“Your duties?”

“To hand the Signor what he requires. The rope, the balls, the doves. And to take things from him when he is finished.”

Finch nodded slowly. He walked past her, his gaze sweeping the narrow space behind the flats. It was a chaos of discarded scenery, ropes, and dust. He stopped, his attention caught by something at the foot of a large, painted canvas depicting a pastoral scene. He knelt. When he rose, he held a single, tiny object between his thumb and forefinger. It was a small, wooden disc, no larger than a shilling, and from one side of it protruded a short, sharp pin.

He returned to the table and placed the disc upon the velvet. He then asked the Signor for the returned bracelet, which the Duchess had surrendered to the police inspector. It was a sad-looking thing, the paste stone clouded and the gold lacking lustre.

“Observe,” Finch said to me, his voice holding the quiet satisfaction of a man who has found the key to a lock. “The duchess’s bracelet is of a fine, lightweight design. The clasp is delicate.” He held up the counterfeit. “This one, however, is heavier. The metal is crude. It is designed for a different purpose entirely.” He took the small wooden disc and, with a deft movement, pressed the pin into the clasp of the false bracelet. It held fast. He then placed the false bracelet upon the velvet and gave it a slight push. It slid across the deep pile with an unnerving smoothness, as if on ice.

“A weighted disc, magnetised or simply pinned to the clasp,” he explained. “It gives the object momentum and a low centre of gravity. Now, the trick is not in the box, but on the table.” He looked at the conjuror, whose face had gone the colour of putty. “You placed the real bracelet in the box. A moment later, with the audience’s eyes upon your face and your hands, you slid the false one from a hidden pocket in the velvet drape. It travelled across the table, guided by this disc, into the waiting hand of your assistant in the wings.”

All eyes turned to Marie Dubois. She did not flinch, but a single tear traced a path through the white powder on her cheek.

“She passes you the props,” Finch continued, his voice relentless. “She also receives them. The real bracelet, having been palmed from the box during your flourish, was passed to her on a subsequent exchange—perhaps when she handed you the silk for the ‘pass’. The evidence is in her costume.”

The inspector, a stout man with a bristling moustache, stepped forward. Finch held up a hand. “A moment, if you please.” He turned to the girl. “The pin, mademoiselle. It was to give the false bracelet a perfect, sliding trajectory. A clever device. But it left a clue. On the sequins of your costume, at the level of your hip, there is a single, bright scratch in the gilt of a sequin, where the pin must have snagged as you concealed the real bracelet in your skirt.”

Marie Dubois let out a small, choked sob. She did not deny it. The conjuror, Valeriano, collapsed onto a nearby crate, burying his face in his hands. The motive, it transpired, was not greed, but a desperate need to pay for a life-saving operation for the girl’s younger brother in Paris.

Finch turned away from the pathetic scene, his work done. He looked out from the stage into the darkened, empty auditorium, where the ghosts of the evening’s laughter and applause seemed to linger.

“A conjuror’s art,” he said to me, his voice soft and reflective, “is the art of misdirection. They persuade you to look here, while the truth happens there. The tragedy is that the most brilliant misdirection is often born of the most human desperation. Come, Watson, the curtain has fallen. Let us find a cab.”
