There is no more dangerous thing in London than a woman who is perfectly good. Lady Alvering had that reputation, and she wore it as she wore her pearls, with a lustrous calm that made one feel that virtue, like a diamond, is chiefly a question of cutting and setting. She was the arbiter of a dozen causes, the friend of bishops, and the silent partner in a political party; her drawing-room in Grosvenor Street was the only place in England where a radical might dine with a duke without either of them losing his principles, for the simple reason that neither of them had any. It was here that I first met Mr. Cyprian Vale.

He had come up to London, as so many clever young men do, with nothing but a silver tongue and a suit that was just respectable enough to suggest a future. Within three years he had taken silk in his imagination and a seat at every dinner-table of consequence in fact. He was not handsome, which was a great advantage, for it compelled him to be charming; and he was not rich, which was a greater one, for it made him indispensable to those who were. His wit had the polish of a well-kept blade, and he turned it upon the follies of the age with such exquisite grace that even the fools he wounded were flattered to have been the occasion of such a brilliant stroke. He was, in short, the sort of man who is destined for Parliament, and Parliament was the only destiny he desired.

‘My dear Mr. Vale,’ said Lady Alvering to him one evening, when the last of the vicars and the first of the politicians had gone, ‘you are wasting your talents upon juries. A man who can make a lie sound like a modest truth in the Old Bailey could make a national bankruptcy sound like a national triumph in the House of Commons. I have a seat going at Westmorebridge. It is a pocket borough, of course, but then all the best things are kept in pockets.’

Vale bowed. ‘You are too kind, Lady Alvering. But a man must have a cause.’

‘A cause is a very expensive luxury,’ she replied, smiling into the fire. ‘One needs a wife to pay for it. My niece, Sybil, has just come into her fortune. She is a good girl, a little too fond of flowers and the poor, but those are faults that marriage usually cures. She admires you immensely. She thinks your speeches are like perfect sonnets.’

There is a particular kind of silence that falls in a drawing-room when a man is asked to barter his heart for a career. It is not the silence of shock, but of calculation. Vale looked at Lady Alvering. She was leaning back against the cushions, her face half in shadow, and in that moment she was not the philanthropist or the political hostess, but something infinitely more dangerous: a woman who knew exactly what she was offering, and what she was withholding.

‘And what does Lady Alvering think of my speeches?’ he asked, his voice perfectly level.

‘I think,’ she said slowly, ‘that they are very dangerous. They make one believe in things that one knows are not true. But then, that is the definition of a great politician, is it not?’

She offered him a cigarette from a tortoiseshell box. Their fingers brushed. It was a contact so slight as to be accidental, yet it carried the voltage of a confession. In that touch, he understood the whole geometry of the trap. She was offering him the niece, the seat, the future—a life of polished mediocrity in exchange for the one thing he actually wanted: the chance to stand in her shadow and be warmed by it. To marry the niece was to become a permanent fixture in the house of the aunt. It was a masterpiece of cruelty, disguised as a kindness.

‘Sybil is a sweet girl,’ Vale said, accepting the cigarette.

‘She is an angel,’ Lady Alvering corrected him. ‘And angels, I am told, make excellent wives. They are so accustomed to the society of the Almighty that they are never disappointed by men.’

The marriage was arranged with the efficiency of a military campaign. Sybil was delighted; she looked at Vale with the wide, adoring eyes of a convert. She was pretty in a soft, water-colour way—lots of pale blues and whites, like a sketch by Burne-Jones before he discovered the joys of sin. She talked about Ruskin and the dignity of labour, and Vale listened with the grave attention he usually reserved for hostile witnesses. He found her innocence to be a kind of moral opulence; it was thick, cloying, and entirely without flavour.

The wedding was a triumph of social engineering. Lady Alvering stood at the back of the church, dressed in a deep violet that was almost black, looking like a magnificent, tragic widow at the birth of a new era. When Vale led his bride down the aisle, he caught the lady’s eye. She smiled—a small, enigmatic curve of the lips that contained the entire history of their transaction. It was the smile of a sculptor admiring a statue she had commissioned but would never own.

Life at Westmorebridge was everything Lady Alvering had promised. The constituency was as docile as a flock of sheep, and the seat in the House was as comfortable as a club armchair. Vale rose rapidly. His charm was a currency that never devalued, and his cleverness a weapon that never dulled. He spoke of Empire with the fervour of a lover and of the poor with the pity of a god. Sybil managed his household and his charities with a serene competence that left him entirely free to be brilliant. They were the perfect couple: he provided the wit, she provided the virtue.

Yet, there was a hollowness at the centre of the arrangement. Vale found that he could not look at Sybil without seeing the aunt. The girl’s pale blue eyes were a diluted version of the steel grey ones he craved. Her touch was cool and chaste, a poor imitation of that accidental spark in the drawing-room. He had bought a beautiful cage, but the bird he wanted was still flying free in Grosvenor Street.

One evening, a few years later, he was summoned to Lady Alvering’s house. The season was over; the leaves were turning gold in the Park, the colour of old lace and regret. She was alone in the library, sitting by the fire. She looked older, perhaps, but time had treated her as it treats a fine wine, merely adding depth to the colour.

‘You have done well, Cyprian,’ she said. ‘The Prime Minister was speaking of you only yesterday. He says you are the coming man.’

‘I have had an excellent teacher,’ he replied, standing by the mantelpiece.

‘Sybil is happy?’

‘Sybil is an angel,’ he said, echoing her words from years before. ‘She is perfectly happy. She has just opened a soup kitchen in Bethnal Green. She says it is the most fulfilling work she has ever done.’

‘And you? Are you fulfilled?’

Vale looked at her. The firelight played across her face, illuminating the fine lines around her eyes, the heavy luster of her pearls. She was holding a small jade paperweight in her hand, turning it over and over.

‘I have everything I ever wanted,’ he said. ‘I have the seat. I have the reputation. I have a wife whom the world admires.’

‘But do you have what you love?’ she asked softly.

The question hung in the air, heavy and fragrant, like incense. It was the one question he could not answer with a quip. He looked at the woman he had wanted, the woman who had sold him her niece to keep him close, the woman who had orchestrated his entire life like a symphony. He realized then that he had not been the clever one. He had been the instrument.

‘Love is a luxury for the poor, Lady Alvering,’ he said at last, his voice hardening into the tone he used in the courtroom. ‘The rich have to settle for beauty. And I am surrounded by it.’

She smiled again, that same enigmatic smile. ‘How very wise you have become,’ she said. ‘It is such a pity. Wisdom is so often the tomb of the spirit.’

She rose and walked to the window, looking out at the darkening street. ‘You may go, Cyprian,’ she said, without turning round. ‘Sybil will be waiting for you. She is always waiting. It is her greatest virtue.’

Vale bowed to her back. As he left the room, he felt a sudden, overwhelming urge to smash the jade paperweight she had been holding, to shatter the silence, to scream that he hated the soup kitchen and the angels and the perfect, suffocating goodness of his life. But he did not. He walked out into the London fog, a man with a brilliant future and a hollow heart, realizing too late that the only thing more expensive than a cause was a woman who loved you enough to make you suffer for it.
