The house stood solitary on the moor, a grey stone edifice beaten by every wind that swept down from the crags, and I had come to it in autumn, when the heather was fading and the skies were low and leaden. Mr. Ashworth had engaged me as governess to his son, a boy of nine years, and had told me, in a voice that permitted no inquiry, that the child was of a sullen and intractable disposition, and that I must be firm with him. The mother, I was given to understand, had died some four years since of a wasting illness; and on this subject the household preserved a silence so complete that I soon learned to imitate it.

Edmund was not sullen. I discovered this within a fortnight of my arrival, and the discovery troubled me more than his reputed moroseness could have done. He was silent, yes—silent as a creature that has learned to keep its own counsel—but his silence was not the stolid indifference of a dullard, nor the insolence of a spoiled child. It was the silence of fear. I saw it in the way his eyes flew to the door when a step sounded in the passage; in the way his small hands clenched upon the edge of his desk when, of an evening, the master of the house returned from his rounds; in the way he would start and pale at the sound of his father's voice below stairs, though that voice was never raised in anger that I could hear.

At first I set it down to the natural awe of a child for a stern parent, and I endeavoured to win him by gentleness. I read to him in the long afternoons, when the rain beat against the windows and the fire burned low in the grate; I walked with him on the moor when the weather permitted, and taught him the names of the wildflowers that lingered in the hollows. He would listen, and sometimes he would smile—a fleeting, furtive smile, as though he feared to be caught in the act of pleasure—but he never spoke of his father, and if I chanced to mention Mr. Ashworth's name, he would fall silent at once and fix his eyes upon the ground.

One evening in November, when the wind was howling round the house like a thing possessed, and Mr. Ashworth had ridden to the village on some matter of business, I sat with Edmund before the schoolroom fire. He had been restless all day, starting at every sound, and now he sat with his knees drawn up to his chin, staring into the flames with a fixedness that was not natural in a child.

"Edmund," said I, "what ails you? You have been like a frightened hare since morning."

He did not answer, but his thin fingers plucked at the hem of his jacket.

"Come," I pursued, "you are not to be afraid of me. I am your friend, if you will let me be. Tell me what troubles you."

He lifted his eyes to mine, and in that look I saw a struggle—the struggle of a child who has been sworn to secrecy, and whose small heart is bursting with the weight of it. Then he said, in a voice so low that I had to bend towards him to catch the words: "She will come back. She said she would come back."

"Who will come back?" I asked, though a cold foreboding seized me even as I spoke.

"My mother," he said. "She is not dead. She is not in the churchyard. She went away in the night, and she said she would come back for me, and she has not come, and I wait and I wait—" His voice broke, and he hid his face in his hands.

I sat as one turned to stone. The wind shrieked in the chimney, and the fire leaped and sank; but I heard nothing, I saw nothing, save the small bowed figure before me and the terrible revelation that his words had made. Not dead! Not in the churchyard! Then the household lied—his father lied—and the child had been left to feed upon a hope that must surely be false, or a truth that was worse than any lie.

"Edmund," I said at last, and my voice was steady, though my heart beat thick and fast, "who told you that your mother was dead?"

"Mrs. Rook," he answered, naming the housekeeper. "She said I was never to speak of her—that she was dead and gone, and that I must not ask questions. But I know she is not dead. I heard them talking once—Mrs. Rook and the master—and they said she had gone off in the night with nothing but the clothes she stood in, and that she would never dare to show her face again. But she said she would come back. She kissed me and said, 'Wait for me, Edmund, and I will come back.' And I have waited—I have waited four years—"

His voice failed him utterly, and he sobbed as though his heart would break. I drew him to me and held him, and I felt the thin, trembling body shake against my breast, and I knew that I had stumbled upon a secret that the master of this house had striven to bury as surely as though he had buried it in the earth.

What manner of woman was she, I wondered, who had fled from her home in the dead of night, leaving her child behind? What manner of man was he who had told his son that she was dead, and had set a seal of silence upon the whole household? I looked into the fire, and I thought of Mr. Ashworth's cold, hard face, and of the way his eye could chill the blood; and I thought of the boy's terror when his father's step was heard, and I said to myself that I had seen enough of tyranny in my life to know its marks.

"Hush, my child," I said, when his sobs had somewhat abated. "Hush, and listen to me. You have done right to tell me. You shall not be alone with this any longer. But you must not speak of it to any other—not to Mrs. Rook, not to the servants, not to your father. Do you understand?"

He nodded, wiping his eyes with his sleeve.

"Your mother," I continued, choosing my words with care, "may yet return. But whether she does or no, you must be brave, and you must not let your fear of your father—" I stopped, for I saw his face whiten at the word.

"He will be angry," he whispered. "He will be very angry if he knows I have told."

"He shall not know from me," I said. "And now you must go to bed, and try to sleep, and remember that you have a friend in this house—one who will not betray you."

I took him to his chamber, and sat by him until his eyes closed and his breathing grew even and deep. Then I went back to the schoolroom, and stood at the window, and looked out upon the black moor and the driving clouds, and I said within myself: This is a house of lies, and I am dwelling in the midst of them. The mother is not dead—she fled—and the father has made a grave of his own silence, and buried his child's hope in it. What shall I do? What ought I to do?

The wind answered nothing. The rain beat on the pane. And far off, on the edge of the moor, I saw a light moving—some belated traveller, perhaps, or a will-o'-the-wisp—and I thought of the woman who had gone out into the night four years since, with nothing but the clothes she stood in, and had never returned. Where was she now? Did she live? Did she think of the boy she had left behind? Or had the moor swallowed her up, as it swallows so many, and was her promise but the vain words of a desperate creature who knew not what she said?

I turned from the window with a heavy heart. The fire had burned low, and the room was growing cold. I stirred the embers, and as the flame leaped up, I saw my own face reflected in the dark glass—pale, and troubled, and resolute. I was but a governess, a dependant, a nobody in this house; but I had a will of my own, and a sense of right and wrong that no master of a moorland grange should trample underfoot. The boy had trusted me. I would not betray him. And if, in the days to come, I should find the means to learn the truth of what had happened in this house, I would learn it—and I would not rest until I had done what lay in my power to right the wrong that had been done.

The door opened behind me, and I turned. Mr. Ashworth stood upon the threshold, his riding-cloak wet with rain, his face dark and stern in the firelight.

"You are late, Miss Everard," said he. "Where is the boy?"

"In bed, sir," I answered, "and asleep."

He looked at me narrowly, as though he would read my thoughts; but I met his eye without flinching, and after a moment he turned away.

"See that he is ready at seven," he said, and closed the door behind him.

I stood alone in the silent room, and I heard his step retreating down the passage, and I said to myself: I know your secret now, Mr. Ashworth, and you shall not frighten me.
