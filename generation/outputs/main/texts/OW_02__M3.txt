Arthur Pendlebury, a stockbroker of considerable respectability and absolutely no imagination, sat in the morning-room of his villa in Clapham, opening his letters. He performed this daily ritual with the solemn precision of a man who believes that the stability of the British Empire rests entirely upon the punctual perusal of his correspondence. Among the bills, the scribbled invitations from acquaintances he did not wish to see, and the usual circulars, lay an envelope of a peculiarly heavy texture, sealed with crimson wax and addressed in a hand that seemed to belong to a bygone century. It bore a Lisbon postmark.

He opened it, read it, and frowned. The letter, written in English of a slightly archaic and elaborate character, informed him that, through a complex and deeply romantic tangle of genealogical descent, he was the rightful heir to the Marquisate of Santa-Cruz. The present incumbent, a distant relative of incredible age and inveterate vice, had, it seemed, finally expired in a convent in Évora. The writer, who styled himself a devoted servant of the house, implored Mr. Pendlebury to assert his claim before the Portuguese court.

Arthur Pendlebury folded the letter, placed it back in its envelope, and deposited it beneath a paperweight of serpentine marble on his writing desk. Then he rang for his tea. He was a man who had always believed that romance was a thing one left on the continent when one returned to the serious business of making money in the City. To suddenly discover that one was expected to wear a coronet seemed to him not an honour, but a grave social inconvenience.

“It is some absurd mistake,” he said to his wife, Eleanor, when she swept into the room an hour later in a gown of silver-grey silk that rustled with the gentle insistence of autumn leaves. “A letter from a lunatic in Lisbon. It appears I am to be a Portuguese marquis.”

Eleanor Pendlebury paused in her adjustment of a vase of dead chrysanthemums. She was a woman of immense ambition and very little patience, who had married Arthur because society demanded it, and had spent the subsequent years attempting to transform her drawing-room into a salon that might attract people who had actually done something. She looked at her husband with a sudden, sharp interest that entirely altered the placid arrangement of her features.

“A marquis?” she repeated. “Of what place, did you say?”

“Santa-Cruz, I believe. Or some such name. It is entirely without foundation, my dear. I shall write to the fellow and tell him he has made a grievous error. I have no time for foreign titles. I have a meeting with the board of the South African Gold Fields at half-past two.”

He finished his tea and departed for the City, carrying the letter in his breast pocket, not because he wished to keep it, but because he had forgotten to throw it away.

In the hansom cab, as the dreary streets of Clapham gave way to the grander, more mercantile architecture of the City, Arthur Pendlebury found his thoughts straying from the price of gold. A marquis. The word had a curious, heavy, golden ring to it, like the sound of a coin dropped upon marble. He considered the Portuguese climate, which he had always heard was agreeable, and the Portuguese vineyards, which he presumed were profitable. He wondered what the uniform of a Portuguese marquis looked like, and whether the order of the Golden Fleece was given to men of finance. He was slightly annoyed with himself for wondering. By the time the cab reached the Bank of England, he had resolved to dismiss the matter entirely. He was an Englishman, and Englishmen do not abruptly become Portuguese noblemen. It is not done.

He spent the day at the Stock Exchange, but the figures on the tape seemed to dance in a foreign script. He looked at his fellow brokers, with their red faces and loud voices, their talk of consols and debentures, and for the first time in his life, he found them faintly vulgar. He lunched alone at his club, drinking a bottle of ordinary claret, and thought of the vineyards of the Douro, imagining them heavy with purple fruit, bathed in an Iberian sun. He remembered the letter in his pocket, took it out, read it again, and put it back. It was absurd, of course. And yet, he could not stop thinking about it. The idea had taken root in the orderly garden of his mind like a brilliant, foreign weed, refusing to be uprooted by the rake of common sense.

Meanwhile, in Clapham, Eleanor Pendlebury had not been idle. She had retired to her boudoir, a room decorated with a severity that she liked to think of as aesthetic, and had rung for her maid. She had then spent the morning in a state of quiet, intense, and entirely practical exhilaration.

When Arthur returned that evening, somewhat earlier than usual and in a mood of unusual abstraction, he found his wife awaiting him in the drawing-room. She was seated at her writing desk, a piece of parchment before her, examining a book of heraldry she had sent for from the London Library.

“My dear,” she said, without looking up, “the Santa-Cruz arms are azure, a cross argent, between four crescents or. They are exceedingly handsome. I have been sketching them.”

Arthur stared at her. “Eleanor, you are not seriously entertaining this preposterous claim?”

“I am entertaining nothing,” she replied, with the composure of a woman who has already furnished the future in her mind. “I am merely preparing for it. There is a great difference between the two. To entertain a thing is to admit doubt. To prepare for it is to display the proper confidence of one’s station.”

“But I have not replied to the letter,” he protested, loosening his collar, which seemed to him to have become unexpectedly tight. “I have no intention of replying. The whole business is ridiculous. I am a stockbroker. I have no desire to be a marquis.”

“Everyone has a desire to be a marquis, Arthur,” she said, turning a page of the heraldic dictionary with languid precision. “Those who say they do not are merely those who have never been offered the opportunity. As for replying, I have taken the liberty of doing so myself.”

Arthur sank into a chair, feeling somewhat as he had felt in his youth when he had been thrown from a horse—the breath entirely knocked out of him. “You have replied?”

“I have written to the gentleman in Lisbon,” Eleanor said, “informing him that the claim will be pursued, and that we shall require the necessary legal documents to establish my husband’s right. I also asked him for the measurements of the family seat, as I wish to order new curtains. The present ones in the best bedroom are entirely faded. I have also,” she continued, rising and moving to a side table, “ordered new stationery.”

She held up a sheet of thick, cream-coloured paper. It was embossed, in gold, with a coronet. Beneath the coronet, in an elegant script, were the words: *The Marchioness of Santa-Cruz*.

Arthur gazed at the stationery. He looked at his wife, who was examining it with the critical eye of a connoisseur. He thought of the board of the South African Gold Fields, of the loud men at the Stock Exchange, of the price of consols. He thought of the vineyards, the sun, the coronet, the heavy, golden ring of the word *marquis*. He looked at the letter on his desk, still resting beneath the serpentine marble.

“Eleanor,” he said, slowly, a strange, new note of authority entering his voice, a note that, had it been heard on the Stock Exchange, would have caused a flutter in the market, “I do not believe the coronet is quite correct. It should be a coronet of pearls, not strawberry leaves. If we are to do this thing, we must do it properly.”

Eleanor smiled, a smile of pure, undiluted triumph. “I thought you had no desire to be a marquis, Arthur,” she murmured.

“I have no desire to be an improper one,” he replied, taking the stationery from her hands and examining it with a sudden, keen attention. “If the Portuguese are willing to give me a title, I see no reason why I should not take it. After all, it is always the English who make the best foreigners.”
