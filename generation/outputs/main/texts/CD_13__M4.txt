Among the innumerable small establishments which cluster and cling, like barnacles upon a ship’s hull, to the muddy and malodorous shores of Rotherhithe, there stood, in the year ’forty-seven, a little pie-shop of so modest and retiring a countenance that it seemed to blush for its own existence between a coal-shed and a marine-store. It was kept by one Captain Bartholomew Bung, a retired sea-cook, who had for five-and-thirty years buffeted the waves in a manner that had left him with a complexion like a pickled walnut, a gait that rolled him from side to side of the pavement as if the solid earth were a deck in a heavy swell, and a vocabulary of which the least offensive terms were nautical and the most were not to be repeated. He had laid out the whole of his savings—a hoard of sovereigns kept in a stocking beneath his bunk, and counted so often that the milled edges were worn smooth as glass—in the purchase of this little shop, its fixtures, its oven, and its stock; and there he sat, of an evening, in a canvas apron, with his arms folded upon his chest, and his eye fixed upon the river, as if he expected the Custom House to send him a consignment of pork.

His pies were good. Let me do justice to the man: his pies were excellent. They were made after a receipt he had learned in the South Seas, from a cook who had been eaten by cannibals before he could divulge the whole of it, and Captain Bung had supplied the deficiency from his own invention. The crust was a marvel of flaky lightness; the gravy was a dark and mysterious fluid that warmed a man to the very cockles of his heart; and the meat within was always tender, always plentiful, and never—as was the custom with certain of his neighbours—of a character to provoke inquiry. The shop was called “The Jolly Tar,” and over the door hung a painted board representing a sailor with a wooden leg and a tankard, who had been intended for a likeness of the Captain himself, but who resembled him only in the particular of the wooden leg, which the Captain did not possess.

Now, it came to pass that upon a certain foggy morning in November, when the river was the colour of pea-soup and the air was so thick that a man might have spread it upon his bread, there presented himself at the door of “The Jolly Tar” a boy. He was a small boy, and a ragged one; his jacket had once been blue, but was now the colour of the mud beneath the water, and his trousers terminated abruptly at the knee, leaving his legs to the mercy of the elements. His face was a face to be remembered: sharp as a penknife, with two eyes in it that seemed to have been borrowed from a much older person and put in by mistake, so bright and knowing were they; and a mouth that curled at the corners with an expression of such serene and universal impudence that it was impossible to be angry with it. He wore no shoes, and his hair was the colour of a carrot that has been left too long in the ground.

“Pies,” said this apparition, in a voice that was hoarse as a raven’s and small as a sparrow’s. “Pies, and a prime lot of ’em. Which is the guv’nor?”

“I am the master of this here craft,” said Captain Bung, rolling forward from the oven, “and what may you be wanting, you young shaver?”

“I wants to sell your pies,” said the boy, without a moment’s hesitation. “I knows the river front from ’ere to Lime’ouse, and I knows the men as works it. I can sell your pies for you, and you can pay me a commission. One in ten, and a pie for my dinner. That’s my terms, and I don’t bate ’em.”

Captain Bung looked at the boy, and the boy looked at Captain Bung. The Captain was a man who had dealt with savages, with mutineers, with sharks, and with the Admiralty; but he had never before encountered a pair of eyes like those which now regarded him from beneath the carrot-coloured hair.

“What’s your name?” said the Captain.

“They calls me Snib,” said the boy.

“Snib!” repeated the Captain. “And what sort of a name is that for a Christian boy?”

“It’s the only one I’ve got,” said Snib, “and it’s a good sight better than none. Do you take my offer, or do I go and offer it to the cove at the corner?”

“I’ll take it,” said Captain Bung, “and be hanged to you. But if you eat more pies than you sell, I’ll have the law on you, you young pirate.”

“I ain’t a pirate,” said Snib, with dignity. “I’m a commercial man.”

From that day forth, the fortunes of “The Jolly Tar” underwent a change as sudden and as wonderful as any transformation in the Arabian Nights. The boy Snib was a genius in his way. He was up before the sun, and abroad upon the river-front with his tray upon his head, crying the pies in a voice that carried from one end of the wharf to the other. He knew every lighterman, every waterman, every coal-heaver, every mud-lark, every bargeman, and every loafer upon the shore; he knew their names, their habits, their hours, and their weaknesses; and he sold pies to them all. He would stand at the corner of a lane and whistle a particular air, and presently a head would appear at a window, and a hand would be thrust out with a penny in it. He would insinuate himself into the very heart of a gang of labourers at their dinner-hour, and come away with his tray empty and his pockets full of coppers. He had a word for every man, and a joke for every woman, and a wink for every child; and he was so impudent, so cheerful, and so universally successful, that the Captain, who watched his progress from the doorway of the shop, was wont to declare that the boy was worth his weight in gold, and that he had never seen his equal except once, in a monkey, upon the coast of Africa.

The business of “The Jolly Tar” doubled, and then trebled. The oven was heated twice a day, and then three times. The Captain was compelled to engage a boy to help him, and then another boy, and then a woman to wash the dishes. He took down the painted sailor with the wooden leg, and put up a new sign, upon which was inscribed, in letters of gold, “BUNG’S CELEBRATED PIES,” and beneath it, in smaller letters, “As Supplied to the Nobility.” He bought himself a new hat, and a silver watch, and a pair of boots that creaked when he walked; and he began to talk of taking a larger shop, and of setting up a cart, and of sending Snib out with a horse and van.

It was at this precise juncture, when fortune seemed to smile upon him with her most benignant aspect, that Captain Bung made a discovery which smote him to the very soul.

He had risen early one morning—earlier even than his wont, for the tide was to serve at six, and he had a great batch of pies to prepare for a party of bargemen who were to sail at eight—and he was standing at the back door of his shop, which opened upon a narrow and miry lane, when his attention was arrested by the sound of voices in low and earnest conversation. He listened. The voices were those of his boy Snib and a man whom the Captain had never seen before: a man in a rough greatcoat, with a slouched hat pulled low over his eyes, and a face that was all shadow and no feature. The man was speaking in a hoarse whisper, and Snib was answering in a tone of easy confidence.

“You’ll find ’em at the old place,” said the man. “The watchman’s been squared, and the door’s on the latch. It’s a good night’s work, and there’s a crown in it for you.”

“And what about the Captain?” said Snib.

“What Captain?”

“The old ’un. Him as makes the pies. He’s a good sort, he is, and I don’t want no trouble with him.”

“You leave the Captain to me,” said the man. “He’ll never know. And if he does—why, what’s a pie-shop to a man like you, Snib? You’ll be a rich man yet, and you won’t want to be selling pies for a living.”

The Captain heard no more. He stood as if rooted to the spot, his face the colour of the ashes in his oven, his hands clenched at his sides, and his heart beating a tattoo against his ribs. The man in the greatcoat slunk away down the lane, and Snib, after a moment’s pause, turned and walked slowly back towards the shop. He stopped when he saw the Captain.

There was a silence between them. The fog rolled in from the river, and the distant cry of a waterman came faintly through it. Snib looked at the Captain, and the Captain looked at Snib; and in the boy’s eyes there was neither shame nor defiance, but only a kind of weary resignation, as of one who has been found out in a world where finding out is the rule.

“So,” said Captain Bung, at last, in a voice that was very quiet and very terrible, “so you’re a thief, are you? You’re a thief, and I’ve been feeding you and paying you and trusting you, and all the time you’ve been running errands for a fence, and selling my pies to pay for your thieving. Get out of my shop, you young villain! Get out of my sight, and never let me see your face again!”

“Captain,” said the boy.

“Don’t ‘Captain’ me!” roared the old man, his wrath breaking forth at last like a tempest. “Don’t you dare to ‘Captain’ me! I’ve a good mind to take you by the scruff of your neck and hand you over to the police! I’ve a good mind to—”

He stopped. The boy had not moved. He stood there in the fog, with his tray upon his head and his hands in his pockets, and his sharp little face was very white.

“I never stole from you, Captain,” he said. “I never took a pie nor a penny as wasn’t mine. I sold your pies fair and square, and I gave you your share. What I did on the other side of the bridge was my own business, and it didn’t hurt you nor yours. But if you say I’m to go, I’ll go.”

He took the tray from his head and set it down upon the step. Then he turned and walked away into the fog, and the Captain stood in the doorway of “The Jolly Tar” and watched him go, and something in his old sea-hardened heart ached as it had not ached for many a year.
