It was in the winter of 1814, amidst the gathering fogs and freezing rains that shroud our metropolis in a season of desolation, that my brother, Valentine, first presented the creations of his spirit to the tribunal of public judgment. From his earliest youth, Valentine had been a being apart, marked by an ethereal sensibility that separated him from the common run of men. While other boys pursued their crude diversions in the muddy lanes, he wandered the solitary reaches of the heath, his soul vibrating to unseen harmonies, his heart swelling with an ambition that aimed at nothing less than the illumination of his species through the divine art of poesy. To this singular end he had devoted his existence, shunning the ordinary pleasures of youth, and nursing within his bosom a flame so ardent, that I, who watched his career with the intense devotion of a twin-born sister, often trembled to behold its perilous intensity. He had gathered his verses, the slow accumulation of years of solitary vigil and ecstatic suffering, into a single volume, and carried them to the feet of the venerable Lord Ashcroft, a critic whose decrees were held as the oracles of literary fame, and to whose judgment all aspirants submitted as to an unappealable destiny.

We waited, my mother and myself, in our humble lodging near the river, during the absence of Valentine on that fateful errand. I endeavoured to engage myself in some trivial task, but my heart throbbed with a suffocating anxiety; for I knew the immeasurable stake he had set upon this cast, and I feared alike the overwhelming power of a sudden joy, and the desolating effects of a hoped-for success denied. At last he returned. He entered the room with a calmness so unnatural, so frozen, that it struck a chill to my vitals more terrible than any passionate burst of grief. He was pale as death; his lips were firmly compressed, and his dark eyes gleamed with a fire that seemed to consume the very moisture of their orbs. He sat down, and for a full quarter of an hour remained silent, gazing fixedly on the floor. My mother dared not speak; she read ruin in his altered mien. At length he looked up, and a smile, so ghastly and despairing, crossed his pallid features. "It is over," he said, in a voice hollow and tremulous. "He has spoken. The world will never hear of Valentine Carey. My verses are lifeless weeds; they possess neither truth, nor beauty, nor the divine spark. I am no poet."

The words were few, but they conveyed a sentence of doom to his spirit. Lord Ashcroft, blind to the profound and melancholy beauty that shone through every line my brother had penned, had judged him with a coldness and severity that were little short of cruelty. He had spoken of affectation, of misplaced ambition, of a total want of that practical wisdom which alone, he declared, could give enduring value to the labours of the mind. Valentine heard him to the end; and then, without a murmur of self-defence, without one word of pleading, he gathered his manuscripts together, and, with a profound obeisance, left the presence of his judge. That night, as the storm raged without, and the wind howled through the crannies of our ancient dwelling, my brother sat alone in his chamber. I, who watched from the adjoining room, heard the ceaseless tramp of his footsteps, and the occasional stifled groan that spoke of mental agony too deep for utterance. Sleep was banished from my eyes; I listened in an agony of apprehension, until the grey dawn began to steal through the casement, and the sounds within his apartment ceased.

He emerged at the morning's light, his countenance haggard, but wearing an expression of settled, immoveable resolve. He bore in his hands a small furnace of iron, and a pile of papers, which I recognized, with a thrill of horror, as his manuscripts. Without a word he proceeded to the hearth, kindled a flame, and, with a deliberate and steady hand, cast the cherished labours of his genius into the destroying element. I shrieked aloud, and would have rushed forward to snatch them from the fire; but he waved me back with a gesture of such command, such mournful dignity, that I stood rooted to the spot, gazing through my tears at the curling flames that consumed the children of his brain. "It is well," he said, as the last black fragment crumbled into impalpable ashes; "the dream is over. I have been a madman, nursing a phantom in my bosom; but I am awakened. Farewell to poesy, farewell to the idle visions that have consumed my youth; I will henceforth devote myself to that knowledge which is substantial, to science, whose rewards are sure, and whose dictates do not depend upon the caprice of a fellow-creature."

From that hour Valentine Carey became another being. The radiant, hopeful youth was gone, and in his place stood a man of stern, unyielding purpose, who pursued the study of nature with an ardour that was not less intense because it was tinged with despair. He buried himself in the works of the great anatomists and chemists; he passed his days and nights in the laboratory, and shut out from his heart every remembrance of the bright aspirations that had once illuminated it. It was a piteous thing to see him thus transformed; to mark the heavy cloud that sat upon his brow, and to hear the suppressed sigh that sometimes escaped him in the midst of his abstruse researches. His natural gaiety had given place to a sombre taciturnity; and though he was never unkind, a cold, impassable barrier seemed to have risen between him and all the gentle affections of life.

But while he thus immolated his hopes upon the altar of what he deemed a rational philosophy, I, who knew the inmost depths of his nature, refused to believe that the divine gift had been utterly withdrawn. I could not forget the transports of enthusiasm with which he had formerly poured forth his soul in song; and I felt assured that the fire which had been so rudely stifled was not extinct, but only slumbering, waiting for some awakening touch to call it forth again in renewed vigour. It was during the period of his first poetic fervour that I had formed the design of preserving his verses, unknown to him. I had watched his careless way of scattering his compositions, and I feared lest these precious effusions should be lost to the world. Night after night, while he slept, I had copied them, line by line, into a volume which I treasured as the dearest relic of his genius. When I saw him destroy his manuscripts, a secret, unutterable joy took possession of my soul, amidst all my grief; for I knew that the offspring of his mind was not annihilated, but still lived, safely garnered in my keeping.

I have never breathed this secret to any human being. Valentine believes his poems given to the flames; and I have not had the courage to undeceive him. Sometimes, when I see him bowed over his scientific tomes, his cheek pale with study, and his eye dimmed by the midnight lamp, a voice within me pleads powerfully for disclosure. "Shall I not," I ask myself, "restore to him the lost treasures of his youth? Shall I not rekindle the smouldering embers of his poetic fire, and show him that the verdict of Ashcroft was not the sentence of Heaven?" But then the reflection of his settled despair, the firm, unalterable character of his resolve, and the fear of reopening a wound which time might have begun to heal, withhold me. He has chosen his path, and it may be that he is fulfilling the destiny for which he was created. Perhaps the discipline of suffering was necessary to develop the powers of his mind; and the very energy with which he pursues his scientific researches may be the ultimate means of his highest usefulness and fame. Yet, O my brother, if thou couldst know that the offspring of thy spirit still survive, that they are cherished and preserved by a fond and faithful heart, would it not send a thrill of joy through thy weary frame, and reillumine the desolate chambers of thy soul? I dare not tell him; I can only hope, and wait, and pray that the day may come when the mists of error shall be dissipated, and Valentine Carey, released from the thraldom of a false judgment, shall stand forth in his true character, a poet among the sons of men.
