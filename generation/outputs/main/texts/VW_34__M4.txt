The house, of course, remembered her best; or rather, it remembered the shape she left in it, the hollow of her, which was not the same as the woman herself. For thirty years Mrs. Craddock had moved through the upper corridors with her ring of keys, and now, in the weeks after the funeral, the keys hung on their hook in the still-room, and the house seemed to have lost, not a servant, but a certain gravity, as though a wall had been removed and the rooms, no longer held to their purpose, might drift. It was Mrs. Ellison, the cook, who said it first, wiping her hands on her apron and looking down the long passage to the baize door: “It’s the quiet,” she said. “She kept the quiet. Now it’s gone loose.” And the maids, who had feared her—the young ones, especially, who came up from the village and did not know that a floor could be swept in silence—they spoke of her in whispers, as of a weather that had passed, and one of them, Milly, said that she had never seen the house without Mrs. Craddock in it, and could not think how it would go on, and was told sharply by Mrs. Ellison to think less and dust more.

But the family—the family remembered a different woman. Lady Ashworth, in the morning-room, with the blinds half drawn against the August light, said to her son that she had been a great character, a real character, and that they should not see her like again; and she said it with a little sigh, as though she were speaking of a piece of furniture that had been in the family for generations and had been sold, or of a tree that had been felled. For Lady Ashworth had never quite known Mrs. Craddock, had known only the smooth surface of her, the folded hands, the lowered eyes, the voice that said, “Yes, my lady,” and “Very good, my lady,” and had never once, in thirty years, said, “I think, my lady,” or “If I may suggest.” And yet it was Mrs. Craddock, Lady Ashworth remembered, who had known, without being told, when the late Sir Henry was ill, and had sent up the beef tea before anyone had thought to order it; and who had, on the night of the fire in the west wing, when the servants were running with buckets and the family was standing in the drive in their nightclothes, appeared fully dressed, with a list of the silver in her hand, and had said, “It is all accounted for, my lady,” in a voice that had made the night seem ordinary again. That was the woman Lady Ashworth remembered—the woman who appeared, fully dressed, with a list. But she could not have told you what Mrs. Craddock thought about, or whether she was happy, or what she did on her half-days, or whether she had ever been in love.

Her son, Mr. Charles, who was now the master of the house, remembered her differently again. He remembered her as a boy, when she had been Mrs. Craddock the housekeeper, a tall dark figure in the passage, and he had been afraid of her, and had once, in a fit of childish spite, called her a witch, and had been whipped for it by his father. He remembered the smell of her—lavender and something else, something like cold stone—and the way she had looked at him when he had come in from the rain with his boots muddy, and had said nothing, only looked, and he had felt himself to be small and dirty and ashamed. And he remembered, too, the day she had found him in the library, when he was fifteen, with a book that was not for boys, and she had taken it from him and put it back on the shelf and said, “Your father would not like it, Master Charles,” and had never told, never once, in all the years. He remembered that, and he remembered that she had died in the little room at the top of the house, alone, and that no one had known she was ill until it was too late, and he felt, though he would not have said so, a kind of shame, as though he had failed in some duty that was never quite named.

And the servants—the servants remembered a tyrant. They remembered the way she had of standing at the head of the table in the servants’ hall, with her hands folded, and the silence that fell when she came in; and the way she had of saying, “That will do,” which meant that it would not do at all, and that you would hear more of it later. They remembered the rules—the hours, the uniforms, the precise way the knives were to be laid, the precise way the beds were to be turned down, the precise way the tea was to be made (one spoon for the pot, one for the master, and the water not quite boiling, for Mrs. Craddock held that boiling water spoiled the leaf). They remembered her favorites—for she had favorites, though she would never have admitted it—and they remembered her cruelties, which were small and exact, a word here, a look there, a half-day withheld. And yet, when she was gone, they found, in her room, a box of letters, and in the letters, nothing—only receipts, and lists, and a few dried flowers, and a photograph of a young man in a soldier’s uniform, who was no one they knew, and whose name was not spoken. And Mrs. Ellison, who had hated her and feared her and respected her for twenty years, sat down on the edge of the bed and cried, and could not have told you why.

But the village remembered her best, or remembered her first, which is not the same thing. For she had been born in the village—born in the cottage at the end of the lane, the one with the crooked chimney, and her name had been Ellen Marsh, and she had been a girl who ran barefoot in the stream and had a laugh that could be heard across the green. The old women remembered her—the ones who were left—and they said, “Ellen Marsh? Oh, she went up to the great house, and she never came back.” And they said it with a certain satisfaction, as though it were a story with a moral, and the moral was that you could not go up to the great house and remain yourself. But one of them, old Mrs. Pettigrew, who was ninety and sat by the window all day, said, “She came back once. She came back when her mother died, and she stood in the churchyard in her black, and she looked at the grave, and she said nothing. And I said to her, ‘Ellen, you’ve done well for yourself,’ and she said, ‘Yes, Mrs. Pettigrew, I’ve done well.’ And she looked at me, and her eyes were the eyes of the girl who ran in the stream, but they were not the same eyes, and I thought, ‘She has not done well, she has done something else, and she does not know what it is.’”

And so the house remembered a shape, and the family remembered a surface, and the servants remembered a tyrant, and the village remembered a girl who went away. And none of them remembered the woman herself, who had stood at the window of her little room at the top of the house, in the evenings, and looked out over the park to the village, and had seen, perhaps, the crooked chimney, and had thought—what? No one knew. The keys hung on their hook in the still-room, and the house went on, and the quiet, which had been hers, went loose and drifted through the rooms, and the August light came in at the windows, and the dust settled, and no one, no one at all, could have said what she had thought.
