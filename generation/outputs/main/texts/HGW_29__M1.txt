Miss Eleanor Vance was a woman of forty-two who had achieved, in her twenty years of teaching, a state of perfect and irrevocable compromise. She was head governess to the daughters of Sir Arthur Finchley, a man whose wealth was derived from something in shipping and whose wife’s family had been in something else entirely. In their house in Wimbledon, Miss Vance taught French, German, history, deportment, and the piano to three young ladies who were being prepared, with the grim efficiency of a military campaign, for a life of marriage and social prominence. She was, by all accounts, excellent at her work. She was respected, she was comfortably housed, and she was, in the peculiar and enclosed world she inhabited, almost a person of consequence.

The decision, when it came, did not arrive with thunderclaps or divine revelation. It was a matter of slow, geological pressure. It began with the girl, Clara, a shopkeeper’s daughter from the high street, whom Miss Vance had taken on for a few hours a week in secret, teaching her to read and write and cast accounts. The girl’s mind was not a receptacle to be filled with the polite inanities of the upper classes, but a sharp, hungry instrument. She devoured the history of the Tudors and asked questions about the price of grain that Miss Vance, for all her education, could not answer. It was this girl, and a dozen others like her she saw in the streets of Southgate—bright, capable, with futures that would be squandered behind a counter—that began to feel like a personal indictment.

One Tuesday evening, after a particularly tedious hour spent with the eldest Finchley girl on the subject of embroidery, Miss Vance sat down at her desk and drew up a prospectus. She called it ‘The Southgate Academy for the Daughters of Commerce’. She had saved a small sum from her salary, enough to rent a large, rather draughty house on the edge of the common, with a garden and a schoolroom that smelled of beeswax and chalk. She wrote to Sir Arthur and Lady Finchley, resigning her post with a week’s notice.

The scandal was immediate and, to Miss Vance’s quiet satisfaction, entirely predictable. Lady Finchley’s letter was a masterpiece of wounded astonishment. “We had believed you to be a woman of refinement and principle,” she wrote, “not one to consort with… the daughters of shopkeepers.” The word ‘shopkeepers’ was underlined twice, as if it were a disease. Her friends, the wives of other landed men in the district, crossed the street to avoid her. She had not just resigned a position; she had insulted a class.

The shopkeepers of Southgate High Street, for their part, were no more welcoming. Miss Vance went amongst them herself to post her prospectus, a document printed on good paper and written in her most elegant hand. She visited the grocer, the butcher, and the haberdasher.

The grocer, a Mr. Pumble, a man with flour on his waistcoat and a deeply suspicious squint, read the prospectus and then looked at her as if she were trying to sell him the moon.

“An ‘Academy for Commerce’?” he said, the words tasting sour in his mouth. “For my Martha? What’s wrong with the national school? She learns her letters and her numbers there, and that’s all a girl needs to help with the books.”

“But she will learn so much more, Mr. Pumble,” Miss Vance insisted, her voice earnest. “History, geography, the sciences…”

“And what use is that to a girl who’s going to marry a baker’s boy and keep his house?” he retorted, crossing his arms. “Sounds to me like you’re trying to make her forget who she is. Puttin’ on airs. My Martha’s a good girl. She knows her station. I’ll not have her taught to be ashamed of it.”

The butcher was more direct. “It’s charity, is it?” he boomed, waving a cleaver for emphasis. “You’re a lady, miss. Or you were. We know what you’re about. You want to feel all high and mighty, teachin’ us poor folk how to be better than we are. Well, we don’t want it. We’re honest folk, and we’re proud of it.”

Miss Vance left the butcher’s shop with a flush of frustration on her cheeks. She had imagined them as grateful, as eager for the opportunity she was offering. She had not anticipated this fierce, proud suspicion. They saw her not as a liberator, but as a condescending missionary from an alien tribe, come to tell them their gods were false. For a week, not a single application came in.

She sat in the empty schoolroom, the silence broken only by the ticking of the mantel clock, and faced the very real possibility of ruin. She had burned her boats with the Finchleys, and the shore she had landed on was hostile. She was a woman of no class now, belonging neither to the gentry nor to the tradesmen.

It was Clara who saved her. The girl came to the house one evening, her face pale with resolve. “My father won’t let me come, miss,” she said, her voice trembling slightly. “He says it’s pride, and he won’t have it. But… I’ve been saving my pocket money.”

It was an absurdly small amount. Miss Vance looked at the determined, worried face of the girl and felt a sudden, clear anger. It was not anger at the shopkeepers, but at the vast, stupid, invisible barrier of convention that stood between them. She would not be defeated by it.

The next morning, she did not go to the high street. She went to the offices of the *Southgate & Enfield Mercury*, a small local paper run by a man named Mr. Grimes, who had a cigarette perpetually glued to his lip and a cynical eye. She paid for an advertisement, but she wrote the copy herself. It was not a prospectus.

It read: “Wanted: Daughters of Southgate. Not for a finishing school. Not to learn to be ladies. A school to learn how the world works. History, geography, arithmetic, English literature, and the principles of book-keeping. For girls who intend to be useful, to run a business, to manage a household, and to think for themselves. The Southgate Academy, 14 Park Crescent. Fees: Modest. Enquiries: Miss E. Vance.”

It was a direct challenge. It spoke their language. It did not promise to change them, but to equip them. When the paper appeared on Friday, the effect was remarkable. The butcher’s wife, a sensible woman who did the accounts, came to see her. Then the baker’s daughter, who wanted to manage her father’s expanding trade. Within a month, the schoolroom was no longer silent. It was filled with the sound of young voices reading aloud from a history of the British Empire, the scratching of pens, and the earnest, argumentative chatter of girls learning, for the first time, that their world was as worthy of study as any other. Miss Vance stood before them, a woman without a class, and for the first time in twenty years, she felt she was teaching something that truly mattered. The compromise was over. The work had begun.
