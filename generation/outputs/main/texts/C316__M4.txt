The rain had set in with a steady, remorseless persistence that seemed to Mr. Ellery to be a direct comment on the folly of his hopes. For three days it had fallen on the parsonage at Wren’s Hollow, turning the gravel drive to a soup of mud and penning his five daughters indoors with a restlessness that threatened the very foundations of his peace. The cause of this domestic barometer’s descent was the expected arrival of his cousin, Mr. Josiah Thorne, a man of considerable fortune and no immediate heirs, who had written from London to announce his intention of passing a fortnight with his country relations. The letter had been a model of polite ambiguity, but Mrs. Ellery, a woman of tireless ambition for her children, had read a volume in its few lines.

“He means to choose one of them, Mr. Ellery,” she had declared, her voice a blend of reverence and command. “It is as plain as the nose on your face. A man of his age, without a wife or child, does not tour the country parsonages for his health. He is looking for a helpmeet, and he looks to his own blood to provide her.”

The eldest, Prudence, had received this news with a becoming blush and a flurry of needlework. Charity, the second, had begun at once to practise a more subdued and elegant manner of walking. The third, Verity, had spent the morning composing a piece for the pianoforte, while the fourth, Patience, had retreated to the garden to consult the Book of Common Prayer on the subject of humility. Only the youngest, Temperance, aged fifteen and possessed of a spirit entirely at odds with her name, had greeted the intelligence with a snort of derision.

“So we are to be paraded like heifers at a fair,” she had said, looking up from the book she was reading, a volume of Mr. Godwin’s philosophy that she kept hidden beneath a cushion. “And he, the squire, will poke and prod us and finally choose the one with the best teeth. I hope he brings his own butcher.”

“Temperance!” her mother had cried, genuinely shocked. “You will hold your tongue. Mr. Thorne is a gentleman of property and principle. He deserves our respect.”

“Property, certainly,” Temperance had retorted. “Principle, I shall judge for myself.”

And so, when the great man’s carriage at last squelched to a halt before the parsonage door, it was with a sense of impending doom that the Ellery family assembled in the hall. Mr. Josiah Thorne was a man of middling height, with a comfortable girth that spoke of good dinners and a face that was round and ruddy, like a winter apple. His eyes, however, were small and shrewd, and they missed nothing as he surveyed the line of young women presented to him. He was dressed in the very best London tailoring, and a heavy gold watch-chain strained across his waistcoat.

He was polite, if a little distant, to Prudence and Charity, and he listened with an air of patient boredom to Verity’s account of her musical studies. To Patience, who curtsied so low she nearly toppled, he offered a kind word about the weather. Then his gaze fell upon Temperance, who stood at the end of the line, her arms crossed, her chin lifted in defiance.

“And this must be the youngest,” he said, his voice a low rumble. “Miss Temperance, I presume. Your father’s letters have mentioned you.”

“I am surprised he had the time, sir,” she replied, her voice clear and sharp in the hushed hall. “He is usually so occupied with his sermons. Perhaps he was in need of a subject for one on the sins of youth.”

A collective gasp went through her sisters. Mrs. Ellery’s face went pale. Mr. Ellery coughed into his hand. But Mr. Thorne did not frown. A strange, almost imperceptible light flickered in his small eyes.

“I see,” he said slowly. “And do you consider yourself a great sinner, Miss Temperance?”

“Not at all, sir. I am merely a great observer of the sins of others. It is a far more entertaining pastime.”

From that moment, Temperance made it her business to mock him. It was a campaign of guerrilla warfare, waged with a weapon of quiet insolence. At dinner that evening, as Mr. Thorne expounded on the inferiority of country roads, she listened with an expression of profound fascination.

“You are very knowledgeable on the subject of highways, Mr. Thorne,” she said sweetly, when he paused for breath. “Do you write pamphlets on the matter? I am sure the turnpike trusts would be most grateful for your expertise.”

Her sisters stared at their plates. Prudence kicked her under the table. Mr. Thorne merely buttered a piece of bread. “I have not the literary ambition, I fear,” he replied, unperturbed. “My talents, such as they are, lie in the more practical application of capital.”

“Ah,” said Temperance. “You mean you buy the roads and then charge the tolls. How very practical.”

The next afternoon, finding him alone in the drawing-room examining a rather poor watercolour of a local ruin, she engaged him again. He had just declared that he found the English landscape, while pretty, to be somewhat lacking in the grandeur of the Continent.

“Have you travelled much abroad, Mr. Thorne?” she inquired, taking a seat opposite him.

“A little,” he admitted. “Italy. The Alps.”

“How wonderful. I have read that the Italians are a passionate people. Did you find them so? Or did you find their passion, like their roads, to be in need of improvement?”

He looked at her for a long moment, his head cocked to one side. “You are a very impertinent young lady,” he said, but there was no censure in his tone. It was a statement of fact, like observing that the sky was blue.

“I am told it is my chief failing,” she agreed. “My sisters, as you may have noticed, have none. They are perfectly amiable and accomplished. Prudence can embroider a cushion that would make a bishop weep with envy. Charity sings like an angel who has been taking lessons. They are, I am sure, everything a man of property could desire.”

“And you, Miss Temperance? What are you?”

“I am the one who will tell you when you are being a bore,” she said, and then, rising, she swept from the room, leaving him to his watercolour and his thoughts.

Her sisters were horrified. Prudence wept, convinced Temperance had ruined them all. Charity gave her a wide berth, as if her impertinence were a contagious disease. Mrs. Ellery confined her to her room for a day, a punishment Temperance bore with the serenity of a martyr. But Mr. Thorne, to the astonishment of everyone, seemed to seek her out. He asked her opinion on the poetry of Mr. Wordsworth, and she told him it was the meandering of a man who had spent too long looking at clouds. He mentioned his estate in Norfolk, and she asked if the sheep there were more intelligent than the tenants. He laughed, a great, booming sound that startled the pigeons in the eaves.

On the final evening of his visit, the family gathered in the drawing-room. The atmosphere was thick with tension. Prudence and Charity sat like statues, their hands folded in their laps. Mrs. Ellery was a bundle of nervous energy, her eyes darting between her daughters and their guest. Mr. Thorne, however, appeared entirely at his ease. He took a glass of port from Mr. Ellery and stood before the fire.

“My dear Ellery,” he began, his voice silencing the room. “I have had a most agreeable fortnight. The country air has done me a world of good, and your hospitality has been, as always, beyond reproach.”

He paused, and Mrs. Ellery leaned forward, her breath held. Prudence’s cheeks were aflame.

“I came here,” Mr. Thorne continued, his gaze sweeping over the assembled company, “with a certain… proposition in mind. A man in my position, as you know, has a duty to consider the future.” His eyes settled on Temperance, who was watching him with a look of pure, unadulterated contempt. “I had thought to find a wife. A quiet, biddable girl who would manage my household and provide me with an heir.”

He took a sip of his port. “But I find I have a different proposition to make. I am a man of business, and I have come to value honesty above all things. Flattery is a currency that has been debased by too much circulation. In this house, I have found one person who has not sought to flatter me, who has not pretended to be anything other than she is. Her company has been the most stimulating I have enjoyed in years.”

He set his glass down on the mantelpiece. “Miss Temperance,” he said, and for the first time, his voice was entirely serious. “I am not a fool. I see that you have no more desire to be my wife than you have to be the Archbishop of Canterbury. But I am a lonely man, and I am a rich one. I do not ask for your affection. I ask for your sharp tongue and your clear eye. I will make you a settlement that will make you one of the wealthiest women in the county. You will have your own establishment in London, your own carriage, your own books. You will be free to mock the entire world, if you wish, and you will have the means to do it. All I ask in return is that you occasionally direct your wit at me, to keep me from becoming a bore.”

The silence that followed was absolute. Mrs. Ellery looked as if she might faint. Prudence had gone from red to a mortified white. Temperance stared at him, her arms uncrossed, her expression for the first time entirely unguarded. She looked at his round, honest face, at the shrewd, lonely eyes, and for the first time in her fifteen years, she was utterly without a retort.
