The Reverend Hugh Thornton, Vicar of St. Jude’s-in-the-Meadow, was a man of advanced views and an advanced waistcoat. He believed that the Church of England, like a good claret, had been left to breathe for far too long and had consequently acquired a rather dusty taste. He desired to aerate the Gospel, to introduce a note of aesthetic gaiety into the solemnity of the Liturgy, and to prove that one could be a Christian without being dull. It was for this reason that he had written to Lord Arthur Savile, the celebrated author of *The Vase of Tears* and *A Handbook to the Vices*, inviting him to deliver the Sunday sermon.

Lord Savile accepted. He arrived at the Vicarage on the Saturday evening looking like a magnificent orchid that had wandered by mistake into a herbaceous border. He wore a coat of the most exquisite cut, and his tie was a poem in lilac silk.

"My dear Vicar," said Lord Savile, sinking into a Morris chair as if it were a throne, "I am charmed. I have always felt that the pulpit is the only platform left where one may be didactic without being disagreeable. But tell me, is your congregation prepared for the truth?"

"They are prepared for anything, Lord Savile," replied the Vicar, pouring out some sherry. "I have spent three years preparing them. I have preached on the duty of doubt, the sanctity of art, and the spiritual significance of the Japanese print. They are quite ready."

"Excellent," murmured the aesthete. "For the truth, my dear Vicar, is a thing of exquisite complexion. It requires a certain amount of rouge to look its best in the daylight."

The following morning, the church of St. Jude’s was a sight to behold. It was a building of some antiquity, possessing a rood-screen of carved oak and a smell of damp hymnals, but it had never witnessed such a congregation. The pews were packed with the bonnets of Kensington, the beards of Chelsea, and the opera-glasses of Mayfair. Lady Wotton had come down from London in a dress of emerald green velvet that clashed horribly with the stained glass but matched her eyes perfectly. Even the critics, who generally preferred to damn from the safety of the club window, were present, notebooks hidden beneath their prayer books.

The Reverend Hugh Thornton read the Lessons with a distinct tremor of excitement, his voice rising and falling like a cello. Then, Lord Arthur Savile ascended the pulpit. He looked down upon the sea of faces with the calm, pitying gaze of a god watching a rather disappointing play. He did not open a Bible. He did not adjust his cuffs. He simply began to speak.

"We are told," he began, his voice ringing through the nave with the clear, metallic note of a silver bell, "that we are miserable sinners. We are told that our hearts are deceitful and desperately wicked. I have no doubt this is true. Indeed, I hope it is. For to be good is to be in harmony with the commonplace, and to be in harmony with the commonplace is to be dead. The only thing that is worse than being wicked is being dull, and the only thing that is worse than being dull is being virtuous."

A rustle of shock went through the congregation. Mrs. Arbuthnot, a lady who had organized the parish bazaar for twenty years, dropped her glasses. Lord Savile continued, his eyes fixed on the middle distance.

"Consider the lilies of the field," he said. "They toil not, neither do they spin. They are idle, they are useless, they are absolutely, divinely beautiful. But consider the orchid. It is a flower of sin. It is a parasite. It grows upon decay. It is painted in the hues of the harlot and the tiger. It is the most perfect of all created things. Why? Because it has turned its back upon duty. It has chosen to be beautiful rather than to be good. And in doing so, it has touched the hem of the garment of God."

He leaned forward, his face illuminated by the shaft of sunlight that fell through the East Window. "Sin, my brethren—and I use the word not in its moral sense, for morality is merely the prejudice of the majority, but in its aesthetic sense—is the only thing that makes life worth living. It is the rebellion of the soul against the tyranny of the commonplace. The saint is a person who has conquered his passions; the sinner is a person who has refined them. The saint is a solitary; the sinner is a companion of the gods. For what is a sin? It is a mistake. And what is a mistake? It is an experiment. And without experiment, art is impossible, and without art, life is a manuscript without a text."

Lord Savile paused. He looked at the Vicar, who was smiling in a sort of ecstatic agony. He looked at Lady Wotton, who was applauding silently with her fan.

"Do not think," he concluded, "that I ask you to be wicked. Wickedness is so often a form of stupidity. I ask you only to be beautiful. I ask you to commit the sin of living your own lives. I ask you to love the things that are forbidden, simply because they are beautiful. The fruit of the tree of knowledge was bitter, I admit, but the serpent was charming. Let us, therefore, thank the serpent. Let us thank the artist. Let us thank the sinner. For they are the only ones who know the value of a jewel. The good man, like the bad man, is a bore; but the beautiful man is a miracle. Go in peace, and sin magnificently."

Lord Savile descended from the pulpit. For a moment there was a silence so profound that one could hear the dust dancing in the shafts of light. Then, the church exploded.

It was not a religious explosion. It was a social one. The critics scribbled furiously. Lady Wotton rose to her feet and began to clap, her emerald dress shimmering like a peacock’s tail. The choir, not knowing what to do, began the recessional hymn with a vigor that suggested they were fleeing a fire.

The Reverend Hugh Thornton stood at the door, shaking hands as the congregation poured out. They were scandalised, of course. They were outraged. Mrs. Arbuthnot was purple with indignation. The Churchwarden, a man of solid oak and solid principles, declared he would write to the Bishop immediately. But they were also, undeniably, thrilled. They felt as though they had touched something dangerous and expensive.

"I thought it quite dreadful," said a stout gentleman in a bowler hat, "but I suppose he is a genius. Geniuses are always wrong."

"Wrong?" cried Lady Wotton, sweeping past. "My dear sir, he was perfectly right. It is the first time I have understood the Thirty-Nine Articles. I shall come every Sunday if he preaches."

The Vicar, glowing with the reflected light of the aesthete, turned to Lord Savile, who was standing in the shadow of the porch, lighting a cigarette with a gold matchbox.

"Well?" asked the Vicar.

"Well," said Lord Savile, exhaling a cloud of blue smoke that drifted toward the altar. "I think we have saved them, Vicar. I really think we have saved them from the terrible sin of being good."

"But the Bishop," whispered the Vicar. "The Bishop will be furious."

"The Bishop, my dear Vicar," replied Lord Savile, tossing the match away, "is a man who wears gaiters. One cannot expect him to appreciate the beauty of a bare ankle. Let him be furious. Anger is merely a lack of imagination. But the church—look at it. It is full. It is alive. It is beautiful. For the first time in three hundred years, St. Jude’s has been a theatre. And that, I assure you, is the only form of worship that God really enjoys."

He turned up his collar against the autumn chill and walked away down the path, leaving the Vicar to face the Bishop, the congregation, and the strange, intoxicating perfume of a perfectly successful scandal.
