The failure of his examinations had left Arthur Pym with a profound sense of unreality, as though the solid world of his father’s expectations had been replaced by a kind of academic fog. To clear his head, and to escape the disappointed silence of the family home in Reigate, he had taken a post as assistant to Mr. Craddock, a chemist and druggist in the seaside town of Seaford. The work was a descent into a world of powders and tinctures, of glycerine and rhubarb, and the bell above the shop door announced not opportunity, but the endless, petty ailments of the human form. Arthur found it all crushingly dull, until the arrival of Miss Eleanor Vance.

She was a visitor for the August season, lodging with her aunt in one of the handsome new villas along the Esplanade. She came into the shop one Tuesday for a remedy for sunburn, and Arthur, engaged in the tedious business of counting out quinine pills, found his world abruptly rearranged. She was, he decided at once, a creature of a different order. Her voice was not the clipped, hurried speech of the local tradespeople, but a soft, modulated instrument that seemed to make the very air of the shop more refined. Her conversation, a passing remark to her aunt about the particular quality of the light on the sea, struck Arthur as the most profound poetry he had ever heard. He was, in short, lost.

When she returned for a bottle of lavender water, he had prepared his story. He could not be Arthur Pym, failed scholar and dispenser of corn plasters. The fiction came to him with a ready-made plausibility. “A medical student, down from London for a brief holiday,” he told her, dabbing at a phantom smudge on the glass counter. “One must have a change of air, you know. The lectures at St. Bartholomew’s are dreadfully draining.” The name of the hospital fell from his lips with an easy confidence that surprised even himself. Eleanor Vance seemed appropriately impressed. “How fascinating,” she said. “To be concerned with the great mysteries of the human body.”

Their acquaintance, begun over a lie, grew with alarming speed. They met on the promenade, they shared ices at a confectioner’s, and with every meeting, Arthur’s fiction became more elaborate, more necessary. He spoke of anatomy lectures and the rigours of hospital rounds, and Eleanor listened with an earnest sympathy that was both his delight and his torment. He was in love, he knew, and he was living on a foundation of sand. The gulf between the assistant in a chemist’s shop and the niece of a baronet’s widow felt wider than the English Channel, and his imagined career as a medical man was the only bridge he possessed.

The catastrophe arrived on a blustering Friday afternoon in the shape of a tall, broad-shouldered young man who entered the shop with the air of a man accustomed to having doors opened for him. He had the same dark hair and intelligent eyes as Eleanor, but where her expression was one of gentle curiosity, his was one of frank, appraising severity. He strode to the counter and placed a heavy hand upon it.

“I am looking for my sister,” he said, his voice clipped and upper-class. “I understand she has been in the habit of coming here.”

Arthur’s heart performed a sickening lurch. “Miss Vance? I believe she was in yesterday, sir. For some, ah, aspirin.”

The man’s eyes narrowed. “Indeed. I am her brother, Captain Robert Vance. And you, I take it, are the medical student she has mentioned.”

The words hung in the air, thick with the smell of carbolic soap and impending doom. Arthur felt the blood drain from his face. To lie now would be to compound the felony; to tell the truth would be to lose Eleanor forever. He chose a pathetic middle course. “I… I am studying, sir. Yes.”

Captain Vance’s gaze swept over Arthur’s cheap, ill-fitting jacket, the ink-stain on his cuff, the very shelves of the shop behind him. “Studying. A noble profession. Tell me,” he said, leaning forward slightly, his tone becoming that of an examiner, “what is your opinion on the recent work of Lister regarding antisepsis in surgical practice? A fascinating development, is it not?”

Arthur’s mind was a complete blank. Lister? He had a vague notion of a man with a beard, but the details were lost in a fog of panic. “Fascinating, yes,” he stammered. “Quite… revolutionary.”

“Revolutionary indeed,” the Captain said, his voice dangerously soft. “And which hospital did you say you were attached to? St. Bartholomew’s, was it not? A fine institution. I know a man on the surgical staff there. Perhaps you know him? A Mr. Abernathy?”

The shop suddenly felt airless. The ticking of the regulator clock on the wall was the sound of his own execution. Arthur could only shake his head, a mute, terrified admission. Captain Vance straightened up, his face a mask of cold contempt.

“I see,” he said. “Let me be plain, young man. My sister is a sheltered girl, with a romantic imagination. She has been telling me of a clever, promising medical student she has met. I find instead a counter-jumper with a talent for fiction. I do not know what your game is, but it ends now. You will not see her again. You will not write to her. If you do, I shall return here and make a very public and very unpleasant scene. Do we understand one another?”

Arthur could only nod, his throat too tight for speech. The Captain gave him one last, withering look, then turned and strode from the shop, the bell jangling violently in his wake.

Arthur stood frozen for a long time, the silence of the shop pressing in on him. He had built a castle of air and it had been demolished with a single breath. He went to the window and looked out onto the sun-drenched street. He saw Eleanor walking with her brother further down the Esplanade. Even from this distance, he could see the slump of her shoulders, the way she turned her head away from the Captain’s gesturing hand. She was a visitor, a creature of the summer, and the season was now, for him, irrevocably over. He turned back to the counter, picked up a cloth, and began to polish the already gleaming glass, his movements slow and mechanical. The bell above the door might ring again, for sunburn or for toothache, but the great, transformative event of his life had just walked out of it. He was once again merely Arthur Pym, assistant chemist, and the world was exactly as dull and as solid as he had first found it.
