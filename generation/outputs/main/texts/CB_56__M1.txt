The wind came off the Atlantic with a cutting edge, salt and iron, and it seemed to me it had travelled a vast distance to find me out and mark me for a stranger. All day it had worried the rigging of the ships in the Mersey, making a dreary, metallic music that spoke of departure and of the unending sea. My trunk stood by the door of the inn room, a solid, leather-bound fact against which all my arguments and hopes seemed as flimsy as gauze. I was to sail on the morrow.

Miss Thorne sat opposite me, her back straight as a ramrod, her hands folded in her lap. She had not removed her bonnet, though the fire in the grate was hot and the little room close. It was as if she were but a visitor in the anteroom of my life, and would not presume to settle in it. For five days she had been my companion, and for five days a great gulf of unspoken thought had stood between us. She had shown me the sights of Liverpool—the vast, thronged docks, the new library, the grimy, magnificent streets—with the brisk efficiency of a tour guide, her comments dry and factual. She had approved of my lodgings, inspected my sea-chest, and pronounced my preparations adequate. But she had not spoken of the thing itself: my flight from England, my abandonment of the life she had prepared for me.

“Is there nothing more you require, Ann?” she asked at last, her voice as crisp as the winter air. It was the first time she had used my given name since we had left Yorkshire.

“I think not, Miss Thorne. All is in order.”

“In order,” she repeated, and a faint, bitter smile touched her lips. “A strange word to use for a venture so disorderly, so contrary to all sense and propriety.”

I felt the familiar surge of defiance, hot and sharp in my throat. “Is it contrary to sense to seek a wage sufficient to live upon? Is it contrary to propriety to wish for a sphere of my own, however small?”

“A sphere of your own!” she exclaimed, her composure cracking like ice on a pond. “You speak as if you were a man, seeking his fortune. A woman’s sphere is not her own, child. It is given to her. It is made of duty and dependence, and it is rendered holy by her willing acceptance of it. You had a sphere at my school. You had a post, a roof, the respect of the town.”

“I had a pittance,” I said, my voice low but steady. I would not be shouted down. “I had a room in the attic that was cold in winter and stifling in summer. I had the respect of the town, which meant they called me ‘the old maid schoolmistress’s shadow.’ I had duty, which meant I taught the daughters of tradesmen to sew samplers while my own mind starved. You call that a sphere? I call it a cage, and a poorly-built one at that.”

Her eyes, usually so calm and grey, flashed with a fire I had not seen since I was a girl and had dared to contradict her on a point of scripture. “You were provided for! You were safe! That is more than most women can hope for. You cast it all away for what? For the wilds of New Zealand, to be a governess to a farmer’s children in a log hut? You will be more alone there, more dependent, than you ever were with me.”

“And whose fault is that?” I cried, rising to my feet, unable to contain the force of my feeling. The room seemed to shrink around us. “Who taught me to value learning, and then denied me the means to pursue it? Who filled my head with poetry and history, and then told me my highest calling was to mend the stockings of six-year-olds? You made me unfit for the life you wished me to lead! You gave me a hunger for the world and then locked the larder. Was that kindness? Was that love?”

For a moment she was silent, her face pale. The firelight threw her shadow, gaunt and severe, against the wall. “I did what I thought was right,” she said, her voice tight. “I protected you.”

“You protected yourself!” The words were out before I could stop them, harsh and unforgiving. “You were afraid. You had built your life on this one principle: that a woman must be sheltered, must be kept from the storm. My leaving is a condemnation of your whole existence. It proves that your sacrifice was not a noble choice, but a fearful necessity.”

She flinched as if I had struck her. I saw then the great weariness behind her eyes, the lines of care that I had always mistaken for severity. “Sacrifice,” she whispered. “You speak of sacrifice. What do you know of it? I had a mind, once. I had a heart that was not all dry dust and duty. I had a cousin… a man of letters… who asked me to be his wife and go with him to Edinburgh. But my father died, leaving the school to me and my sister to my care. His affairs were in ruin. There was no one else. Love, or duty. That was my choice. I chose duty. I built this cage, as you call it, not for you, but for myself. It was the only shelter I could construct from the pity of the world. And I put you inside it, because I believed it was better to be safe and cold than to be warm and exposed to the mercy of men and fortune.”

The confession hung in the air between us, raw and terrible. The wind outside howled a long, mournful note. All my anger drained away, leaving a cold, aching pity in its place. I saw her not as my gaoler, but as a fellow prisoner, who had only tried to furnish our shared cell as comfortably as she could.

“Oh, Miss Thorne,” I said, my voice breaking.

“Do not pity me,” she said, and her voice regained some of its old command. “I made my choice. I have lived by it. But you… you are making a different one. You are choosing the storm.”

“Because I would rather be wrecked at sea than rot in harbour,” I replied, and the words felt true and right, a final declaration. “I may fail. I may be lonely and poor and regret this day with all my soul. But it will be my failure. My life will be my own.”

She looked at me for a long time, her gaze searching my face. The fire had burned low, and the room was filled with shadows. At last, she rose and came to me. She did not embrace me; that was not her way. But she took my hand in both of hers, and her grip was firm and strong.

“Then go, Ann,” she said, her voice clear and steady. “Go, and be strong. Do not look back. There is nothing for you here but the life you have rejected. Perhaps you are right. Perhaps there is a courage in your flight that I never possessed. May God grant it does not lead you to destruction.”

She held my hand for a moment longer, a silent passing of something from her life to mine—not a blessing, perhaps, but a grim, hard-won understanding. Then she released it, turned, and walked to the door without another word. I watched her go, her straight back disappearing into the gloom of the inn corridor. I was alone with the sound of the wind and the sea, and the knowledge that I was sailing towards a new world, but leaving a part of my own soul behind me in that small, firelit room in Liverpool.
