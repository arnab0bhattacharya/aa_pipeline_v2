The frost had not lifted from the panes of the weaving shed for a fortnight. It grew on the inside now, feathery and white, so that the looms in the morning seemed to stand in a fog of their own making. Mary Ingham scraped a peephole with her thumbnail and looked out at the village: roofs the colour of wet slate, smoke lying flat against the hill, and the road to Halifax hard as a bone. No carts. No coal. The war had eaten the coal, and the blockade had eaten the war’s leavings, and what remained was a cold that got into the wool and stayed there.

They came down the lane at ten o’clock, eleven of them, the way they had agreed. Mary’s husband, Thomas, went first because he had the steadiest voice, and because his cough had quieted enough to let him use it. Behind him came the Roper brothers, who were young and had not yet learned to be afraid in the right way, and old Seth Wainwright, who had woven at the same loom for thirty years and walked as though the loom were still attached to him. The rest were women mostly, with shawls crossed tight over their chests and their hands hidden. They had the look of people who had rehearsed a thing so often in the cold that the words had gone thin.

The manufactory stood at the bottom of the lane where the water ran fast enough not to freeze. It was a long stone building with a chimney that had not smoked properly since autumn, and a counting-house at one end with a good fire behind its window. Mary watched from the shed as the eleven went up the steps and knocked. She watched the door open, and the clerk’s face, and the way the clerk looked past them at the road before he let them in. Then the door shut, and there was nothing to see but the frost.

She knew what was being said. She had heard her brother say it a hundred times at his own table, with his knife and fork laid down and his voice reasonable. The war, he said. The Orders in Council. The blockade that kept the American cotton out and the finished cloth in. A man could not pay wages out of an empty order book, and a man who paid wages he could not afford was a fool who would drag every hand in the village down with him inside a twelvemonth. He said it sorrowfully. He said it as though it cost him something. And perhaps it did, in the counting-house, in the ledger, in the column of figures that would not come right.

At noon the eleven came back up the lane. Thomas was coughing again, bent over, one hand on the wall. Seth Wainwright walked with his jaw set and his eyes on the ground, and one of the Roper brothers was crying without any noise at all, which was worse than noise. They did not stop at the shed. They went on toward the village, and Mary knew they would go to the chapel and sit in the cold there and not speak, and then they would go home to houses where the fire was out.

She finished her warp. She was a good weaver; she had been a good weaver since she was nine, and her brother had given her the loom nearest the stove out of some old habit of kindness that he no longer knew he had. At half past one she put up her shuttle and took her shawl and went across the yard to the house.

It was a good house, built of the same stone as the manufactory, with a parlour that had a carpet and a clock on the mantel and a fire that was banked but not stinted. Her brother was in the counting-house still. His sister kept his house, and she kept it well, and she had a store cupboard that she had stocked in October against the winter, before the worst of the prices, because she had seen winters before and knew what they did.

She stood in the pantry a long moment with her hands at her sides. Then she took down the sack of meal and the small barrel of salt and the six pounds of bacon that hung from the beam, and she cut the bacon into pieces with a knife that needed sharpening, and she wrapped each piece in a cloth. She put the meal into a linen bag and the salt into a stone jar, and she set them all in a basket and covered them with a blanket as though the basket were a child she was carrying to be christened.

The light was going by then, the way it goes early in that country in January, and the frost was coming down again with the dark. She went out by the back door and along the ginnel and up the lane, and she did not look at the manufactory windows. The first house was the Wainwrights’. Seth’s wife opened the door and looked at the basket and then at Mary’s face, and her mouth worked, and she said nothing at all. Mary put the meal and the salt into her hands and went on.

At the Ropers’ there were five children and no fire, and the youngest was wrapped in a coat that had belonged to a man who had died of the fever in the summer. Mary gave them the bacon and a portion of the meal and told them to boil it slow, and the eldest girl asked her where it came from, and Mary said, “From a friend,” which was true enough, and went on.

She went to nine houses before her basket was empty. At the last, a widow named Hannah Croft who had buried her husband in November and had not eaten since Tuesday, Mary sat down on the stool by the cold hearth for a moment because her legs had begun to shake. Hannah Croft looked at her with eyes that had gone very still, the way eyes do when a person has stopped expecting anything and is trying to understand a thing that has arrived instead.

“He’ll not know,” Hannah Croft said. It was not a question.

“He’ll not know,” Mary said.

“And if he finds out?”

Mary thought of the counting-house, and the ledger, and the reasonable voice at the table saying that a man could not pay wages out of an empty order book. She thought of her brother as he had been when they were children, before the mill, when he had carried her over the beck on his back so her shoes would not get wet. She thought that a man could be two things at once, and that the world did not often let you say so.

“Then he finds out,” she said.

She walked home in the dark with the empty basket on her arm. The frost had made the road treacherous and she went slowly, and the stars came out over the hill, hard and small and very many. At the gate of the manufactory she stopped. There was a light in the counting-house window and her brother’s shadow moving behind it, patient, bowed over the ledger, doing the sums again as he did every night, as though the figures might come right if he only looked at them long enough. She stood there until the cold got through her shawl, and then she went in by the back door and hung the basket on its hook and banked the fire and set the kettle on, and by the time he came through to his supper she had her hands folded and her face composed, and she asked him whether the men had come to see him, and he said that they had, and that it had been a hard thing to do, and that he hoped they understood.
