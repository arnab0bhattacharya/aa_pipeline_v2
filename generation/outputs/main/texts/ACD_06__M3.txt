It was upon a raw, dripping afternoon in the latter part of November, 1894, that Mrs. Eleanor Vane called at our lodgings in Kensington. The fog lay thick upon the treeless squares, and the gas-lamps in the street below were already winking through the yellow murk, when our landlady ushered the lady into our sitting-room.

She was a woman of perhaps forty, dressed in the deepest black, with a pale, handsome face bearing the unmistakable stamp of recent and profound affliction. She carried herself, however, with a composure which showed that her grief was of the ordered, disciplined sort, and she wasted no time upon preliminaries.

"You are Mr. Plexan," said she, her eyes travelling over my companion's thin, eager features. "I have been recommended to you by Sir Horace Bray. I am in the midst of an exceedingly painful business, and the police, while civil enough, can do nothing."

Plexan motioned her to the chair upon the other side of the hearth, and drew up his own with the quick, nervous movement which marked his moments of concentrated attention. "Pray tell me everything from the beginning, madam," said he. "Every detail is of importance, even though it may seem trivial to you."

"My late husband, Dr. Caleb Vane, was, as you may perhaps know, a botanist of some reputation," she began. "He was attached to the Kew establishment for fifteen years. A year ago last April, he undertook an extended journey through the southern and eastern counties, collecting specimens for a monograph upon local flora. He was absent for six weeks. Upon his return, he seemed somewhat altered—quiet, and given to long silences; but I thought it merely the fatigue of travel. Then, in the following February, he contracted a severe chill, which settled upon his lungs, and within a fortnight he was dead."

"A sad, but, one would think, a natural sequence of events," remarked Plexan, his fingers tapping softly upon the arm of his chair.

"I should have thought so," answered the widow, "had it not been for the flowers."

She opened the reticule which she carried, and drew forth a small, flat parcel, which she untied and spread upon the table. It contained a dozen or more dried flowers and leaves, neatly pressed between slips of tissue paper.

"Every week since his death," said she, "I have received by post a single pressed flower, wrapped in plain brown paper and bearing no inscription save my address. There is no accompanying letter and no clue to the sender. The postmarks show that each has been dispatched from a different county."

Plexan drew the papers toward him with a sudden tightening of the lips. He examined the first specimen with his lens, turning it over delicately, and then glanced at the outer wrapper. "You have kept the envelopes?"

"They are here." She produced a bundle of cheap, unpretentious envelopes.

Plexan drew a sheet of paper toward him, and for some minutes sat turning over the envelopes, noting the postmarks, and jotting down names upon the paper with swift precision. "Bodmin, Cornwall," he muttered. "Wincanton, Somerset. Arundel, Sussex. Lewes. Canterbury. Dover. Colchester. Norwich. King's Lynn. Boston. Lincoln. York." He suddenly rose and, crossing to the bookshelf, drew down a large ordnance survey map, which he spread upon the central table.

"Let us see what geography can tell us," said he, placing the list of postmarks beside the map. "Ah! There is a sequence here which is unmistakable. You see, Mrs. Vane, these places trace a continuous route, starting in the far west and proceeding up through the southern counties, across East Anglia, and so northward. It is the very route, you say, which your husband took upon his collecting tour?"

"I have compared it with his notebook," said she. "It corresponds exactly."

"And these flowers?" Plexan laid them out in their order of arrival. "Each is a species native to the county from which it was posted. This first, for instance, is the Cornish heath, found only in the western moors. This is the Sussex rampion. And here is the Norfolk mugwort. Whoever sends them has a thorough knowledge of local botany."

"My husband knew it as few men did," said the widow, her voice trembling slightly.

"Exactly. And now, Mrs. Vane, I must ask you a question which may seem impertinent. Was there any matter—financial, domestic, or otherwise—in which Dr. Vane was engaged during that last journey which seemed to weigh upon his mind?"

She hesitated, and the colour mounted to her pale cheeks. "There was one matter," she said at length. "Before his marriage, my husband had been engaged to a Miss Julia Trent, of Wincanton. The engagement was broken off. I had always understood that she died some years ago. But among my husband's papers, after his death, I found a letter—unsigned—suggesting that she was still living, and that they had met during his journey through Somerset."

Plexan's eyes glistened. "A letter unsigned," he repeated. "May I see it?"

"I have it here." She drew a folded sheet from her reticule and handed it to him.

Plexan read it carefully, and I saw his brows knit. "This is written in a hand which I have seen before," said he, slowly. "Watson, you remember the Grinstead forgery case, and the writing of the brother? No? Well, it does not matter. Mrs. Vane, I think I can promise you that before another week has passed, you shall know who sends these flowers, and why. Have you the address of the house in Wincanton where Dr. Vane stayed?"

"It is called Marsh End, the residence of a Mr. Philip Trent."

"Trent!" cried Plexan, with sudden energy. "Then we have the clue. The brother! Of course, of course! It all fits. Now, Mrs. Vane, I must ask you to leave these flowers and envelopes with me. I shall wire to Wincanton tonight, and I think it very probable that I shall go down there tomorrow."

The lady thanked him with an emotion which her quiet bearing could scarcely conceal, and withdrew.

"Well, Watson," said Plexan, when the door had closed behind her, "what do you make of it?"

"It seems a case of a persecuted woman sending reminders of a dead lover."

"Not so, Watson, not so! The flowers are not reminders—they are a confession. Each is posted from a place upon the route, and each is a species native to that place. But why should a woman, in hiding, take the trouble to send such tokens? Because she is tracing a journey—not his outward journey, Watson, but a journey which ended in his death. There is a crime here, and the flowers are the trail."

I confess I was startled. "You think Dr. Vane was murdered?"

"I think that Dr. Vane, upon his journey, discovered something which made his death desirable to someone. The flowers are sent by someone who knows the truth and is trying, in this strange, half-hearted fashion, to draw attention to it. The sender is not the criminal, but the one who fears the criminal—or loves him."

The next morning Plexan received a wire, read it, and thrust it into his pocket with a gesture of satisfaction. "We go to Wincanton by the 10:15, Watson. Bring your pistol. There may be no need for it, but I have a presentiment that this matter will end in a scene."

We reached Wincanton in the early afternoon, and found Marsh End to be a rambling old house standing in extensive grounds. Plexan walked up the drive, knocked at the door, and inquired for Mr. Philip Trent. We were shown into a dim, oak-panelled parlour, where, after a few minutes, we were joined by a tall, gaunt man with a dark beard and restless eyes.

"Mr. Plexan, I presume," said he. "I had your wire. I know why you have come. I sent the flowers."

"Then you will save us a great deal of trouble by telling me the truth," said Plexan.

Trent turned to the window and stood for a moment looking out upon the dripping garden. Then he faced us.

"I sent them," said he, "because my sister, Julia, is alive, and because Dr. Vane, who jilted her years ago, found her here when he came upon his botanical tour. He pleaded with her to go back to him, for he had learned that his wife was not as he had thought. Julia refused. There was a quarrel, and Vane, who had a weak heart, fell dead at her feet. We buried him in the orchard, and I sent word to his wife that he had died of a chill. But Julia's conscience would not let her rest. She would not confess, but she insisted that I send the flowers, one each week, from each place upon the route, as a sign that God knew what had happened. She thought that someone, sometime, would understand."

Plexan stood for a moment in silence. "And the body?" said he.

"It lies where it fell, beneath the apple-tree by the south wall."

"Then I must ask you to accompany me there, Mr. Trent. The law must take its course, though I think no jury will deal hardly with your sister."
