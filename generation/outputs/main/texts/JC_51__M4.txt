Captain Elias Marlow had commanded the *Saraband* for eleven years, and in that time he had come to know the Gulf of Siam as a man knows the lines upon his own palm—the colour of the water off the Cambodian shore at dawn, the smell of the monsoon before it broke, the way the land breeze carried the scent of pepper and mud and distant fires. He had known these things. Now he knew them only as one knows a face in a photograph, flat and unreachable, for the darkness that had begun as a blurring at the edges of his vision some eighteen months before had closed upon him completely, and he steered his ship by the sound of the engines, by the feel of the swell beneath the counter, and by the voice of Mr. Henry Crewe, his chief mate, who read to him the compass and the patent log and the barometer, and who had his own reasons for keeping the old man's secret.

It was a conspiracy of silence, that little coasting steamer, though none aboard would have called it so. The crew—Malay firemen, a Chinese cook, a half-caste bo'sun named Silva who had sailed with Marlow in the old days—went about their duties with a certain carefulness, a certain hush, as though a sick man lay in the cabin. And so he did, in a manner of speaking; for the Captain's eyes, which had once been the terror of incompetent mates from Singapore to Hong Kong, now stared at nothing, pale and wandering, and he had learned to keep them shaded by the peak of his cap, and to turn his head slowly, as though listening, which indeed he was.

Mr. Crewe read the instruments with a clear, deliberate voice, and sometimes he read them true and sometimes he did not. This was the thing that Captain Marlow did not know, could not know, and perhaps in his heart did not wish to know; for Crewe was a young man of thirty, with a smooth face and a manner that suggested a Sunday-school teacher rather than a seaman, and he had conceived the notion—not altogether unreasonably, in the moral climate of those waters—that a man who could no longer see was no longer fit to command, and that the command, by natural succession, should fall to him.

The owners were Messrs. Guthrie & Khoo, of Singapore, a firm of Scotch and Chinese partnership that had grown wealthy on the coasting trade and was not accustomed to asking questions so long as the dividends came in. But it was old Guthrie himself, a dry, suspicious Scot with a memory for figures, who first noticed that the *Saraband*'s coal consumption had risen by a tenth and her passages lengthened by a day, and who wrote a letter to Captain Marlow that was all polite inquiry and veiled threat. The letter was read aloud by Mr. Crewe in the cabin, and the Captain listened with his hands folded on the table and his blind eyes fixed upon the lamp.

"Tell them," he said at last, "that the monsoon has been contrary, and that the old girl is getting tired. Tell them I will bring her in on Tuesday next, if the weather holds."

Mr. Crewe wrote the letter, and did not mention that the course he had laid was a full point to the southward of the proper one, nor that he had been running the ship for three days by a dead reckoning of his own invention, nor that the coal in the bunkers was being consumed at a rate that would leave them short before they raised the lights of Singapore. He had his reasons. There was a woman in Singapore—a widow, it was said, of a Dutch planter—who had promised to marry him when he had a command of his own, and there was also the matter of a debt to a certain Chetti in Market Street, which would be cancelled, so the Chetti had hinted, when Mr. Crewe was master of a vessel.

The *Saraband* steamed northward through the Gulf, and the heat lay upon her like a weight of wool. The sea was flat and pale, the colour of tin, and the sky at evening was streaked with a red that had no comfort in it. Captain Marlow sat in his chair on the bridge, his hands gripping the arms, his head turned to catch the sound of the engines, and Mr. Crewe stood beside him, reading the compass.

"South by west, sir," he said. "The wind's light from the eastward. We should raise Pulo Obi by morning."

The Captain nodded. He had known Pulo Obi in the old days, a green hump of an island with a white beach on its western side and a spring of fresh water where the Dutchmen used to bury their dead. He could see it still, in the darkness behind his eyes, as clearly as he had seen it forty years before, when he was a young mate on a barque out of Dundee, and the world had been full of light.

"Very well, Mr. Crewe," he said. "Call me at four."

But it was not the mate who called him at four. It was Silva, the bo'sun, who came up the ladder with a lantern and stood before him, and who said, in the careful English he had learned in the ports of the world—

"Captain, the mate is drunk. He has been drinking with the engineer since midnight. The ship is not on her course. I have been watching the stars, and I think we are close to the reefs."

Captain Marlow sat very still. The lantern light fell upon his face, and Silva saw the blind eyes and said nothing. There was a long silence, broken only by the throb of the engines and the hiss of the bow-wave.

"Silva," the Captain said at last, "how long have you known?"

"Since we left Bangkok, Captain. The men know. They do not speak."

"And Mr. Crewe?"

"He knows also. He has known since the beginning. He reads you the compass, but he does not steer by it."

The Captain's hands tightened on the arms of his chair. He thought of the letter to Guthrie & Khoo, of the coal consumed, of the passages lengthened, of the smooth-faced young man who had read him the instruments with such a clear, deliberate voice. He thought of the woman in Singapore, and the Chetti in Market Street, and the command that Mr. Crewe had coveted. And he thought, with a bitterness that was like the taste of quinine, of his own pride, which had preferred the darkness to the shame of being set ashore.

"Call all hands," he said. "Bring Mr. Crewe to me."

They came—the firemen, the cook, the Chinese quartermaster, the engineer with his red face and his guilty eyes—and they stood in a half-circle on the bridge, and Mr. Crewe stood among them, swaying slightly, his collar undone, his smooth face flushed and sullen. The Captain rose from his chair and stood before them, and though he could not see them, he knew them all, knew the smell of them and the sound of their breathing, and he spoke to them as he had spoken to crews for forty years.

"Mr. Crewe," he said, "you will go below and stay there until we reach Singapore. Silva will take the bridge. We will steer by the stars, as I have steered before, and if any man among you thinks he can do better, let him speak now."

There was no sound but the engines and the sea. Mr. Crewe opened his mouth as if to speak, and then closed it again, and went below. Silva took the wheel, and the Captain stood beside him, his blind face turned to the sky, his hands feeling the wind, and he called the courses as they came to him out of the darkness—south by west, and then southwest, and then south again—and the *Saraband* crept through the night toward the lights of Singapore, and the men watched him and said nothing, and the dawn came up out of the sea like a promise that no one believed.
