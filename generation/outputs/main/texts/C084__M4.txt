The letter came on a Tuesday, tucked between the coal bill and a circular advertising a patent boot-black, and it was the handwriting that first arrested Alfred Pike. He knew it at once, though he had not seen it for seventeen years, for it was the hand of his father’s old clerk, a Mr. Craddock, whose looping, laborious script had once filled the ledgers of a business that no longer existed. The letter was brief and plainly written, and it said that the writer had lately come upon a man at the station of Marlow Cross, in Warwickshire, who answered to the name of Edmund Pike and who was employed there as a ticket collector, and that this man, when pressed, had admitted to having once lived in London and to having a son. Mr. Craddock concluded by observing that he did not wish to raise false hopes, but that he felt it his duty to communicate the intelligence, and he remained, etc.

Alfred read it three times in the dim light of the basement kitchen, while his aunt’s voice rattled the crockery above stairs. He was twenty-three years old and he had been told, from the age of six, that his father was dead. The story had been repeated so often and with such grim economy that it had acquired the weight of fact: a fever, a hasty burial, a young widow who did not long survive her husband, and a boy left to the care of his father’s sister, Mrs. Hester Pike, who kept a lodging house in a narrow street behind the Gray’s Inn Road. That his mother was dead he had never doubted. That his father was dead he had accepted as one accepts the furniture of a room he has never thought to leave.

But the letter troubled him. He carried it in his waistcoat for a week, and at night, in his small attic, he took it out and read it again by the stub of a candle, and he began to remember things—a voice, a hand lifting him to look out of a window, the smell of tobacco and wet wool—which might have been memories or might have been the inventions of a mind that wanted them. His aunt, when he asked her, said only that his father was dead and that it was a sin to disturb the dead with idle questions, and she set him to blacking the grates with such vigour that the subject was closed.

He told her nothing of his intention. On a Saturday in early October, when the lodgers had all gone out and his aunt was occupied with the weekly accounts, he took his wages from the drawer in the parlour, put on his good coat, and walked to Euston. He had never in his life travelled further than Hampstead.

The train was slow and smelled of sulphur and damp upholstery, and it stopped at every town of any consequence, and Alfred sat with his hands folded on the head of his stick and watched the fields of Middlesex give way to the hedgerows and the low, red-brick villages of the Midlands. He had a picture in his mind of the man he would find: a figure in a peaked cap, stooped perhaps, grey, but with something of the old authority in his face, and he rehearsed a hundred speeches in which he revealed himself, and a hundred more in which he did not, and by the time the train drew into Marlow Cross his mouth was dry and his heart beat so violently that he could hear it above the clatter of the wheels.

The station was small and mean, with a wooden booking office and a single platform, and beyond it a coal yard and the roofs of a town that seemed to have been built entirely of slate and rain. Alfred stood in the shadow of the waiting-room and watched the passengers disperse. Presently a man came out of the booking office carrying a lamp and a bundle of tickets on a board, and took his place at the barrier, and Alfred knew him at once, though he could not have said how. He was smaller than the picture in Alfred’s mind, and thinner, and his face was the colour of a candle that has burned too long, but there was a way he had of holding his head a little to one side, as though listening for something, that Alfred recognised from his own looking-glass.

He did not go forward. He stood where he was and let the passengers file past, and when the platform was empty and the man had turned to go back into his office, Alfred followed him and said, in a voice that did not sound like his own: “I beg your pardon.”

The man turned. His eyes were pale and tired, and there was nothing in them at first but the mild, professional patience of a man who is used to being asked directions. Then something shifted, and he looked at Alfred more closely, and his mouth opened a little, and he put out one hand to the door-frame as though the floor had moved beneath him.

“You had better come inside,” he said at last, and his voice was hoarse and quiet. “There’s a fire.”

The office was no bigger than a closet, with a stove, a stool, a table covered in timetables, and a smell of coal-dust and stale tea. Alfred sat on the stool and the man stood by the stove with his back to him, and for a long moment neither spoke. Then the man said, without turning: “You are Alfred.”

“Yes.”

“I thought you might be. I have thought it before, when a young man came through. But it was never you.” He turned round. His eyes were wet. “I did not know whether you were alive. I was told—they told me you had died of the fever, with your mother. I was told it by your aunt. I did not believe it, at first, but she was very certain, and after a while I suppose I wanted to believe it, because it was easier than the other thing.”

“What other thing?”

“That I had left you.” He sat down heavily on the other stool. “I was not a good man, Alfred. I was not cruel, but I was weak, and I was proud, and I lost everything I had on a speculation that any fool could have told me was a fraud. Your aunt took the house and the lodgers and she took you, and she said I might go to the devil in my own way and not bring you with me. I went. I have been going ever since.”

Alfred looked at him. He had expected anger, or grief, or some great scene of recognition, but what he felt was a strange, flat calm, as though he had been holding his breath for seventeen years and had only now let it out. “She told me you were dead,” he said.

“I know it. I have known it a long time. I could have written. I could have come. But the years make cowards of us all, and after a while I could not bear to be looked at by a son who would have every right to despise me.” He wiped his face with the back of his hand. “You have her look about you. Your mother, I mean. Not your aunt.”

“I am glad of that,” said Alfred, and was surprised to find that he meant it.

They talked until the lamp burned low and the last train had gone, and the man—his father—made tea on the stove and told him about the station, and the town, and the small, narrow life he had made for himself among the timetables and the coal fires. He did not ask for forgiveness, and Alfred did not offer it. But when at last Alfred rose to go, his father said, “Will you come again?” and Alfred said, “Yes,” and found that he meant that too.

He walked back to the station in the dark and stood on the platform where the lamps made yellow circles on the wet stones, and he thought of his aunt’s kitchen and the blacked grates and the letter in his waistcoat, and he knew that he would not tell her. Not yet. Perhaps not ever. But he would come again, on another Saturday, and he would bring tobacco, and perhaps a book, and he would sit in the little office by the stove and listen to the trains go by, and in time, he thought, the two of them might learn what it was to be a father and a son. The last train came in, and he got on it, and the lights of Marlow Cross slid away into the dark.
