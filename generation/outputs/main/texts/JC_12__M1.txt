The light of the single gas-jet, hanging low and heavy with the accumulated soot of years, threw a circle of sickly, yellow radiance upon the workbench, and left the rest of the little shop in a state of profound and sepulchral gloom. Outside, in the narrow throat of Pelham Lane, the November evening had settled into a wet, clinging fog that muffled the world, reducing the clatter of hooves and the cries of the night-watchman to a ghostly, distant murmur. It was a silence that seemed to press in upon the thin glass of the windowpanes, a silence that Mr. Elias Thorne had come to know as the very texture of his existence.

For forty years, he had sat here, a man of small, precise movements and a temperament that had been ground down, like an over-worn pivot, to the essential metal of routine. He dealt in the resurrection of seconds, in the mending of the delicate, patient hearts that beat within the gold and silver shells of the respectable. He knew the fretful anxieties of the widow, the careless haste of the merchant, the inherited guilt of the heir; he knew them by the scratches upon their cases and the dust within their wheels. It was a trade of confidences, of a sort, though never a word of the soul was ever spoken. Only the machine was laid bare.

And then, on that particular evening, the bell above the door had not jangled. The door itself had opened with a slow, oiled stealth that was more alarming than any sudden intrusion. The man who entered was a shadow first, and then a presence. He was of middle height, dressed in a dark overcoat of a good but anonymous cut, the collar turned up against a face that seemed a composite of all the forgettable faces in London. He wore grey gloves. He did not remove his hat.

“I am told,” the voice was low, with a faint, unplaceable accent, the kind one acquires in the colonies or in the counting-houses of foreign ports, “that you are a man of discretion.”

Thorne had looked up from the hairspring he was coaxing into submission, his loupe still pressed to his eye, which gave him the Cyclopean, unblinking look of a creature of the workshop. “I am a watchmaker, sir. My discretion extends to the mainsprings and the escapements. Beyond that, I am a simple man.”

A smile, thin and without warmth, touched the stranger’s lips. “Precisely.” He placed a small, heavy object upon the counter, on the worn patch of leather where customers laid their timepieces. It was not a watch. It was a cylinder of blackened steel, perhaps three inches long and two in diameter, capped at both ends. It had no ornament, no hallmarks of craft. It was a thing of pure, brutal function.

“It requires adjustment,” the man said. “A timing mechanism within. It is… temperamental. The interval is too long. It must be shortened. By half.”

Thorne did not touch it. He knew what it was. In the great, sprawling body of the Empire, he was but a tiny nerve-ending, but he felt the tremors. He read the papers. He heard the whispers of the men who came for their pocket watches, men who spoke of Fenians and dynamiters with a mixture of horror and a strange, vicarious thrill. This small, dense object was the dark heart of those whispers. It was a promise of sudden, indiscriminate oblivion, a fistful of thunder and shrapnel to be delivered to some unknown place, some public square or railway carriage, where men and women and children would be going about the mundane business of living.

“The fee,” the customer continued, as if discussing the repair of a lady’s chatelaine, “will be fifty guineas. In advance.” He drew a thick fold of banknotes from his inner pocket and laid them beside the device. The money was crisp, new, and obscene in its quiet power.

The fifty guineas represented a year of patient labour, a new roof for the shop, a respite from the gnawing anxiety that was his constant companion. But it was not the money that held him silent. It was the sudden, vertiginous sense of his own place in the scheme of things. He, Elias Thorne, who measured his life in the tick-tock of regulated balance wheels, was being asked to impose his order upon this… this engine of chaos. To refine its purpose. To make it more efficient in its work of destruction.

He looked from the money to the device, and then to the man’s gloved hands. He thought of the police station at the end of the lane, a place of noise and crude authority that stood in absolute opposition to his world of quiet precision. To go there would be to plunge himself into that world, to become a witness, a man marked. The police would ask questions. They would not understand. They would see only a tradesman who had touched something foul.

Or he could refuse. But to refuse this man, whose eyes held the flat, patient certainty of a predator, was to invite a visit of another sort. A visit perhaps not to the shop, but to his small rooms above, where he slept amidst the ticking of a hundred clocks. The thought was a cold draft in the still air of the workshop.

“Why me?” Thorne’s own voice sounded thin and reedy in the oppressive quiet.

The man’s gaze did not waver. “Your reputation. You do not talk. You understand the value of a mechanism, whatever its purpose. You are not a moralist. You are a craftsman.”

There was the final, terrible irony. His virtue, his lifelong dedication to the abstract perfection of the machine, was the very quality that damned him. He had trained his soul to be a passive, objective observer of intricate cause and effect. And now, a cause was being laid upon his bench, and he was expected to calculate its effect with dispassionate accuracy.

“I will… consider it,” Thorne murmured, the words tasting of ash.

“The consideration must be concluded by tomorrow evening,” the man said. He turned, and as silently as he had entered, he was gone. The bell did not ring. The door closed with a soft, final click.

And Thorne was left alone with the fifty guineas and the steel cylinder. The gas-jet hissed. The fog pressed against the glass. He looked at the object, at its simple, terrible geometry. It was a timepiece of a different order. It did not measure the passing of the hours, but the erasure of them. It counted down not to an hour of business or an appointment, but to an hour of fire and blood. And he, the master of time, was being asked to set its terrible, final alarm. The silence in the shop was no longer peaceful; it was the silence of a held breath, the silence before a scream.
