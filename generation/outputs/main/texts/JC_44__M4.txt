The first time she came it was raining, a fine, persistent drizzle that seemed to have dissolved the city into a grey watercolour of itself, and she stood in the vestibule of the great building on the Embankment with the water beading on the shoulders of her thin mantle, looking, I remember, not at all like a petitioner. There was no cringing in her, none of that furtive shiftiness of the eye which is the habitual wear of those who come to ask favours of the police. She gave her name—Ellen Corcoran—in a voice that was low but quite steady, and when I asked her business she said, with a directness that was almost offensive in its simplicity, that her brother was held in one of the cells below and that she wished to speak of him.

I had him brought up, of course, in the fullness of time—a sullen, black-browed lad of perhaps two-and-twenty, with the marks of the gutter upon him and the wild eyes of a creature that has been kicked from birth. He was charged, as she must have known, with the possession of certain papers and the distribution of others, of a nature calculated, in the opinion of the Crown, to incite to riot and the destruction of property. It was not a grave matter as such matters went in those days, but it was sufficient to keep him in a cell for a month or two, and to make his name, for a season, a thing to be whispered in the darker haunts of the East End. The sister stood by the door whilst I questioned him, and I observed that she watched me, not him, with a fixed and disconcerting attention, as though she were committing to memory the lines of my face against some future need.

When it was over she thanked me—thanked me, with a grave inclination of the head, as though I had done her a courtesy—and went away into the rain. I confess I thought no more of her for the space of a day.

The next afternoon she was there again. The same mantle, the same composure, the same request. I told her, with what patience I could muster, that nothing had altered, that the law must take its course, that her brother would be brought before the magistrate in due season, and that her importunities could not hasten the matter by so much as an hour. She heard me out in silence, her hands folded before her, and then she said—and I recall the words precisely, for they had a quality that lingered—"I understand, sir. But I shall come again."

And she did. Upon the third day, and the fourth, and upon the fifth, which was a Saturday and a half-holiday, when the clerks were gone and the great building was emptying itself of its daily burden of clerks and constables and anxious citizens. She came always at the same hour, in the same manner, with the same quiet persistence, and each time she asked the same question, and each time I was compelled to give her the same answer. It became, in a curious fashion, a part of the day's routine, and I found myself, towards the hour of four, listening for the sound of the bell, and experiencing a sensation—I will not call it disappointment—a sensation of the incomplete when she did not come.

There was nothing in her appearance to attract. She was plain, plainly dressed, of a pallor that spoke of indoor life and insufficient nourishment, and there was about her a stillness, a self-containment, that was more disquieting than any agitation could have been. She never wept, she never pleaded, she never raised her voice. She simply stood, and asked, and was refused, and went away. And yet I began to feel, in a manner I could not have justified to my superiors, that I was in the presence of something formidable.

It was upon the seventh or eighth visit—I had lost count, and was ashamed to have lost it—that I broke my rule and spoke to her of other things. I asked her of her circumstances, of her life, of how she came to be so alone in the world with this brother of hers. She answered me with the same grave civility, but with a reticence that was like a wall of glass—you could see through it, but you could not pass. She was a seamstress, she said, in a house in Clerkenwell, and her brother had been a printer's devil, and their mother was dead, and their father had been a seaman who had not come home. It was the whole of her history, delivered in a dozen sentences, and yet it left upon me an impression of vast and sombre spaces, of a life lived in the shadow of great and indifferent forces, of a courage that was not the courage of hope but of habit.

"Why do you come?" I asked her at last, and I heard in my own voice a note that was almost that of appeal. "You know that it can avail nothing. You know that I can do nothing. Why do you persist?"

She looked at me for a long moment, and in her eyes there was neither reproach nor supplication, but something that I can only describe as a patient and terrible lucidity.

"Because, sir," she said, "if I did not come, you would forget him. And if you forgot him, he would be nothing—he would be a name in a book, a number, a thing. And he is not a thing. He is my brother. And so long as I come, he is a man who is remembered. And that, sir, is something."

She went away, and I stood at the window watching her cross the wet stones of the Embankment, a small, dark figure diminishing against the grey river and the grey sky, and I felt—I, who had been twenty years in the force, who had seen the worst that the city could do and had long since ceased to be surprised by it—I felt a chill that was not of the weather.

I have thought of her often since. I have thought of her in the long nights when the rain beats upon the windows and the gas-jets flicker and the silence of the great building is broken only by the measured tread of the constable upon his beat. I have thought of the invincible patience of the weak, of the terrible persistence of the obscure, of the slow, inexorable pressure which the forgotten bring to bear upon the rememberers. And I have wondered—I have wondered whether, in the end, it is the law that is the reality, or the woman who stands in the rain and will not go away.
