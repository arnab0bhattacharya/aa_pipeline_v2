The fog in the court off Seven Dials did not merely hang in the air; it lay upon the streets like a damp, grey wool blanket that had been forgotten by a careless giant, smothering the gas lamps until they glowed like sickly, jaundiced eyes in the gloom. It was a place of perpetual twilight, where the shadows of the leaning buildings seemed to stretch out long, black fingers to grasp the ankles of any passerby who dared to hurry. It was here, in a dwelling that smelled perpetually of lye, boiled starch, and despair, that Mrs. Agatha Pickett, a woman whose spine had been bent by years of scrubbing other men’s filth, stood with her arms crossed over her chest, staring at the object of her sudden, terrifying mercy.

He was a boy, or what had once been a boy, for the creature now crouching in the bottom of her massive, iron boiling copper was less a child than a bundle of rags and ribs. His hair was a matted tangle of straw and dirt; his eyes, when he looked up, were large, black pits of hollow fear, resembling the sockets of a bleached skull. He had been found there, sleeping or perhaps dying, as the fog rolled in through the window, and Mrs. Pickett, who had five children of her own to feed on a diet of bread and thin gruel, had done the unthinkable. She had pulled him out of the water, wrapped him in her own shawl, and placed him by the fire, all without a word, driven by a whim of pity so sudden and violent it had knocked the breath out of her.

“Agatha!” cried a voice from the street, shrill and piercing as a needle through silk. It was Mrs. Jellaby, the next-door neighbour, a woman whose face was as thin and angular as a cheese grater, and whose tongue was even sharper. “Agatha, you fool! You old fool! What is that stench? What is that crying? I heard a noise like a rat in the walls. Come out, I say! The Beadle is coming!”

Mrs. Pickett did not move. She looked at the boy, who was now shivering so violently that the teeth in his jaw chattered like a pair of castanets. “Let him come,” she whispered, though her voice trembled like a leaf in a gale.

But the Beadle did not need to be invited. Mr. Silas Crumb, the parish officer of the district, arrived with the inevitability of a tax demand. He was a small man, with a large hat that seemed to dwarf his head, and a face that looked as if it had been compressed in a vice. He stood in the doorway, his boots caked with the mud of the street, and surveyed the room with the dispassionate eye of a man inspecting a warehouse of defective goods.

“Mrs. Pickett,” said Mr. Crumb, his voice dry as old paper. “We are informed that you are harboring a vagrant child. A foundling, likely. An orphan. A nuisance. The law is clear, Mrs. Pickett. The law is a fence, and we are the gardeners who must prune the weeds. This boy belongs to the workhouse. He is a burden on the public purse, and a stain on the moral fabric of this respectable court. I demand you surrender him to my custody immediately, so that he may be returned to his proper place of confinement.”

Mrs. Pickett stepped forward. She was a small woman, but in that moment, she seemed to expand, taking up space with a dignity that was entirely foreign to her usual hunched posture. “That boy,” she said, her voice rising with a sudden, fierce energy, “is my nephew. His name is Thomas. He is my brother’s son. He came to stay with me because his mother is ill, and because my brother is dead, and there is no one else to look after him. He is my blood. He is my responsibility. And if you touch him, Mr. Crumb, if you lay a finger on that boy, I shall scream until the roof falls in, and I shall drag this matter to the magistrate, and I shall see what you can do with a woman who has nothing to lose but her life.”

Mr. Crumb blinked. He looked at the boy, who was now hiding his face in Mrs. Pickett’s shawl. He looked at the five children, who were gathered around the fire, watching with wide, solemn eyes. He looked at Mrs. Jellaby, who had appeared in the doorway behind him, her mouth agape in a silent O of shock.

“A nephew?” Crumb repeated, the word tasting like vinegar on his tongue. “In this court? In this age of plague and poverty? And you have never mentioned a brother? Never mentioned a nephew? You are a widow, Mrs. Pickett. A widow of long standing. And now, suddenly, a nephew appears in the copper?”

“It is a matter of family pride,” Mrs. Pickett lied, her face flushing with the heat of the deception. “My brother was a man of high standing, though he fell on hard times. He did not wish his son to be known as the child of a washerwoman. It was a secret. A sacred secret. And now, it is your business to respect it, or to leave this house.”

For a moment, the silence in the room was absolute. The only sound was the ticking of the clock on the mantelpiece, which seemed to beat louder and louder, like a second heart. Mr. Crumb’s face shifted, a complex dance of suspicion, calculation, and a flicker of something that might have been pity, though it was quickly suppressed. He was a man of procedure, and procedure was his religion. But procedure also had its limits, and the limit was the potential scandal of a public confrontation with a woman who had just threatened to bring down the roof.

“I shall make a note of this,” Crumb said, his voice dropping to a mutter. “I shall note that Mrs. Agatha Pickett claims kinship with the boy. I shall note that the boy is to be kept within the premises. And I shall note that if he is seen in the street, or if he causes a disturbance, I shall return. And next time, Mrs. Pickett, I shall not ask. I shall take.”

With a snap of his fingers, he turned on his heel and marched out, his boots clanking on the stone steps. Mrs. Jellaby followed him, her eyes narrowing as she passed Mrs. Pickett. “You are a fool,” she hissed, the words like spittle. “You will be ruined. You will be in the workhouse with him. I hope the fever takes you all.”

But Mrs. Pickett did not hear her. She was looking at the boy, Thomas, or whatever his name was. He was staring at the fire, his eyes no longer hollow, but filled with a dawning, fragile hope. He reached out a hand, thin as a twig, and touched the edge of the table.

“Thomas,” Mrs. Pickett said softly, kneeling down to his level. “Do you have a name? A real name?”

The boy looked at her, and for the first time, a small, tentative smile broke across his dirty face. “Will,” he whispered.

“Will,” she repeated, and the name hung in the air, warm and solid. “Well, Will. You are safe here. For now, you are safe here.”

Outside, the fog continued to swirl, erasing the boundaries of the world, but inside the small, cramped room, a new boundary had been drawn. It was a fragile line, made of lies and love, but it was there. And in the court off Seven Dials, where the shadows were long and the nights were cold, it was enough.
