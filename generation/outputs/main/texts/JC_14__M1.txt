There was no wind to speak of in the days that followed the last of the trade, only a stagnant, white-hot breathlessness that hung over the water and pressed upon the roofs of the two dwellings. For a month, perhaps two, we spoke of the *Sylph* as one speaks of a season that is late—she was behind time, that was all; the currents of the Straits were tricky, the captain a drinking man, the owner in Macassar a gambler. But as the shadows lengthened and the heat did not break, the absence of the vessel ceased to be a delay and became a condition of our existence. We were marooned by an administrative oversight, left to the mercy of the monsoon and the damp, rotting silence of the island of Sembilan.

I have been a missionary in the Archipelago for twenty years. I know the patience of the forest, the moral weight of the silence that falls between the crash of a falling tree and the screech of a parrot. I know the souls of the Malay people, simple and dark, like the water of the stagnant pools in the mangroves. But it is the white man’s soul in these latitudes that requires the most constant vigilance. It is a fragile vessel, subject to the corrosion of the climate, the isolation, and the proximity of the beast within.

And there was Van der Hoeven. He lived in a house of plank and thatch thirty yards from my own, separated only by a patch of trampled sand and a scraggly hibiscus. He was a trader—of copra, of tortoiseshell, of whatever the islanders scraped from the mud—and he despised me with a silent, heavy contempt that was as tangible as the humidity. He saw in me the interference of a feeble sentimentality; I saw in him the naked, unadorned greed that has turned half the world into a marketplace. We were the only two white men on Sembilan, and we were bound together by the absolute necessity of each other’s company, a necessity which served only to sharpen the edges of our dislike.

When the third month passed without the smoke of a steamer on the horizon, the change in Van der Hoeven was subtle but profound. He did not complain—men of his sort do not complain; they calculate—but his movements became heavier. He spent his evenings on his verandah, staring out at the line where the sky met the sea, his face impassive, his eyes holding the blank, fixed stare of a man looking into a void. The supply of tobacco began to fail. The tinned beef, which we had both scorned as food for dogs, became a delicacy to be rationed with the gravity of a religious sacrament.

"You are keeping a calendar, Reverend?" he asked me one evening. The sun had gone down behind the headland, leaving the sky a bruised purple.

"I mark the days," I said. "It is necessary to keep the order of time."

"Time," he grunted, shifting his bulk in the rattan chair. "Time is money, they say. Here, it is nothing. It is just the heat."

He was a man of little education, his English broken by the guttural inflections of his Dutch ancestry and the pidgin of the ports. Yet, in his silence, there was a kind of brutal philosophy. He did not fear God, nor did he fear the devil; he feared only the failure of a transaction. When the transaction was suspended indefinitely, as it was now, he seemed to drift, untethered from the only reality he understood.

The islanders watched us. They are a superstitious race, easily awed by the white man’s power, and equally quick to scent his weakness. They stopped bringing the daily basket of fish and yams, not out of malice, but out of a vague unease. The two white men, who were supposed to be the masters of the place, were now like castaways, waiting for a ghost ship that did not come. The silence of the jungle, usually filled with the buzzing of insects and the rustle of palms, seemed to encroach upon us, pressing against the walls of our huts.

It was in the fifth month that the fever took hold of Van der Hoeven. It was not the violent, shaking ague that kills a man in three days, but a low, creeping heat that sapped his will and left him lying on his cot, staring at the thatched ceiling. I tended to him. It is my duty to tend to the sick, whatever their creed or their character. I brought him water, I wiped the sweat from his brow, and I sat by his side in the stifling heat of the afternoon.

He resented my presence. I could see it in the way his eyes followed me, dark with a suspicion that my charity was a form of triumph.

"You think I am dying, Reverend?" he rasped one afternoon. The rain was falling outside, a steady, grey curtain that cut us off from the world.

"All men die," I said softly. "It is the one certainty."

"I am not dying," he said with a stubbornness that was almost pathetic. "I am waiting. The boat will come. The *Sylph*."

"The boat will come," I repeated. It was a lie, of course. We both knew it. The monsoon had changed; the company had likely written off the station; the trade had moved elsewhere. We were forgotten.

"You despise me," he said, his voice thin. "You think I am a thief. A robber of the poor."

"I despise the sin," I corrected him gently. "Not the sinner."

"Ah," he breathed, a sound that might have been a laugh or a groan. "The sin. The sin is the hunger. The sin is the need. You preach of a heaven where there is no hunger. I trade in this world where there is nothing else."

He turned his face to the wall. There was a moral grandeur in his stubbornness, a terrible, primitive dignity in his refusal to acknowledge the spiritual reality of his situation. He was dying of the heat and the isolation, and his only thought was of the cargo that had not arrived. It was a horrifying example of the blindness of the materialist, yet I felt a strange, unwanted stirring of respect for the consistency of his delusion.

In the sixth month, the rain ceased. The sun returned, fiercer than before. Van der Hoeven recovered slowly, rising from his cot like a spectre, thinner, his skin yellowed, but his eyes hard once more. We did not speak of his illness. We did not speak of the boat. We lived in a state of suspended animation, two enemies locked in a cell, bound by the rhythm of the rising and falling tide.

One evening, as the darkness fell swiftly and completely, as it does in the tropics, I saw him standing by the water’s edge. He was looking out at the phosphorescence that glimmered in the surf, a ghostly green fire that broke and reformed against the black sand. He stood there for a long time, motionless.

I did not go to him. I sat on my verandah, the Bible open on my knee, though I could not read the words in the gloom. I felt the immense, indifferent weight of the ocean, the terrible patience of the islands, and the smallness of our human quarrels in the face of that vast, unheeding silence. We were two specks of dust on a grain of sand, waiting for a ship that was perhaps at the bottom of the sea, or perhaps had never been sent at all. And in that moment, the distinction between the missionary and the trader, between the soul and the flesh, seemed to dissolve into the darkness, leaving only the shared, solitary fact of our existence.
