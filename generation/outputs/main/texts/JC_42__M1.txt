The house stood a little apart from the rest, on a rise of ground that commanded the grey, unceasing activity of the harbour. It was a house of dark slate and heavy stone, built to withstand the sea-gales that came howling up the Bristol Channel, and it possessed that quality of silent, grim endurance which is so common a feature of the Welsh coast. I had been summoned there by a note, delivered by a grimy urchin from the quay, and the summons had felt less like a request than a command issued from some superior court of the sea itself.

She received me not in a parlour, but in a room that was half library, half chart-room, smelling faintly of leather, salt, and dried lavender. The light from the tall window, which looked out over the town and the water, was the colour of pewter, and it fell upon the face of the woman who sat waiting for me, Mrs. Rhys, the captain’s widow. She was a woman of formidable stillness, her face a mask of respectable grief, her hands folded in the lap of her black dress. She did not rise, but merely inclined her head towards a chair.

I was but a boy then, seventeen years of age, and the sole survivor from the deck of the *Sea Lark* when she went down on the Tuskar Rock in that awful gale of the previous November. The captain, Mr. Rhys, had been a man of the old school, a master mariner of some reputation in the port, and his death had been spoken of with the hushed reverence reserved for those who go down with their ship. I was the living proof of that legend, the boy who had been flung clear and clung to a spar for a day and a night until a passing tramp steamer had picked me up. My survival, it seemed, only heightened the tragic dignity of his end.

“Sit down, Thomas,” she said. Her voice was low and clear, without the soft Welsh lilt of the town, but with the clipped, formal cadence of a woman who had spent her life in the company of men who valued brevity. “You are pale. The memory is a heavy burden, I do not doubt.”

“Yes, ma’am,” I managed to say. My own voice was a stranger’s, thin and reedy in the great quiet of the room.

“I have sent for you because I must know,” she continued, her eyes fixed upon me with an unnerving intensity. “The reports speak of the captain’s heroism. They say he was seen on the bridge to the very last, giving orders, a figure of command against the storm. But words in a newspaper are cold things. They are written by men who were not there. You were there. I wish to hear it from you. How did my husband die?”

The question, so direct, so terrible in its simplicity, seemed to hang in the air between us. It was a physical thing, a weight that settled upon my chest and made the air in my lungs feel thick and insufficient. Outside, a ship’s horn moaned in the harbour, a long, mournful note that was swallowed by the silence of the room. I could see it all again, not as a sequence of events, but as a series of impressions etched upon my memory by terror and the cold. The green-black mountains of water. The shriek of the wind in the rigging, a sound like a soul in torment. The splintering of the deck timbers.

And I could see the captain.

Not the figure of legend, not the stoic commander of the newspaper reports. The man I remembered was a creature of pure, animal panic. When the first great wave had struck us broadside, taking the mainmast with it in a tangle of cordage and torn canvas, he had not been on the bridge. He had been in his cabin, and he had come bursting out onto the tilting deck with his face as white as sea-foam, his eyes wide with a horror that was not of the storm, but of his own helplessness before it.

“He was very brave,” I began, the words tasting of ash in my mouth. It was a lie, a simple, childish lie, and I knew it for what it was. “He… he was at his post.”

She did not seem to hear the falseness in my tone. She leaned forward slightly, a flicker of desperate hope in her eyes. “Tell me. I wish to hear all of it.”

My mind scrabbled for a foothold, for some thread of truth I could weave into this tapestry of deceit. I could tell her that he had given orders to the crew to lash themselves to the wreckage. It was true. But he had screamed those orders from behind the chart-house, where he had crawled for shelter, his voice cracking with hysteria. I could tell her that he had faced the sea. He had, indeed, turned his face towards the final, monstrous wave that broke over the stern. But he had not faced it with courage. He had turned to it with a scream, a thin, lost sound that was instantly torn away by the gale, and he had thrown his arms up over his head as if to ward off a blow.

The truth of his death was a small, sordid, and ugly thing. It was not a grand tragedy. It was a pathetic failure. He had died as a man dies who has been stripped of all his certainties and found wanting. He had died a coward. To tell this woman, who sat there with the rigid dignity of her grief, would be to take a hammer to the edifice of her memory. It would be a greater cruelty than the sea itself. The sea had taken his body; I would be taking his soul.

“It was very fast, ma’am,” I said, my eyes fixed on a brass sextant on a shelf behind her. Its polished surface reflected a distorted, wavering image of the room. “There was no time for… for anything. The last wave… it was a mountain of water. It came over the stern. He was looking at it. He was facing it. And then… he was gone. The ship was gone.”

I fell silent. The lie was complete. I had given her the image she needed: the captain, the master, facing the end with his eyes open, a part of the ship to the last. It was a noble fiction.

For a long moment, she said nothing. The silence stretched, filled only by the distant cry of a gull and the faint, rhythmic ticking of a clock somewhere in the house. I dared to look at her. A single tear traced a slow path down her cheek, but her expression had not changed. It was one of grim, terrible satisfaction. I had given her back her husband, the man she had married, the hero of the port.

“Thank you, Thomas,” she said at last, her voice thick with unshed tears. “That is… that is what I wished to hear.”

She rose and went to the window, turning her back on me, a silhouette against the grey light of the dying day. I knew I was dismissed. I stood up, my legs unsteady, and walked from the room, my footsteps silent on the thick carpet. I had done my duty. I had preserved a reputation, shielded a widow from a truth that would have broken her.

But as I stepped out into the damp, salt-laden air of the street, the lie settled upon me. It was a cold weight in my gut, heavier than any sea-chest. I had buried the captain a second time that day, but this time the grave was in my own memory, and I knew that the ghost of his terror would walk there with me, a silent and unwelcome companion, for all the days of my life. The truth, I understood then, was not a thing to be given or withheld at will. It was a current, and I had turned its course, and now I would be swept along by the consequences of my own making.
