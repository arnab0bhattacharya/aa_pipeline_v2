If any honest chronicler of the human comedy were to set down the exact measure of soot that descended upon the old city of London in the autumn of the year eighteen hundred and forty-two, it would surely be found to exceed, by many thousand ton, the collective virtues of its citizens. The fog, which rolled in from the river like a monstrous, suffocating eel, lay heavy upon the courts and alleys, wrapping the Temple in a wet, grey blanket and giving the gas-lamps the appearance of so many bewildered fireflies lost in a peat-bog. It was in this vaporous gloom, and in the chambers of Messrs. Taper, Grigsby, and Taper, that the junior clerk, Mr. Bartholomew Buckle, sat upon his high stool, casting a long, wan shadow over a mountain of parchment.

Mr. Buckle was a young man of a decidedly anxious disposition, possessing a nose of the sort that seemed expressly designed to sniff out coming trouble, and a pair of watery eyes that perpetually expected the ceiling to fall in. The senior partner, Mr. Grigsby—a gentleman whose face bore a striking resemblance to a sun-dried apple, and whose voice creaked like an un-oiled door—emerged from his sanctum, rubbing his hands together as if he were washing them of all responsibility.

‘Buckle!’ said Mr. Grigsby, in a whisper that was surprisingly loud. ‘Take the evening coach. Go to Essex. Great Maldon Priory. Deliver this packet to Lady Hester Vexington. Do not, under any circumstances, lose it, for it contains—I say no more!—matters of the utmost gravity. Gravity, Buckle! Do you hear?’

‘I hear, sir,’ stammered Buckle, accepting the packet, which was wrapped in a thick, yellowish paper and sealed with a great deal of red wax, as if it were a particularly explosive substance. ‘And what am I to say, sir, if her ladyship should inquire as to the nature—’

‘You are to say nothing!’ interjected Mr. Grigsby, his small eyes widening. ‘Nothing, except that it is from the firm. The less you know, Buckle, the better for your constitution. Go! The coach leaves in an hour!’

Buckle went. The journey to Essex was undertaken in a public conveyance that jolted and pitched over the stones in a manner suggesting the horses were engaged in a private war with the road. By the time he arrived at Great Maldon Priory, his bones felt disjointed, and his hat sat upon his head at an angle that spoke of severe moral defeat. The Priory itself was a vast, pile of brick and shadow, with twin towers that vanished into the evening mist and a front door of such prodigious weight that it seemed to have been constructed to keep out an army.

He knocked, and presently was admitted by a butler, a man of such immense height and extraordinary thinness that he appeared to be a pair of tongs stretched out to reach the ceiling. He looked down at Buckle with an expression of profound, unyielding melancholy.

‘The packet!’ said the butler, in a voice that seemed to rise from his very boots. ‘I will convey it to her ladyship. Wait.’

‘But, my good man,’ Buckle protested, ‘I was strictly enjoined by Mr. Grigsby to place it in the hands of Lady Hester herself!’

The butler’s face did not alter by so much as a twitch. He merely blinked, slowly, like a melancholy owl.

‘Her ladyship,’ he pronounced, ‘is in the Yellow Drawing-room. It is not permitted.’

Buckle, feeling very small and very damp, was ushered into a hall that smelled of beeswax and long-forgotten dinners. After a quarter of an hour, he was conducted upstairs. Lady Hester Vexington sat in a chair by a fire that roared up a chimney vast enough to roast an ox. She was a handsome woman of a certain age, with a face that might have been carved from marble, were it not for a pair of eyes that snapped and sparkled with a restless, almost feverish energy.

‘You are the boy from Taper’s,’ she said, not looking at him.

‘I am the clerk, my lady. Mr. Buckle.’

‘Put it there,’ she said, waving a hand toward a small, inlaid table that stood beside her. The table was already crowded with a bewildering array of objects: a porcelain cupid, a miniature portrait, a silver bell, and several other yellowish packets, all sealed with red wax, all precisely identical to the one Buckle now placed upon the pile.

‘It is delivered, my lady,’ said Buckle. ‘Mr. Grigsby desired me to say—’

‘Quite so,’ she interrupted, her gaze still fixed upon the fire. ‘You will call again. Next week. Same day. Same hour. Good evening.’

‘But, my lady—’ Buckle began, feeling that some explanation was due to the gravity of the situation.

‘Good evening,’ repeated Lady Hester, in a tone that would have frozen the Thames in July.

Buckle found himself outside the great door, standing in the mist, with the distinct impression that he had been a party to some elaborate mystification. He returned to London, reported his success, and Mr. Grigsby, rubbing his hands, said, ‘Excellent, Buckle. You will go again next week. And the week after. And the week after that. Until I tell you to stop.’

And so began a routine that would have driven a less patient man to distraction, but which Buckle, being of a yielding nature, accepted as one of the inscrutable burdens of his calling. Every Friday, he boarded the Essex coach, carrying a fresh packet. Every Friday, he was admitted by the butler, who invariably said, ‘Ah. The packet. Wait.’ Every Friday, he was shown into the Yellow Drawing-room, where Lady Hester, without opening the packet, would direct him to place it on the inlaid table.

The pile of packets grew. It grew into a pyramid, a monument, a veritable cairn of yellowish paper and red wax. As the months passed, Buckle’s curiosity, which had at first been a mere spark, was fanned into a steady, consuming flame. What could possibly be in those packets? Were they legal documents of terrible import? Confessions of hidden crimes? Love-letters from a foreign count?

He watched the pile grow, and he watched Lady Hester. She never opened them. She never referred to them. She merely sat by her fire, her eyes fixed upon the coals, and asked him, in that same abrupt tone, to call again the next week.

The seasons turned. The winter fogs gave way to spring rains, and the spring rains to summer heat. The coach drivers came to know Buckle by name, and the butler, whose name was Mr. Sallows, occasionally unbent so far as to observe that the weather was ‘changeable’ or ‘settled.’

‘Her ladyship is in fine spirits tonight, Mr. Buckle,’ Mr. Sallows remarked one evening, as he conducted the clerk up the winding staircase. ‘She has been singing.’

‘Indeed,’ said Buckle, who had never heard Lady Hester utter a note. ‘And the packets, Mr. Sallows? Do you know what they contain?’

Mr. Sallows paused, one long foot upon the stair. He looked down at Buckle with an expression of gentle rebuke, as if the clerk had asked him to dance a jig.

‘They contain,’ said Mr. Sallows, ‘what her ladyship pleases.’

This was perfectly true, and perfectly unhelpful.

It was on a Friday, a full year to the day since his first visit, that Buckle arrived at the Priory to find the great front door standing open, and a cart loaded with trunks in the courtyard. His heart, which had become accustomed to a dull, administrative rhythm, gave a sudden lurch. Mr. Sallows met him in the hall, looking even more melancholy than usual, which is saying a great deal.

‘Her ladyship is leaving for the Continent,’ said Mr. Sallows. ‘She will not return.’

‘Not return!’ cried Buckle. ‘But the packet! I have it here! Mr. Grigsby said—’

‘Her ladyship will receive you in the Yellow Drawing-room,’ interrupted the butler. ‘For the last time.’

Buckle climbed the stairs with a heavy tread. He found Lady Hester standing by the window, looking out over the grey expanse of the park. She turned as he entered, and for the first time, she smiled. It was a small, tired smile, but it transformed her face entirely.

‘Mr. Buckle,’ she said. ‘You have been very punctual.’

‘I have endeavoured to do my duty, my lady.’

‘You have,’ she agreed. She gestured toward the table, where the pile of packets now reached an astonishing height, a tower of sealed paper that seemed in imminent danger of toppling over. ‘Do you know what is in them?’

Buckle swallowed. ‘I have not the slightest idea, my lady.’

‘They are empty,’ said Lady Hester.

Buckle stared at her, then at the packets, then back at her. ‘Empty, my lady?’

‘Empty. Save for a single sheet of blank paper in each. You see, Mr. Buckle, I had a wager with my brother, Lord Pomeroy. He said I could not endure a year of absolute silence on a certain matter. I said I could. Every week, I was to receive a packet from London, and every week, I was to place it, unopened, upon that table. If, at the end of the year, the pile was complete, I won the wager. If I opened even one, I lost.’

She paused, and her smile deepened.

‘I won, Mr. Buckle. My brother will be furious. And you, by your faithful and uncomplaining attendance, have been my witness.’

Buckle felt the room sway slightly. All those miles, all those jolting coaches, all those Friday afternoons spent in the damp mists of Essex—for blank paper! For a wager!

‘But, my lady,’ he managed to say. ‘The expense! The time! The—’

‘The expense is mine,’ she said, turning back to the window. ‘And the time. You have been paid for your trouble, I trust?’

Mr. Grigsby had, indeed, allowed Buckle a shilling for coach-fare and a bun, which was not, perhaps, a king’s ransom, but which Buckle had accepted as the way of the world.

‘Then we are quits, Mr. Buckle. You will tell Mr. Grigsby that the business is concluded. Good evening.’

Buckle bowed and left the room. As he descended the stairs, he passed Mr. Sallows, who was carrying a leather portmanteau.

‘The packets, Mr. Sallows,’ Buckle could not help asking. ‘What will become of them?’

Mr. Sallows paused, and a faint, almost imperceptible smile flickered across his long face, like a ray of sunlight on a coffin-lid.

‘They will be burnt, Mr. Buckle. Her ladyship has no further use for them. She has won.’
