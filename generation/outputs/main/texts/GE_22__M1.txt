The market-place of Shillingford, on a Tuesday morning, was a microcosm of the county’s life, a shifting tableau of human industry and human desire, where the price of a pound of butter was a matter of some consequence and the turn of a man’s coat could signify his entire standing in the world. It was here that Prudence Arkwright presided over her small but tidy collection of goods—a few dozen eggs, neatly arranged in a straw-lined basket, and three cheeses, their rinds a pale, hard yellow, which she had pressed and turned herself in the cool dairy of her mother’s cottage. Her face, framed by a bonnet of faded cotton, possessed the quiet composure of one who has learned early that life is a serious business, though a faint flush on her cheeks, like the bloom on a ripening plum, hinted at the warm and hopeful heart that beat beneath her simple wool dress.

It was this very composure, this air of self-sufficiency, that had first attracted the notice of Mr. Caleb Brand. Mr. Brand was a grazier of considerable substance, a man whose prosperity was written in the very set of his shoulders and the solid, well-fed look of his countenance. He was not a young man, perhaps forty years of age, with a thoughtful brow and eyes that were accustomed to calculating the weight of a bullock or the yield of a pasture. His courtship, when it began, was as methodical and as certain of its own success as the turning of the seasons. He would approach her stall not with the bold gallantry of a soldier, but with the measured deliberation of a man making a sound investment. “A fine day for your cheeses, Miss Arkwright,” he might say, his voice a low rumble. “They have the look of solid worth. A man knows what he is getting with such goods.” He would buy more than he needed, and the coins he placed in her palm were heavy and warm from his own pocket, a tangible symbol of the comfortable, secure life he could offer. To Prudence’s mother, Mrs. Arkwright, a woman whose own life had been a long struggle against poverty’s chill draughts, Mr. Brand’s attentions were nothing short of providential. “He is a man of substance, Prue,” she would whisper in the evening, her voice sharp with a mother’s anxious love. “He has a house with a slate roof and a byre full of cattle. He would give you a life free from this endless counting of pennies. Do not be a fool, child, and throw away such a chance for a fancy.”

But the heart, as any philosopher must allow, is a territory less amenable to reason and calculation than the fields of a grazier. It has its own wilder geography, its own unaccountable attractions. For Prudence, this other territory was mapped by the face and voice of Tom Welling, a stonemason whose clever hands could coax a block of granite into a semblance of a rose or a lion’s head. Tom was poor; that was the plain fact of the matter, and one which Mrs. Arkwright was fond of stating with grim regularity. He had nothing but his trade, his small cottage thatched with moss, and a mind so quick and lively that it seemed to have more in common with the darting swallows than with the heavy stones he worked. He had courted her not with gifts of substance, but with gifts of a different sort—a sprig of hawthorn blossom, a story of a strange bird he had seen, a verse he had written on a scrap of paper, his spelling a little uncertain but his meaning clear and bright. And one evening, as the dusk gathered in the lane behind the cottage, he had taken her hand and spoken of a future that was not a fortress against want, but a shared venture, a building of something new together. “I cannot offer you a fine house, Prue,” he had said, his voice earnest, “but I can offer you a heart that is all your own, and hands that will never be idle while you have need. Wait for me. Give me two years to make my name, and I will build you a home with my own craft.” And in the warm, unthinking confidence of her affection, she had promised.

Now, the two years felt less like a bridge to a future and more like a chain binding her to a past impulse. Mr. Brand’s suit, once a distant prospect, had become an immediate and pressing reality. He had spoken to her mother, and a tacit understanding hung in the air of the cottage, as heavy as the scent of baking bread. Her mother’s hopes, her own future comfort, the sheer, undeniable logic of the match—all these things pulled her in one direction, like a powerful tide. Yet when she saw Tom Welling in the market, his face smudged with stone dust but his eyes alight with a quick intelligence as he haggled with the ironmonger, she felt a different current, a pull towards a life that was uncertain, perhaps difficult, but which was charged with a vivid and compelling energy. She was caught between two kinds of reality: the solid, dependable reality of Mr. Brand’s prosperity, and the potent, imaginative reality of Tom Welling’s affection. To choose one was to extinguish the other, and the weight of that extinguishment felt immense. She saw the future not as a single path, but as two diverging roads, one broad and well-lit, the other narrow and winding into shadows, and she stood at the crossroads, the egg-basket heavy in her hand, the promise she had made a quiet but insistent voice in her soul. It was a small promise, perhaps, made in the twilight of a girl’s fancy, but it was the foundation of a world she had built in her heart, and to abandon it felt like a betrayal not only of Tom Welling, but of some essential part of her own being. The market bustled on around her, a great, indifferent machine of commerce and gossip, and she was but one small cog within it, yet in her own mind, the entire world seemed to have paused, waiting for her to decide.
