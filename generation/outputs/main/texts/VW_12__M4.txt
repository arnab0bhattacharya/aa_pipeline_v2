The steamer had slipped her moorings at Mundal before the mist had properly lifted, and now the whole party sat in a row along the upper deck, their faces turned to the north, as though the glacier might at any moment heave into view and demand of them some comment. But the fjord gave nothing yet but water the colour of a knife-blade, and the hills rising on either side so steep and so near that the sky was reduced to a pale ribbon overhead, and the sound of the engines came back off the rock in a series of soft concussions, like a heart beating somewhere under the floor.

Mr. Aldworth had taken the chair at the stern, a rug across his knees, his hat pushed back, and his eyes—which were of that washed blue that seems to have had the weather in it—fixed upon the water with an attention that was not quite observation. He was a man of fifty-five who had been, for eleven years now, a widower, and had grown into the habit of sitting apart, so that his solitude had come to seem less a grief than a garment he put on each morning without thinking. Beside him, though not so near as to suggest any design, sat Miss Tregarthen, who was forty and had never married, and who had spent the voyage so far in a state of mild, continuous astonishment at the scenery, the food, the punctuality of the steamers, and her own capacity for enjoying all three.

They had not spoken to one another since Bergen. Now she said, without turning her head: "It does not look like the pictures."

"Nothing does," said Mr. Aldworth.

"No—but I mean that it is so much emptier. One expects—I don't know—a great many waterfalls, I suppose. And perhaps a few more people."

"The people are all at the bottom," he said. "On the farms. You can see the smoke if you look—there, where the hill folds. That little blue thread."

She looked, and could not see it, but said that she could, and then was ashamed of herself, and then, because the silence threatened to become an accusation, added: "My brother has a photograph of the Nærøyfjord pinned above his desk. He says it is the most beautiful thing in Europe. He has never been to Europe."

"Ah," said Mr. Aldworth.

"He is a schoolmaster in Penzance. He collects photographs. He has a whole album of places he will never see, and he shows it to visitors as though he had painted them himself." She laughed, a small dry laugh that was gone almost before it had begun. "I think he is happier than I am, really. To have one's longings so neatly filed."

Mr. Aldworth turned his head at that, and looked at her—really looked, for the first time, at her neat grey coat and her hands folded on the rail and the little lines about her eyes that the wind had been at. "I don't know," he said. "I have been to the places. It is not always an improvement."

She felt that she must not ask, and so asked nothing, and the steamer pushed on, and the mist thinned and the hills drew back a little and showed themselves for what they were—immense, indifferent, their upper slopes scarred with the white streaks of old snow, and here and there a farm set so high and so solitary that it seemed less a dwelling than a thought that someone had once had and abandoned.

At the other end of the deck, the younger members of the party had got themselves into a dispute. Mr. Rennick, who was twenty-four and wrote verse that he did not show to anyone, had declared that Swinburne was the greatest poet of the age; Miss Carew, who was twenty-two and had been brought up on Tennyson and thought it a kind of moral failing to admire anything later, had replied that Swinburne was not a poet at all but a sort of musical instrument that had been left out in the rain. Their voices came down the deck in fragments, blown about by the wind.

"—all sound and no—"

"—at least he has passion, which is more than—"

"—passion! It is merely a noise, like a—"

"Like a what?"

"Like a—" Miss Carew could not think of anything, and so said, "Like a great many things," which was not satisfactory, and they both fell silent, and looked at the glacier, which had now appeared at the head of the fjord, a great grey-white tongue licking down between the black rocks, so still and so remote that it seemed to have nothing to do with them at all, and to be, in fact, a kind of reproach.

"It is very quiet," said Miss Tregarthen.

"Yes."

"I had thought—I don't know what I had thought. That there would be a great roaring. The ice breaking off. Like in the books."

"The books are always wrong," said Mr. Aldworth, "about the noise. It is the silence that is so loud. I remember—" He stopped.

She waited. The steamer's engines slowed, and the water slapped against the hull, and somewhere below a man was laughing at something, and the laugh came up to them thin and strange, as though it had travelled a long way to reach them.

"I remember," he said, "standing at the top of a pass in the Dolomites with my wife. It was the year before she died. We had walked up in the morning and there was nothing to see but cloud, and we sat on a rock and ate our sandwiches and said how foolish we had been. And then the cloud lifted—all at once, the way it does—and there was the whole range, all the peaks, and she said—" He paused again, and she saw his hands tighten on the rug. "She said, 'Well, I shan't say anything now. It would only spoil it.' And we sat there for an hour and never spoke. It was the best hour of my life, I think. And I have never been able to tell anyone about it, because there is nothing to tell."

Miss Tregarthen did not answer at once. She looked at the glacier, which was growing larger now, and at the little black figures of the other passengers who had gone down to the bow and were pointing, and she thought of her brother's album, and of all the places she had never seen, and of the strange fact that one could be forty years old and still be surprised by the shape of a hill.

"I think," she said at last, "that is the only kind of thing worth telling. The other kind—the things one can say—they are never quite—" She made a small gesture with her hand, as though to indicate the inadequacy of hands.

"Yes," he said. "Exactly."

And then Miss Carew's voice came down the deck again, high and triumphant: "—but at least Tennyson never wrote a line that was merely there for the sound of it!"

"Ha!" cried Mr. Rennick. "You have not read him, then!"

And the steamer gave a long shudder and stopped, and the glacier stood before them, immense and pale and utterly silent, and the mist closed over the top of it, and the whole party rose as one, and went to the rail, and looked, and said nothing, or said only "Oh" and "Yes" and "How strange," and Mr. Aldworth and Miss Tregarthen rose with the others, and stood side by side at the rail, and did not look at one another, and did not speak, and watched the ice.
