It was a truth universally acknowledged in the family of the Vane-Hunters—though acknowledged with a variety of countenances, according to the temper of each member—that Miss Honoria Vane-Hunter possessed a mind of uncommon solidity, and that this solidity, being lodged in a frame of corresponding plainness, was a dispensation of Providence not to be quarrelled with. Her guardian, Sir Marmaduke Vane-Hunter, a gentleman of ample person and ample prejudices, was wont to observe at his own table, with a laugh that shook the pendent crystals of the epergne, that Nature had given the girl a head for figures and a face for a fireplace, and that she might thank her stars she was not called upon to make a figure in the marriage market. The jest was received with the applause it merited by the gentlemen of the neighbourhood, who, having dined, were disposed to find all things excellent; but it may be doubted whether the consciousness of a fine understanding is a compensation for the absence of a fine complexion, and whether the praise of sagacity is not sometimes a bitter morsel when administered by those who have never known the want of admiration.

Honoria, for her part, had early discovered that the world's opinion of her person was a fact to be reckoned with, like the weather or the price of consols, and not a verdict to be appealed against. She had therefore applied herself to the study of those sciences which, while they demand no beauty in the student, exact a devotion that is its own reward. The bones of the human frame, the circulation of the blood, the mysterious chemistry of the tissues—these had for her a fascination which the quadrille and the water-colour sketch had never possessed; and when her cousin, Mr. Aubrey Vane-Hunter, returned from his travels with a portfolio of Alpine sketches and a mind stored with the gossip of Continental watering-places, she had listened to his descriptions of the medical schools of Paris and Vienna with an interest that quite outshone her polite attention to his anecdotes of the Casino.

It was this interest, perhaps, that first drew Aubrey's notice to his cousin. He was a young man of pleasing exterior and facile sympathies, who found it agreeable to be admired, and who had a genuine, if somewhat indolent, kindness of heart. He had returned to England with no very definite purpose beyond that of inheriting the family estate in the fullness of time, and of amusing himself in the interval with such pursuits as a gentleman of fortune might follow without derogation. The spectacle of Honoria poring over a treatise on anatomy, her brow contracted and her lips moving as she mastered the nomenclature of the muscles, struck him at first as a whimsicality—a harmless eccentricity which a little ridicule might cure. But as he observed her more closely, he became aware of a certain grandeur in her absorption, a self-forgetfulness which contrasted strangely with the flutter and pretence of the young ladies who adorned the county balls. Her face, when she was interested, lost its somewhat wooden composure and became animated with a light that was not beauty, but something better worth looking at.

"You are very profound to-night, Honoria," he said to her one evening at the Abbey, when the elders were at whist and the young people had dispersed to their several amusements. "I believe you would rather dissect a frog than dance a polka."

"I would rather understand the mechanism of the heart," she answered, without looking up from her book, "than learn a thousand polkas. But I am not so ungallant as to expect you to share my preference."

"And if I were to say that I did share it?" he returned, seating himself on the arm of the sofa with an air of easy intimacy. "What if I were to say that I admired your—your perseverance, and that I wished to be of use to you?"

She raised her eyes then, and regarded him with a steadiness that was a little disconcerting. "In what way could you be of use to me, Aubrey?"

"By marrying you," he said, with a laugh that was meant to be frank and brotherly. "You want to study medicine—a shocking ambition for a woman, but I am not a martinet. Marry me, and you shall have your books and your lectures, and I will keep the world at bay. I have a house in town; you shall have a room for your studies, and I will not interfere. Only you must promise to dine with me now and then, and to tell me what you are learning, that I may not be quite a dunce."

It was a proposal so entirely in accordance with the easy good-nature of the man that Honoria could not but recognise its generosity. He was offering her the one thing she most desired—freedom to pursue her vocation—and he was offering it with a kindness that was not wholly selfish. Yet she hesitated, for she was a woman who had learned to look below the surface of motives, and she discerned in his offer a subtle alloy. He wished to be her benefactor; he wished to have the credit of her emancipation; he wished, perhaps, to feel that she owed him a gratitude which would bind her to him more effectually than any passion. It was not that he was insincere—he was too indolent for insincerity—but that his generosity was of a kind that exacts a perpetual acknowledgment, and Honoria had a proud spirit that revolted from the thought of living on sufferance.

"You are very good," she said, after a pause. "But I think you do not quite understand what you offer. If I marry you, I must be your wife, not your pensioner. I must have my own will in my own affairs, or I am no better than a child playing at independence."

"But, my dear Honoria," he remonstrated, "you cannot expect me to give up all my—my authority. A man must be master in his own house."

"Then I will not enter your house," she said, quietly. "I would rather earn my bread as a nurse, or a—a something, than be a queen who is not allowed to govern."

The conversation was interrupted by the entrance of Sir Marmaduke, who had finished his rubber and was in high good humour. He had heard, perhaps, a rumour of Aubrey's intention, for he came up to them rubbing his hands.

"Well, well," he said, "what is this I hear? Aubrey, you rogue, you are not going to rob me of my daughter?"

"She is not my daughter, Sir Marmaduke," said Aubrey, with a conscious laugh.

"Pooh! pooh! cousin, niece, it's all one. But I tell you what, Honoria, if you are going to marry, you must give up this nonsense about medicine. It is not becoming in a woman. I have said it before, and I say it again. The world is not ripe for it."

"The world is never ripe for anything until someone has the courage to plant it," she answered, with a spirit that made her plain face almost handsome.

Sir Marmaduke stared, and then laughed. "You are a wilful wench," he said. "But you will come to your senses. Aubrey, my boy, you must tame her. A husband is the best schoolmaster for a woman."

It was perhaps this speech, more than any other, that determined Honoria. She saw herself, in the imagination of her guardian, as a creature to be tamed—a wild bird to be caged and fed and taught to sing the songs he approved. She looked at Aubrey, who stood smiling and embarrassed, and she read in his face the reflection of her guardian's thought. He would be kind to her, but he would be her master; he would let her study, but he would expect her to be grateful; and gratitude, when it is demanded as a right, is a galling burden.

"I thank you both," she said, rising and gathering her books. "But I will not be tamed, and I will not be grateful. I will be a physician, if I can, and if I cannot, I will be a nurse, or a—a something. But I will not be a wife on sufferance."

She left the room with a dignity that was more impressive than beauty, and the two men looked at each other in silence. Sir Marmaduke was the first to speak.

"She will come to her senses," he said, but his voice lacked conviction.

Aubrey said nothing. He was conscious of a vague sense of loss—of having offered a gift that had been refused, not because it was worthless, but because it was not the gift he had meant to give. He had thought to be generous, and he had been only patronising; he had thought to set her free, and he had only offered her a larger cage. The reflection was not agreeable, and he dismissed it as soon as possible. But it returned to him at intervals, as such reflections will, and when he next saw Honoria, poring over her book in a corner of the drawing-room, he did not laugh at her, but watched her with a curiosity that was not far removed from respect.

For her part, Honoria had no leisure for regret. She had made her choice, and she would abide by it. She had heard of a hospital in London where women were admitted to the wards, and she had written to the matron, and she had received an answer that was not wholly discouraging. It was a small beginning, but it was her own; and as she folded the letter and placed it in her pocket, she felt a satisfaction that no proposal of marriage could have given her. The world was not ripe for her, perhaps, but she was ripe for the world; and she would wait, and work, and in the end, she doubted not, she would find her place.

The fire burned low in the grate, and the candles guttered, but Honoria sat on, reading her letter and planning her future, with a patience that was not the patience of resignation, but of hope.
