King Aldric of Velanthos had not smiled in seventeen years. The physicians had examined his facial muscles, the philosophers had debated the nature of his melancholy, and the astrologers had charted his stars twice over, but no one could name the precise day his joy had left him. Some said it was when his wife died. Others said it was when his son went to war. A few whispered it was when he looked out over his kingdom and saw the gardens growing without anyone to share them with.

Velanthos was a kingdom built on water and bloom. Its capital, Atherel, spilled across a network of hills and valleys, with stone aqueducts feeding a thousand fountains. Gardens terraced down from the palace walls, their hedges shaped into animals and arches, their flowerbeds shifting color with the seasons. The air smelled of jasmine, citrus, and wet stone. In the evenings, the fountains caught the light and threw it back in fragments, and the city looked like a place built from broken rainbows.

But Aldric did not see the rainbows. He sat on his throne, which was carved from pale green marble veined with gold, and he held his face the way a man holds a door shut against a wind. His jaw was set. His eyes were flat. His mouth existed only to issue decrees and to take in the small, measured portions of food his physician required.

The court had long since stopped trying to amuse him. Then, six months ago, Aldric himself had made the proclamation. He stood before his court, his voice flat as a closed book, and said: "I will give half my kingdom to anyone who makes me smile."

The proclamation traveled. It traveled by merchant ship and by messenger on horseback. It traveled by word of mouth through market squares and by ink on parchment nailed to tavern walls. Jesters came from the coastal kingdoms, their bells jingling, their faces painted in red and white. They juggled, they tumbled, they told jokes that made the courtiers roar. Aldric watched them the way a man watches rain from a window.

Poets came. They recited epics and satirical verses. One poet from the southern isles wrote a piece about a duck who believed he was a king, and the court laughed so hard that two lords had to be carried out for lack of breath. Aldric's face did not move.

Philosophers came and argued that laughter was the soul's recognition of its own absurdity. They constructed elaborate logical paradoxes designed to crack his composure. One philosopher, a thin woman from the mountain university of Karath-Vel, presented a proof that existence was inherently funny. Aldric listened for forty minutes and then said, "Next."

A musician came with an instrument no one had seen before, a kind of glass harp that produced tones like water laughing. The sound was beautiful. The court wept. Aldric did not smile.

A man brought a cat that could walk on its front legs while wearing a tiny hat. A woman told a story about her grandmother outwitting a tax collector. A child did a puppet show. None of them moved the king's face by so much as a twitch.

The court grew bored of the spectacle. The petitioners grew desperate. Some tried tricks, some tried sincerity. A jester from the north wept openly in the throne room, which was against all custom, and begged the king to smile so that the jester could go home. Aldric looked at him with something that might have been pity and said, "You may go home."

By the time autumn turned the gardens amber and russet, the stream of petitioners had thinned. The king sat alone on his throne most days, and the court went about its business, and the fountains played, and the gardeners trimmed the hedges, and life in Velanthos continued without the king's joy.

In the kitchens beneath the palace, the work continued regardless of kings and their moods. The kitchens were a world unto themselves, steam-filled and flour-dusted, presided over by a head cook named Madrene, who was built like a wine barrel and had a voice that could be heard three corridors away. She commanded a staff of twelve, and she ran her domain with absolute authority. Tarts did not leave her kitchen tilted on their trays. Sauces did not leave her stoves unsalted. And kitchen maids did not leave her sight without knowing exactly what they were supposed to be doing.

Among her staff was a girl named Lissen. She was seventeen, small, with dark hair she kept tied back in a cloth, and she had worked in the kitchens since she was eleven. She was not the cleverest of Madrene's staff, nor the quickest, but she was steady. She did not drop things. Madrene trusted her with the delicate work, the pastry shells and the sugar flowers, because Lissen had hands that understood fragility.

Lissen had never been to the throne room. She had never seen the king. She knew his face only from the coins she sometimes held, the stamped profile that looked, she thought, like a man who had bitten into something sour and was too proud to admit it. She had heard talk of the proclamation, because everyone had, but she had paid it little attention. Her world was flour and butter and the timing of ovens. Kings and their moods belonged to a different country.

On the day it happened, Madrene had prepared a tray of citrus tarts for the king's afternoon meal. The tarts were small, each one a delicate construction of pastry and curd, topped with a spiral of candied peel. They were Madrene's pride, and she did not trust just anyone to carry them. But her usual bearer, a boy named Tamm, had burned his hand on a soup pot that morning, and Madrene was short-staffed because two of her assistants had sneaked off to watch a juggler perform in the east garden.

"You," Madrene said, pointing at Lissen with a wooden spoon. "Take the tarts. Straight to the throne room. Do not stop. Do not look at anyone. Do not speak to anyone. Set the tray on the side table and leave."

Lissen nodded. She took the tray. It was heavy, ceramic, laid with a white cloth, and the tarts sat on it in neat rows, their candied peels gleaming under the kitchen lamplight like tiny golden spirals.

She walked. Up the servants' stair, through the corridor that led past the fountain gallery, where the sound of water followed her like whispered commentary. Through the antechamber, where a guard looked at her, looked at the tray, and waved her through. Into the throne room.

The throne room was larger than any room Lissen had ever been in. Its ceiling was painted with clouds and birds, and its walls were lined with tapestries showing the gardens of Velanthos in their full glory. Fountains played in alcoves along the walls, their water catching the light from high windows. The throne itself sat at the far end, a pale green shape against a backdrop of climbing ivy.

And on the throne sat King Aldric.

He was alone. The court had not yet assembled for the afternoon session. He sat in his green marble chair, and his face was the face on the coins, except worse, because it was real. It was a face that had forgotten what it was for. He stared at nothing, his hands on the armrests, his crown slightly askew, and he looked like a man who had been sitting there for so long he had become part of the furniture.

Lissen walked toward the side table. She walked carefully, the way Madrene had taught her, one foot placed deliberately before the other, the tray balanced on her palms. She was halfway across the room when her foot found the edge of a loose flagstone.

It was a small thing. A tiny catch, a shift of weight. But the tray tilted, and the tarts, those perfect golden-spiraled constructions, slid. Lissen tried to correct, overcorrected, and the tray went sideways in her hands. She grabbed for it, caught the cloth, and pulled the whole thing off balance.

The tarts hit the floor. Every one of them. They did not land neatly. They burst. Citrus curd splattered across the flagstones in bright yellow streaks. Pastry shattered. Candied peel spiraled across the floor like tiny golden commas. The tray clattered and spun and came to rest upside down. The white cloth settled over the wreckage like a surrender flag.

Lissen stood in the middle of the destruction. She looked down at it. She looked up at the king.

Aldric was looking at her. His face was, as always, perfectly still. Lissen felt the blood leave her face and then return in a hot rush. She opened her mouth. Nothing came out. She looked down at the ruined tarts, at the bright curd pooling between the flagstones, at the candied peel stuck to the hem of her skirt. She looked back at the king.

And then she did something she could not later explain. She laughed.

It was not a polite laugh. It was not a courtier's laugh. It was a sudden, helpless, snorting sound that came from somewhere below her ribs, and it was immediately followed by a clap of her hand over her mouth and a look of absolute horror. Her eyes went wide. Her shoulders hunched. She stood there, dripping with citrus curd, and she was laughing, and she could not stop.

King Aldric looked at the kitchen maid. He looked at the tarts, scattered across his floor like golden shrapnel. He looked at the cloth, draped over the wreckage. He looked at the girl, her hand over her mouth, her eyes wide with terror and mirth, her apron stained yellow, her hair coming loose from its cloth.

Something moved in his face. It was small at first, a flicker at the corner of his mouth, a shift in the flatness of his expression. Then it spread. His jaw loosened. His eyes changed. And then, for the first time in seventeen years, King Aldric of Velanthos smiled.

It was not a grand smile. It was not the smile of a man who has won a war or seen a child born. It was the smile of a man who has just watched a girl drop a tray of tarts and then laugh at herself in the middle of a throne room. It was small, and bewildered, and real.

Lissen saw it. She stopped laughing. She stared.

"You're smiling," she said.

"I suppose I am," said the king.

"I dropped your tarts."

"I can see that."

"I'm going to be in so much trouble."

The king's smile widened, just slightly. He looked at the mess on his floor, the bright curd and the golden peel, and he thought of all the jesters and poets and philosophers who had tried so hard, and he thought of this girl who had not tried at all, who had simply been human and ridiculous and alive in his throne room, and the smile stayed.

"What is your name?" he asked.

"Lissen," she said. "I work in the kitchens."

"Lissen," said the king. "I believe I owe you half my kingdom."

Lissen stared at him. Then she stared at the tarts. Then she stared at him again.

"Could I just have the tarts back?" she said. "Madrene is going to kill me."

The king laughed. It was a rough sound, unpracticed, like a door opening after years of disuse. But it was a laugh, and it echoed off the painted ceiling and the tapestried walls and the playing fountains, and somewhere below, in the steam and the flour, Madrene heard something she had not heard in seventeen years and stopped, her wooden spoon suspended in the air, and listened.
