It was a truth not universally acknowledged, but held with great firmness by Miss Charlotte Harcourt, that her elder sister, Eleanor, possessed a mind of the most admirable serenity, and a heart to which the passions were perfect strangers. Where other young women of their acquaintance were subject to the violent agitations of love and disappointment, Eleanor moved through the world with a placid grace that Charlotte, in her more charitable moods, considered a mark of superior good sense, and in her less so, a certain deficiency of soul. To see Eleanor was to see a creature of reason and propriety; her affections were neatly ordered, extending in due measure to her family, her friends, and her duties, but never, Charlotte was convinced, in a manner that could disturb her equanimity or compromise her judgement.

It was this settled conviction that rendered the discovery so entirely astonishing. The event occurred on a wet Tuesday at Haverton Lodge, when the dreariness of the Devonshire rain had confined the sisters to the house. Charlotte, in search of a stray volume of Cowper, had ventured into Eleanor’s bedchamber, a place she seldom entered. Finding the book not upon the mantelpiece, she had, with a slight pang of impropriety, opened the drawer of Eleanor’s writing-desk. There, beneath a folded set of handkerchiefs, lay not the expected stationery, but a small packet of letters, bound with a simple blue ribbon.

Her first sensation was one of purest shock, as if she had lifted a stone in the garden and found a nest of unfamiliar, delicate creatures beneath it. The hand on the direction was not Eleanor’s own, but a fine, masculine script, addressed to her at a London post office. With a trembling hand, Charlotte untied the ribbon. The paper was of good quality, and the ink, though faded in places, spoke of a correspondence of some duration. She read the first few lines, and a warmth, wholly unaccountable, rose to her cheeks. This was not the language of friendship or of casual acquaintance. It was the language of deep, ardent, and confessed attachment.

The writer, who signed himself only as ‘J.M.’, spoke of Eleanor’s ‘unrivalled understanding’ and her ‘sweetness of temper which soothes every care’. He wrote of a shared walk by the river Exe, of a conversation held in the library at some assembly, of a glance across a crowded room that had ‘fixed his destiny’. Charlotte read on, her initial astonishment giving way to a profound and bewildering sense of trespass. Each letter was a window into a world she had never suspected, a world in which her sister was not a placid observer, but the very centre of a man’s hopes and anxieties. The final letter in the packet was the most affecting. It spoke of a recent parting, of an understanding that must for the present remain private, and of a hope for a future happiness that depended entirely on Eleanor’s constancy. ‘Though circumstance and the opinions of others may present a formidable obstacle,’ J.M. had written, ‘I place my whole reliance upon the strength of that affection which I know resides in your gentle heart.’

Charlotte closed the drawer as quietly as she had opened it, the letters feeling like a burning coal in her hand before she replaced them. She sat for some time upon the edge of the bed, her mind in a state of complete tumult. The serene, rational Eleanor of her imagination had vanished, replaced by a creature of secret passions and clandestine promises. The thought was at once thrilling and deeply disconcerting. How could Eleanor, who counselled prudence in all things, have embarked upon such a perilous course? And how could she have concealed it so perfectly? Every calm smile, every measured word, now seemed to Charlotte not a sign of good sense, but of the most accomplished duplicity. She felt herself an utter fool, a child who had mistaken the still surface of a deep lake for a shallow pond.

When she descended to the drawing-room, she found Eleanor seated by the fire, engaged in her embroidery, her countenance as composed as ever. She looked up as Charlotte entered and remarked upon the persistence of the rain, her tone one of mild, philosophical resignation.

‘It is a day for reading, I think,’ Eleanor said, her needle pausing for a moment. ‘Have you found your Cowper?’

‘I have,’ Charlotte replied, her voice sounding strange to her own ears. She felt that she was looking at her sister for the first time, searching for some sign of the tumult she now knew to be raging beneath that placid surface. But there was nothing. Eleanor’s eyes were clear, her posture relaxed. She was the very picture of tranquil domesticity. The contrast between the woman before her and the woman in the letters was so absolute that Charlotte felt a fresh wave of confusion.

‘You are very quiet this afternoon,’ Eleanor observed, with a gentle smile. ‘Has the weather quite subdued your spirits?’

The question, so ordinary and kind, felt like a challenge. Charlotte was seized by a powerful impulse to confess all, to lay the letters upon the table and demand an explanation. But what would she say? ‘Sister, I have been reading your private letters, and I find you are not the person I took you for’? The accusation, even if unspoken, would wound Eleanor deeply, and betray a want of delicacy in Charlotte that she could not bear to own. To speak would be to shatter the very peace she so admired, and to acknowledge a knowledge she had no right to possess.

‘The weather is a little tiresome,’ Charlotte managed to say, turning towards the window. ‘But I am quite well.’

The conversation lapsed, and the afternoon wore on in a silence that was, to Charlotte, fraught with unspoken meaning. She watched her sister’s steady, even stitches, and thought of the hand that had written those impassioned lines. She considered the ‘formidable obstacle’ mentioned in the letter—was it a question of fortune? Of family opposition? The name ‘J.M.’ gave her no clue. She felt a sudden, fierce protectiveness towards this unknown suitor and towards the sister she was only just beginning to know. To betray Eleanor’s secret would be an act of cruelty, a violation of the very trust that had allowed the drawer to remain unlocked. Yet, to remain silent felt like a form of deceit.

At length, Eleanor set aside her work and said, ‘I believe the rain is easing. Shall we walk to the end of the lane before the light fails entirely?’

Charlotte agreed, and as they donned their pelisses, she felt that the decision had been made for her, though not by her own will. The secret was not hers to tell.
