The engagement of Miss Harriet Ellington to Mr. Charles Ashby was of that description which gives universal satisfaction, being founded on a mutual affection of four years’ standing, and supported on his side by a landed estate of clear twelve hundred a year, and on hers by a person uncommonly handsome and a temper uncommonly sweet. It was therefore with no small degree of surprise, and with a warmth bordering on resentment, that her particular friend Mrs. Catherine Delamere received the intelligence that the gentleman was at Brighton.

“At Brighton!” cried that lady, letting fall her needlework. “At Brighton, and not a line to prepare us! And in such a season, when every second person one meets is either a gamester or a rake, and the rest are but one remove from the theatre! I declare I shall not have a moment’s peace.”

“You are too severe upon the place,” said Harriet, laughing. “Mr. Ashby is a clergyman’s son, and has the living of Wexcombe in his gift. He can hardly be a gamester.”

“My dear Harriet, you are very young, and know nothing of the world. A clergyman’s son may keep a mistress as well as another man, and a great deal more conveniently, for he is not obliged to be seen with her.”

The party at Brighton consisted of Mrs. Delamere, a widow of independent fortune and decided opinions; her brother, Captain Delamere of the militia, who thought the war a great inconvenience and the peace a greater; and Harriet, who had been left to their joint guardianship by a father whose chief anxiety had been to dispose of his daughter pleasantly and at small expense. They had taken a house in the New Steine for six weeks, and were engaged to be happy.

It was on the third morning after this conversation that Mrs. Delamere came down to breakfast with an air of triumph which her companions had learned to dread.

“I have it from the most undoubted authority,” said she, “that Mr. Ashby is not at all the person we imagined. He is a man of the most dissipated character. He has fought two duels, run away with an heiress, and ruined a widow in Bath. Lady Ferrers told me so herself, and Lady Ferrers never exaggerates.”

“Lady Ferrers,” observed the Captain, “would not exaggerate a fire if it were burning her own house, for fear of being thought to have set it.”

“I do not desire your wit, brother. Harriet, you will oblige me by never dancing with Mr. Ashby, and by receiving him with the coldest civility if he should presume to call.”

“I have never yet spoken to Mr. Ashby,” said Harriet, with a composure that belied the flutter at her heart, “and I see no reason to suppose he will call.”

But he did call. He came the very next day, and was shown into the drawing-room, and found Mrs. Delamere upon the sofa with a book, and Harriet at the window with her work, and the Captain at the sideboard with the newspaper. He was a tall, grave young man, with a countenance rather handsome than agreeable, and a manner which, though perfectly civil, was so far from being easy as to amount to constraint. He spoke of the weather, and of the rooms, and of the difficulty of procuring a decent lodging; and having said these things, he said nothing more, but sat with his hands folded upon his knee, and looked at the fire.

Mrs. Delamere, who had prepared for impertinence and was resolved to meet it with severity, found herself disarmed. It is difficult to be severe with a man who inquires after your health with the solicitude of a physician and then falls silent for a quarter of an hour. She exerted herself to be agreeable, and was rewarded by a remark upon the herrings, which lasted until it was time for him to go. He bowed to them all, and to Harriet he said, with a look of some meaning, “I hope I may have the honour of seeing you at the assembly on Thursday.” And then he was gone.

“Well!” cried Mrs. Delamere, when the door was shut. “I never was so deceived in a man in my life. He is not a rake; he is a methodist. I dare say he has a meeting-house in his pocket.”

“He is very dull,” said the Captain.

“He is very serious,” said Mrs. Delamere. “I do not know which is worse.”

Harriet said nothing. She had been prepared to meet a villain, and had found a man who asked after her health and looked at the fire. She had expected to be shocked, and she was disappointed. It was the most unreasonable disappointment in the world, and she was ashamed of it, and the more ashamed because she could not account for it.

At the assembly on Thursday she danced with Mr. Ashby twice. He danced very correctly and without the smallest variation of countenance, and he spoke to her of the weather, and of the rooms, and of the difficulty of procuring a decent lodging, and she found herself answering him with a vivacity that was almost impertinence. She laughed at nothing; she made observations upon the company that were meant to be witty and were only pert; she said that she supposed he did not care for dancing, and he replied that he cared for it very much when he had a good partner. It was the dullest speech in the world, and she coloured to the ears.

“You are not yourself tonight,” said Mrs. Delamere, as they went home. “I never saw you so flighty. I hope you are not going to be in love with this Mr. Ashby after all.”

“In love with him! My dear Catherine, he is the dullest man in Brighton.”

“That is very true,” said Mrs. Delamere, “and yet you danced with him twice.”

“Because he asked me.”

“And you laughed at everything he said.”

“Because he said nothing.”

“And now you are out of humour.”

“I am not out of humour,” said Harriet, and went to bed in a passion with herself and everybody about her.

The truth was, that Harriet had been bred upon novels, and had formed her idea of a dangerous man from the perusal of them. A dangerous man, in her estimation, was a gentleman of dark brow and haughty manner, who spoke in epigrams, rode a black horse, and had a wife in every county. Mr. Ashby spoke in plain sentences, rode in a chaise, and had, as far as she could learn, no wife at all. He was kind, moreover, in a quiet way that she had not looked for. He sent her the second volume of a book she had mentioned, having observed that she had not been able to procure it. He walked with her to the library when the wind was high, and gave her the sheltered side of the street without seeming to know that he did so. He listened to her when she talked, which was more than Captain Delamere ever did. And yet he was dull. He was so dull that she could have cried with vexation.

“I cannot account for it,” said Mrs. Delamere, a week later, to Lady Ferrers. “Harriet is quite altered. She will not go to the rooms, she will not sing, she will not be entertained by anything. And all because Mr. Ashby has gone to London.”

“Gone to London!” cried Lady Ferrers. “My dear Mrs. Delamere, he is gone to be married. He has been engaged these five years to a Miss Somebody in Devonshire, a very plain girl with a large fortune, and he is the most constant creature in the world. I thought everybody knew it.”

Mrs. Delamere repeated this intelligence to her brother with an air of triumph, and to Harriet with an air of condolence. Harriet received it with great composure, and said that she was very glad to hear it, and that she hoped they would be happy. Then she went to her own room and sat down at the window, and looked out at the sea, and acknowledged to herself, for the first time, that she had been very foolish.

She had been warned against a villain, and she had found a good man; and she had been disappointed because he was not a villain. She had been angry with him for being dull, when she might have been thankful that he was honest. She had expected to be amused, and she had been loved—no, not loved; she had been treated with a kindness that she had not known how to value, and she had repaid it with a flippancy that she could not remember without a blush. She thought of his grave face and his folded hands and his inquiry after her health, and she understood at last that she had been in love with him, and that she had lost him, and that she had nobody to thank for it but herself.

“I hope you are not fretting about Mr. Ashby,” said Mrs. Delamere, coming in without knocking.

“I am not fretting,” said Harriet. “I am only thinking how very silly I have been.”

“That is a very proper reflection,” said Mrs. Delamere, “and I hope it will do you good. I said from the first that he was dull.”

“You said from the first that he was a rake,” said Harriet.

“That,” replied Mrs. Delamere with dignity, “was before I knew him.”

Harriet laughed, and dried her eyes, and went down to dinner; and if she was not quite so merry as she had been at the beginning of the season, she was a great deal wiser; and she had learned, what she had never learned from any novel, that a man may be very dull and very good, and that a young lady may be very clever and very foolish, and that the two are not always so far apart as the world imagines.
