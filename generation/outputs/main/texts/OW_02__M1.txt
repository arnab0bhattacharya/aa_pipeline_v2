The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which is a day of the week that has never yet produced anything of interest, and which Mr. Septimus Ponder, of Ponder, Chalk & Sibthorpe, had long ago consigned to the category of necessary indignities. It lay upon the breakfast table between the marmalade and the Times like a small grey blasphemy. It was addressed in a hand that had all the elegance of a forgery and the audacity of a birth certificate. Mr. Ponder, who was a man of solid proportions and of opinions to match, regarded it with the profound suspicion that a respectable Englishman must always feel in the presence of anything that is not on ruled paper.

“It has no stamp,” he said, turning it over with a butter-knife as if it were a peculiarly offensive beetle. “Nothing that is of the slightest value ever arrives unstamped, Clara. It is one of the few laws of Providence that I have found to be absolutely reliable.”

His wife, Mrs. Clara Ponder, who was a woman of considerable beauty and of aspirations that were considerably larger still, did not look up from her correspondence. She was at that moment engaged in the delicate task of making a list of the people who were to be invited to her autumn dance, and who were to be pointedly excluded from it. This second list, which was written in a different coloured ink, was much the longer of the two.

“Then you must throw it away, dear,” she murmured, her pen travelling with the steady, inexorable progress of a glacier. “If it has no stamp, it is certainly not a bill, and if it is not a bill, it cannot possibly be of any consequence.”

Mr. Ponder, who had spent twenty years in the City making money and thirty years in Clapham spending it, felt that this was a sound and sensible view. He opened the letter.

It was written in a Portuguese that had been translated into an English that was far too good for the post. It informed him, with a gravity that bordered upon the ceremonial, that the Marquisate of Algarve was without a direct heir, and that the genealogical investigations of the Conde de Silves had revealed that Mr. Septimus Ponder—born in the year 1854 in the parish of St. Olave’s, Hart Street—was the rightful claimant to the title and to the estates of Villa das Oliveiras.

Mr. Ponder read it twice. Then he laughed. It was not a laugh of amusement, but of relief. It was the laugh of a man who has been told that he has a fatal disease, only to discover that the doctor has mistaken his name.

“Clara,” he said, “it appears that I am a Marquis.”

Mrs. Ponder’s pen stopped. She looked up, and her eyes, which were the colour of a very expensive sherry, took on the sudden, hard brilliance of a gemstone.

“A what?” she said.

“A Marquis. Of Algarve. It seems that my grandfather, who was a man of very few virtues and of an indeterminate number of mistresses, was also a Portuguese nobleman who made his money in wine and his enemies in Lisbon. I am, it seems, the last of the line.”

He tossed the letter across the table with a gesture of magnificent dismissal. “It is, of course, a complete absurdity. I am a stockbroker. My grandfather was a grocer. The only thing we have ever inherited from Portugal is a taste for port wine, and I have always found that to be a most regrettable habit.”

Mrs. Ponder did not laugh. She picked up the letter and read it with the intense, silent concentration of a scholar deciphering a lost gospel.

“Septimus,” she said, her voice very low, “do you know what this means?”

“It means that some very clever criminals are trying to get my bank details, my dear. It is the modern equivalent of the highwayman, only with a better vocabulary.”

“It means,” she said, “that we shall need new stationery.”

Mr. Ponder stared at her. He had always known that his wife was a woman of ambition, but he had never suspected that her ambition was of such a poetic and immediate character.

“New stationery?” he repeated. “My dear Clara, I have not yet replied to the letter. I have not even decided whether it is true. I may well be a grocer’s grandson with a very poor imagination and a bad case of indigestion.”

“You cannot be a grocer’s grandson and a Marquis at the same time,” she said, with the absolute finality of a woman who has spent her life reconciling contradictions. “It is a question of identity. And identity, as you know, is entirely a matter of presentation.”

She stood up and went to the writing-desk. It was a large, rosewood affair, covered in the accumulated debris of a decade of social warfare: invitations, menus, lists of guests, and the small, poisonous notes that pass between women who are not on speaking terms.

“I shall write to Smythe’s,” she said. “They do the most exquisite crests. I think we shall have the olive branch. It is so suggestive of peace, and of the Mediterranean. And perhaps a small motto. *Fide et Fortuna*. Faith and Fortune. It has a very nice ring to it, don’t you think?”

“Clara,” said Mr. Ponder, “I am a man of the City. My motto is *Caveat Emptor*. Let the buyer beware.”

“That is very cynical, Septimus. And it is not at all suitable for a crest.”

She began to write. Mr. Ponder watched her. He saw the way her hand moved across the paper, the way her head was tilted, the way the morning light caught the silver in her hair. He had married her because she was beautiful, and because she had a way of looking at him that made him feel as if he were the only man in the world. He had stayed married to her because she was the only person who had ever made him feel that his life was not entirely a matter of percentages and dividends.

But now, as he watched her order stationery for a title he did not possess, for a country he had never seen, for a life he could not imagine, he felt a sudden, sharp pang of something that was almost like fear.

“It is a strange thing,” he said, “to be told that you are someone else.”

Mrs. Ponder looked up. “It is not strange at all,” she said. “It is the dream of every woman in Clapham. And of every man, too, though they are too proud to admit it.”

“But what if I do not want to be a Marquis?” he asked. “What if I am happy being Mr. Ponder?”

“No one is happy being Mr. Ponder,” she said, with a kindness that was more devastating than any cruelty. “They are merely comfortable. And comfort is a very poor substitute for distinction.”

She blotted the letter and sealed it with a wafer.

“There,” she said. “That is done. Now, we must discuss the matter of the liveries. I think a dark green would be very handsome. And gold braid. Definitely gold braid.”

Mr. Ponder sighed. He looked at the letter on the table, with its elegant handwriting and its absurd claims. He thought of the City, of the noise and the smoke and the relentless, grinding machinery of money. He thought of the villa in Clapham, with its neat garden and its quiet rooms. And he thought of Portugal, of a villa among olive trees, of a title that was as old as the hills and as bright as the sun.

He picked up his spoon and stirred his tea.

“Gold braid,” he said. “Yes. I think gold braid would be very nice indeed.”

And as he said it, he realized that he had already begun to believe it.
