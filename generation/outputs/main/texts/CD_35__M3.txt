If any one spot in the whole tangled web of England’s inland navigation can be said to be more completely given over to dampness, soot, desolation, and the bewildering idiocy of barge traffic than another, let that spot be the Seventeen Locks at Mudford. Here, the canal—a sluggish, green-scummed serpent that has long since forgotten its way to the sea—winds its venomous length through a brickyard, a dye-works, and a manure wharf, presenting a panorama of human misery so cheerfully awful that it might have been expressly designed by Providence to teach the wandering stranger the absolute futility of hope. The water itself was a thick, dark treacle, heavy with the refuse of a hundred towns, exhaling a vapour which, if it did not positively kill the rats that infested its banks, at least imparted to them a sickly, philosophic melancholy.

Upon the deck of the barge *Muddy Margery*, which lay wedged in the lock like a stubborn cork in a dirty bottle, stood young Tobias. He was no longer the wretched, soot-choked wisp of a boy who had crept, bleeding and half-suffocated, from the brick-lined hell of Mr. Grindle’s chimney-flues five years before. The canal, with its rough winds and rougher usage, had broadened his back and blown fresh air into his lungs, until he stood there, a sturdy, bright-eyed youth of fifteen, handling the heavy lock-paddles as though they were mere playthings. He owed this miraculous transformation to Captain Gudgeon, master of the *Margery* and father to the canal, a man whose legs were so bowed by years of deck-walking that they formed a perfect segment of a circle, and whose voice was pitched at a perpetual bellow.

‘Heave away, Tobias, my lad!’ roared the Captain, who was at that moment steering with one hand and wrestling a piece of salt pork to his mouth with the other. ‘Heave away, or we shall be stuck in this liquid grave till the angels blow the last trump! And pray to heaven the trump sounds louder than that there whistle at the dye-works, for I shall not hear it otherwise!’

‘Aye, aye, Cap’n!’ Tobias shouted back, leaning his weight against the heavy windlass. ‘She’s rising!’ 

‘Rising she is, and rising she must, for water will find its level, as my poor wife, rest her soul, was wont to say before the ague took her! But what of your letters, boy?’ the Captain continued, bellowing down the deck. ‘You have not had your nose in the book today!’

Tobias grinned, wiping a grimy hand across his brow. ‘I shall have ‘em tonight, Cap’n. Ruth promised to learn me the next page. We are at the letter P. We are come to Piracy and Plunder!’

‘Piracy and Plunder!’ echoed the Captain, spitting a fragment of pork into the canal with an air of profound moral disapproval. ‘A dark and bloody trade! Mind you stick to the Ps that stand for Providence and Pork, my boy, and leave the Plunder to the lords and the lawyers!’

The subject of this paternal discourse was the Captain’s daughter, Ruth, a slender, bright-faced girl of seventeen, who had just emerged from the narrow cabin, bearing a tin mug of tea. Ruth was the undisputed scholar of the *Muddy Margery*, having been to a charity school in her infancy for six entire months, a period of time which had furnished her with a complete mastery of the alphabet, a moral horror of idleness, and a tattered spelling-book, which she now held as a sacred text. It was from this revered volume that she had, with infinite patience and a good deal of unconscious ingenuity, taught the runaway sweep to read.

‘Here is your tea, Father,’ said Ruth, handing up the mug with a tenderness that sat oddly upon a barge-deck. ‘And here is the book, Tobias. But it is not Piracy we are come to; it is Patience. You skipped over the page when I was mending the sail.’

‘Patience be blowed!’ muttered the Captain, taking a scalding gulp. ‘A barge can’t wait for patience, and neither can a growing boy! But never mind the Ps, Tobias, for I see the lock-keeper stirring, and if we do not get through this gate before that idle rascal goes to sleep again, we shall be here till Michaelmas!’

Tobias threw his weight upon the gate-beam, the massive timber swung slowly back, and the *Muddy Margery* wallowed out of the lock into the broader cut beyond, her heavy hull parting the green scum with a gentle, gurgling sound. Tobias leaned upon the tiller, feeling the deep satisfaction of a boy who has mastered his trade, and Ruth, seating herself upon a coil of rope, opened the worn spelling-book upon her lap.

‘Now then, Tobias,’ she began, in the high, instructive tone she adopted when assuming the role of pedagogue. ‘We are at Patience. Can you tell me what Patience means?’

Tobias, his eyes fixed upon the distant, smoking chimneys of Mudford, pondered for a moment. ‘It means waiting for the lock to fill, Ruth, without swearing at the lock-keeper?’

‘Partly,’ said Ruth, smiling, ‘but it means more. It means bearing your troubles without complaining. Now, spell it.’

‘P-A-T-I—’ began Tobias, when his voice was abruptly arrested, and the colour fled from his face, leaving it a sickly, sooty grey.

He was staring at the bank. A man had just emerged from the lane leading to the manure wharf, a man whose long, shambling figure and bent shoulders seemed horribly familiar to the boy. He was dressed in a greasy, corduroy coat, fastened tightly up to the throat, and he carried under his arm a soot-stained canvas bag, from which protruded the handles of the great brushes that were the instruments of his trade. It was Grindle’s man, the cruel and crafty assistant, the very fiend who had driven him up the flues with a lighted straw, and who had sworn, with many foul oaths, to fetch him back if he ever ran.

The man stopped at the sight of the barge, and his small, red-rimmed eyes, blinking in the smoky light, travelled slowly over the deck, over Captain Gudgeon, over Ruth, and finally fixed themselves, with a slow, wicked leer of recognition, upon the face of Tobias.

‘Well, I’m blowed!’ said the man, in a hoarse, grating voice, like the scraping of a rusty flue-brush. ‘If it ain’t little Sooty Toby!’

Tobias dropped the tiller and retreated a step, his hands clenching at his sides. ‘I am not your Toby,’ he said, his voice trembling. ‘I belong to the barge.’

‘You belong to the master, you young vagabond!’ snarled the man, stepping to the edge of the bank. ‘You belong to Mr. Grindle, and Mr. Grindle you’ll come to, if I have to drag you by the hair of your head! I knowed you by the scar on your cheek, for I give it you myself with the poker!’

‘You lie!’ cried Tobias, his old terror giving way to a sudden, fierce rage. ‘I was sold to him by the parish, and the parish never sold me to be murdered in a chimney!’

‘Hold hard there, my fine fellow!’ roared Captain Gudgeon, who had been listening to this dialogue with a countenance of steadily gathering fury. He advanced to the side of the deck, his bowed legs giving him the appearance of a sea-god striding the waves. ‘What’s this about parish and master? Who are you, you black-faced scoundrel, that comes aboard my barge with your foul talk of dragging boys by the hair?’

‘I am Jem Grindle, sir,’ said the man, with an offensive smirk, ‘and I am the lawful apprentice-holder for my brother, Mr. Grindle, of the borough. This here boy is a runaway ‘prentice, and I am come to take him back to his lawful duties, which is sweeping chimneys, and a very honourable trade it is, sir, if I do say it myself!’

‘Honourable!’ echoed the Captain, with a snort of supreme contempt. ‘A pretty trade, indeed! A trade that stunts a boy’s growth, rots his lungs with soot, and sends him up a burning flue to scratch his knees on the brickwork! I know your honourable trades! I have seen ‘em at work in the towns, and I have seen the little black skeletons they leave behind! You may tell your brother, Mr. Grindle, that the boy is my ‘prentice now, bound to the canal, and that the canal will keep him!’

‘You can’t do that, Cap’n,’ said Jem, with a sinister grin. ‘The law is the law, and the law says a runaway ‘prentice must go back. I have got the papers in my pocket, and I have got the constable waiting at the wharf!’

‘The constable!’ roared the Captain, shaking his fist. ‘The constable! Let him come aboard, and I’ll give him a taste of the tiller, and a taste of the law at the same time! I’ll have no constable aboard my barge, and I’ll have no chimney-sweep either!’

But Tobias, seeing the man’s hand go to his pocket, and knowing the nature of the papers, felt a sudden coldness in his heart. He knew the law, and he knew that, however cruel the master, the law would give the boy back. He stepped forward and laid his hand on the Captain’s arm.

‘It is no use, Cap’n,’ he said, quietly. ‘He has got the papers, and he has got the law. I must go with him.’

‘Go with him!’ cried Ruth, springing to her feet, the spelling-book falling from her lap. ‘No, Tobias, you shall not go! Father, you will not let him go back to that dreadful place!’

‘Ruth,’ said Tobias, turning to her with a smile that was very sad, but very brave, ‘I must go. But I shall come back. I have learned Patience, Ruth, and I have learned to read. They cannot take that from me.’

‘They shall not take you!’ cried Ruth, her eyes flashing. ‘Father, do something!’

But the Captain, seeing the look of quiet resolution on the boy’s face, and knowing the futility of resistance, could only shake his head and lower his eyes. Jem Grindle, with a triumphant leer, held out his hand.

‘Come along, Sooty Toby,’ he said. ‘The master is waiting, and the chimneys are cold.’

Tobias stepped to the side of the barge, and, with a last look at Ruth and the Captain, sprang lightly to the bank. He did not look back, but walked steadily on, his head erect, and his step firm, following the shambling figure of Jem Grindle down the lane towards the manure wharf.

Ruth stood at the tiller, watching him until he disappeared behind the brickyard. Then she picked up the worn spelling-book, and, smoothing out the crumpled page, read the word at the top, with a sob in her throat
