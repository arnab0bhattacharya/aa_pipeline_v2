The morning had not yet broken the hold of the night when the widow Consolata Paredes went down to the shore. It was that grey hour, not of light but of a lessening of darkness, when the sea and the sky are of one substance and the world is a vast, breathing emptiness. The air, heavy with the damp of the ocean and the faint, sweet rot of the mangrove swamps inland, clung to her skin. For three years since the sea had taken her husband, Ramiro, she had met this hour on the sand, a solitary figure in a world of shifting water and indifferent wind, her vigil a form of prayer without words, a communion with the vastness that had both given her a life and then, with the same placid indifference, taken it away. The tide, having completed its mysterious errand in the deep, was now returning, and it left upon the dark, wet sand not only its usual cargo of weed and shell but a thing that did not belong.

He lay as if asleep, but no man sleeps with such a terrible finality, with his face turned to the unseeing sky and his limbs arranged by the casual, powerful hands of the surf. His uniform, the coarse grey of the government’s coastal troops, was plastered to his body, the insignia on his collar rendered meaningless by the salt. He was young, impossibly young, with the fine bones of his face still sharp beneath the pallor of death. The sea had been neither gentle nor cruel; it had simply returned what it had taken. At his side, still attached by a frayed leather strap to his shoulder, was a canvas satchel, dark with water and heavy with the weight of the sea’s incursion.

Consolata stood for a long time, her bare feet sinking slightly into the cold, yielding sand. There was no fear in her, only a profound and weary recognition of the tragedy that washed up on these shores as regularly as the timber from a wreck. She had seen drowned men before. But this one, with his satchel, was different. He was not merely a fisherman lost to a squall or a drunkard who had stumbled from the pier. He was a part of the great, noisy, violent quarrel that was tearing the republic to pieces—a quarrel that seemed, from this remote village of Puerto Seco, like the distant, meaningless shouting of madmen. The war was a rumor of taxes and conscription, of coffee prices fixed by men in the capital who had never seen a coffee plant, of young men leaving and never returning. Now, it had returned to her beach in the form of this silent boy.

With a sigh that was swallowed by the whisper of the waves, she knelt. The satchel’s brass clasp was green with verdigris and sealed shut by grime. Her fingers, knotted and strong from a life of hauling nets and mending sails, worked at it with a patient dexterity. The leather was stiff, but it gave way at last with a sucking sound. Inside, protected by layers of oilcloth that had only partly done their duty, were the letters. Dozens of them. Some were in stiff, official-looking envelopes, the kind used for business or by the authorities. Others were mere scraps of paper, folded and refolded, soft as old cloth. She could not tell one from the other by their content, for to her the markings on them were as mysterious and arbitrary as the tracks of birds in the sand. They were merely shapes, a language of ink and intention that was as sealed to her as the heart of the drowned man. But she saw, with a certainty that required no reading, that some were addressed to the Palace of the Republic, and others, she knew with a similar, unshakeable intuition, to the mountain stronghold of the General who called himself El Lobo. The two fires that were consuming the country.

The problem, then, was not what the letters contained, but who was to be trusted with their contents. The act of having them read was an act of allegiance, a declaration. She carried the satchel back towards the village, her shadow long and thin in the growing light. To whom could she turn? There was Father Mateo, a man whose faith was as worn as his cassock, who preached submission to God and, by implication, to the authorities in the capital. He would see the letters to the rebels and, in a moment of patriotic or perhaps merely fearful clarity, deliver them to the Alcalde. And the Alcalde, a man with a politician’s smile and a merchant’s soul, would see only a means to improve his standing with the Palace, to prove his vigilance. He would take the letters to the government, and perhaps a reward would follow, a few silver pesos that would taste of the young soldier’s blood.

Then there was Don Luis, the old schoolmaster, who had taught her Ramiro to read. A quiet man, given to long silences and a bitterness that was not directed at any man but at the whole sorry business of the world. He was a man of principles, but his principles were of a sort that led only to poverty and a certain lonely integrity. He might read them and, seeing in them a desperate plea from the rebel cause, urge her to find a way to send them to the mountains, an act of folly that would bring the Alcalde’s men down upon her and her small house like vultures. Each man, she understood, would read the letters not for what they were, but for what he was. The words on the page would be a mirror, and she would be handing the mirror to whichever face she chose to see.

The sun was now above the headland, its heat beginning to press down upon the village. The smell of frying fish and woodsmoke filled the air. Consolata sat on the low stone wall of her own small dwelling, the satchel heavy on her lap. She was a woman alone, a fact that in the ordinary course of things was a misfortune, but in these times had become a kind of fortress. She had no son to be conscripted, no husband to be denounced. Her only allegiance was to the memory of the man the sea had taken, and to the simple, hard reality of her own survival. The letters were a complication, a piece of the mainland’s madness that had drifted to her shore. To pass them on was to enter into that madness. To destroy them was to make a choice of a different, perhaps more terrible, kind—a choice of silence, of negation. She looked from the satchel to the horizon, where the sea lay flat and shimmering, a sheet of hammered silver. It had given her this problem. It had taken her husband, and it had given her a dead boy and his unreadable words. It was the only power she truly knew, a power that asked for nothing and explained nothing. The decision, she felt with a sudden, clear certainty, was not hers to make by any human hand. It belonged to the same vast, indifferent authority that governed the tides and the storms. Her part was only to see it done.
