I was in the very act of wrapping a half-pound of mixed biscuits for Miss Daphne Pell when the end of the world came to Brixcombe. It is a curious thing, but I had been thinking how excessively dull the afternoon was. The gas was alight in our shop, though it was barely three, and the sea fog was creeping up the hill, when the floor gave a sudden lurch. 

I do not mean a gradual trembling, such as one reads of in foreign parts, but a violent, sickening heave. The biscuit tin slid off the counter and struck Miss Pell a sharp blow upon the ankle. The plate-glass window fell outward into the street with a prodigious crash, and before I could even cry out, the whole of the ceiling came down upon us. 

I found myself sitting in a confined space, half-stifled by a thick, yellowish dust. A heavy joist had fallen across the counter, completely walling me in, and the shelves to my right had been hurled flat. I was bruised, and a stream of blood was trickling down my nose from a cut in my forehead, but I was otherwise sound. 

"Are you hurt, miss?" I asked, coughing out the plaster.

There was a moment of silence, followed by a sob. "I am buried alive," said Miss Pell, with the sharp, distinct enunciation of a young lady who has been carefully brought up. "I shall suffocate. I know I shall suffocate."

I could not see her, for the dust was blinding, but I judged she was somewhere to my left. "If you can move your arms, miss," said I, "you are not buried. You are merely trapped. It was an earthquake, I fancy."

"An earthquake!" she repeated, in a tone of profound indignation. "In Brixcombe! It is perfectly preposterous."

I was becoming acutely aware that if I did not bestir myself, we might indeed suffocate. The air was already close. I found my matches in my waistcoat pocket, and after several attempts, struck one. The flare showed me a narrow, cave-like space, roofed by a fallen beam and a tangle of plaster. Miss Pell was sitting upon the floor, entirely covered in white dust, looking more like a miller's wife than the daughter of the Brixcombe bank manager. The entire front of the shop had vanished, replaced by a solid bank of rubble that reached up to the broken beams above.

"Dear me," I said, as the match burnt my fingers. "The whole street must have gone."

"We must ring for assistance," said Miss Pell, beginning to recover her composure. "Ring the bell, please."

"The bell-handle is in the street, miss," I replied, "and very likely the street is in the cellar. We shall have to dig our way out, I think."

"Dig?" she said, with a rising inflection. "Dig with what?"

I had already begun to clear the plaster from about my knees. The joist that formed our roof rested upon the overturned cash desk, and beneath the counter, I remembered, we kept a small coal-shovel. It was the work of a minute to grope my way to it. I thrust it into her hands.

"You can use this, miss," said I. "I will use the biscuit scoop. We must clear the rubble at the base there, where the window was."

Miss Pell took the shovel with a reluctance I had often observed in her when asked to carry a parcel. "I have never done such a thing in my life," she protested.

"Then it is quite time you began," I replied, perhaps a trifle sharply, for the foulness of the air was making my head ache. "If you will clear the loose stuff, I will try to prise up this beam."

I set to work with my scoop, and after a moment's hesitation, Miss Pell began to shovel. It was awkward, ungentlemanly work, but I was surprised to find she applied herself to it with a sort of desperate energy. We worked in silence for what seemed an hour, though I dare say it was twenty minutes, the dust choking us and making our throats raw. I found a bottle of Benger’s Digestive Mixture on a lower shelf, miraculously unbroken, and we divided it between us, which served to moisten our lips.

As we dug, my mind raced ahead with a sort of scientific curiosity. I had read of the subterranean adjustments of the earth's crust, but I had always understood the south of England was practically dead, geologically speaking. The whole stratum of the Wealden chalk might be shifting. I found myself wondering if the shock had been felt in London, and whether the newspapers would have it in time for the morning editions.

At last, I managed to get my shoulder under the main beam. With a heave that strained every muscle in my back, I lifted it an inch, and then another. "Pull the counter away!" I gasped.

Miss Pell seized the end of the broken counter and dragged it backward with a strength I should not have credited her with. The beam slipped, crashed down an inch, and left a narrow gap. I scrambled up, tearing my coat, and found myself looking out upon a scene of utter devastation.

The High Street was a chaos of fallen masonry. The front of the draper's shop opposite had entirely collapsed into the roadway, and the roof of the town hall had slid bodily into the street. A thick, grey haze hung over everything, and the silence, after the roar of the falling masonry, was profound. It was as if the entire town had been wiped out in a single breath.

I turned and gave my hand to Miss Pell, pulling her up through the gap. She stood beside me on the ruined threshold, her pretty muslin dress torn and grey with plaster, her hair hanging in dusty tangles about her face. She looked out upon the destruction with wide, frightened eyes.

"Is anyone there?" I shouted. "Hullo!"

A faint cheer answered from somewhere to the right, and I saw a party of men, the grocer and a couple of sailors from the fishmonger's, clambering over the rubble toward us.

"Here!" cried one of them. "Is anyone hurt?"

"Only shaken," I replied. "But the shop is gone."

Miss Pell clutched my arm. "My mother," she said, in a whisper. "She is at home. I must go to her."

"You are in no state to walk," I said. "Let the men help you."

She looked at me, and I think it was then that the full realization of what had happened came to her. She had been born and bred in comfort, waited upon and sheltered, and now she stood in the ruins of her familiar world, dependent upon a shop assistant and a coal-shovel for her life. The irony of it appealed to me even then. We had been very formally acquainted, and now we were comrades in a disaster.

"I am quite able to walk," she said, with a return of her old manner. "I thank you for your assistance, Mr.—"

"Simms," said I. "Arthur Simms."

"I thank you, Mr. Simms. You were very practical."

"It was the only thing to be," I replied. "But we had better get out of the street. There may be another shock."

We made our way over the heaps of broken brick, and as we reached the level pavement, the church bells began to ring out, wild and uncontrolled, from the tower of St. George's. It was a hideous, jangling sound, as if the world had gone mad. I looked back at the ruin of the shop, and at the girl beside me, and I could not help but laugh.

"It is no laughing matter," said Miss Pell, severely.

"I am not laughing at the earthquake, miss," I said. "I am laughing at us."
