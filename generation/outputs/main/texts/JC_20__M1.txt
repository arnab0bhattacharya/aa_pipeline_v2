The heat of San Cristobal was not a heat that one could escape by seeking the shade; it was a positive quality of the air, a weight that pressed upon the eardrums and filled the lungs with a vapour that smelled of dry dust, rotting oranges, and the invisible, exhalations of the fever-haunted jungle beyond the hills. It lay upon the town like a shroud, preserving the decay of the revolution in a state of suspended animation. I had been established there for three years as a ship chandler—Ferrier & Co., a name painted in peeling white letters upon the dark timber of the warehouse fronting the quay—and in that time I had learned that the passions of the Latin temperament, like the tides of the gulf, rose and fell with a terrifying regularity, leaving behind them a silt of broken promises and unburied dead.

It was in the stagnant hour before the evening gun, when the shadows of the palm trees stretched long and black across the plaza, that Mr. Alistair Hemming, the retiring Consul of Her Britannic Majesty, came to my office. He was a man who seemed to have been drained of all vital fluid by the climate, leaving only a brittle shell of linen and bone, a ghost of the Service preserved by habit and the memory of a cool English garden. He did not sit, but paced the narrow strip of floorboards with a jerky, nervous energy that set the dust motes dancing in the shafts of amber light.

"It is a matter of confidence, Ferrier," he said, his voice thin and reedy, lacking the resonance of command. "Of absolute, sacred confidence."

I looked at him from behind my desk, where the ledgers lay open like the wings of a dead bird. I was a practical man, a man of invoices and bills of lading, and I had little patience for the vapourings of diplomats who sought to imbue their bureaucratic failures with the dignity of a tragedy. But the Consul was in earnest; his hands, clasped behind his back, were trembling slightly, and his gaze darted toward the shuttered window as if he expected to see the muzzle of a Mauser rifle staring back at him.

"The archives, Ferrier," he whispered, leaning across the desk, the smell of camphor and stale tobacco wafting from him. "The Consular archives. The treaties, the claims, the... the correspondence. You know how it is. In this country, a piece of paper is a weapon. It is a loaded gun left upon a table."

I knew indeed. I knew that the new regime, the Junta of the Colonel, was hungry for legitimacy, hungry for the old concessions that the English had extracted from the weak republics of the past. And I knew that the Colonel, a man of obscure Indian blood and terrifying ambition, did not care for the nuances of international law. He cared only for the power that lay dormant in those boxes of yellowing parchment.

"The packet boat leaves at dawn," Hemming continued, his eyes searching my face for a sympathy I did not feel. "I cannot take them. The Captain refuses. He fears an inspection at the mouth of the river. But I cannot leave them for *him*." He did not say the Colonel's name. One did not. "He would interpret the silence... badly. But if they were merely... misplaced. If they were in the keeping of a British subject, a man of property. A man of... integrity."

He used the word 'integrity' as one might use a bribe, expecting it to purchase my compliance. I felt a surge of that ironic amusement which is the only defence of the white man in the tropics against the absurdity of his surroundings. Here was this frail creature, this man who had spent his life signing documents that meant nothing, asking me to risk my life for the sake of a few boxes of paper that meant even less.

"And if the Colonel should inquire?" I asked, my voice sounding harsh and loud in the stillness.

"You will say you know nothing," Hemming said eagerly. "You will say the boxes were taken by the stevedores. You are a merchant, Ferrier. You deal in goods. These are... goods. I will pay you. Freightage. A handsome sum."

"It is not the money, Mr. Hemming," I said, rising and walking to the window to look out at the harbour. The water was like oil, smooth and unbroken, reflecting the bruised purple of the sky. A few lights began to twinkle on the men-of-war anchored in the stream, indifferent sentinels of a civilization that felt very far away. "It is the inconvenience of being shot."

I agreed, of course. There is a fatalism in the seafaring life, a weariness of the spirit that makes a man accept the next hazard simply because it is there, and because to refuse it would require an energy he no longer possesses. I took the boxes—four of them, heavy with the dust of history—and stowed them in the lower hold of the warehouse, behind a wall of coal sacks.

For three days, the heat continued, oppressive and unmoving. The town waited. Revolution, like the rain, announced itself with a change in the pressure of the air. Then, on the fourth afternoon, the silence of the siesta was broken by the rhythmic tramp of boots and the jingle of spurs.

I was in the counting-house when the door was thrown open. It was not the Colonel himself, but his shadow, a man they called the Captain of the Guard, a mestizo with a face like a carved idol and eyes that held no expression at all. Behind him stood two soldiers, sweating in their heavy blue tunics, their rifles held loosely, pointing at the floorboards.

"Señor Ferrier," the Captain said. His Spanish was clipped, devoid of courtesy. "The Colonel sends his compliments. He requires the papers of the English Consulate. He knows you have them. He is a busy man. He does not wish to search."

I leaned back in my chair, the cane creaking beneath me. I looked at the man, and I felt a strange detachment, as if I were watching the scene from the roof of the building. I thought of Hemming, safe on a ship steaming toward the Cape, and I thought of the Colonel, sitting in the Government Palace, and I thought of myself, a middle-aged chandler with a bad liver, caught between the vanity of empires.

"The Consul left nothing with me," I said. The lie tasted of dust. "I deal in oil, rope, and canvas. I do not deal in politics."

The Captain stepped forward. He did not shout. He did not threaten. He simply looked at me, and the heat of the room seemed to intensify, becoming a physical pressure upon my chest. The soldiers shifted their weight, the leather of their belts creaking.

"The Colonel is a man of order," the Captain said softly. "But he is also a man of... consequence. If the papers are not produced, it is an insult to the Republic. And the Republic, Señor, is thirsty."

It was then that the moral weight of the situation, which until that moment had been merely a practical annoyance, settled upon me. It was not about the papers. It was about the submission of will. It was about the hair's-breadth between a man who stands by his word and a man who sells his life for the safety of his skin. I looked at the Captain's hand, resting near the butt of his revolver. I looked at the sweat beading on his upper lip. I realized that I was not afraid of death—death is a common occurrence in San Cristobal—but I was afraid of the shame of yielding to this mediocrity, to this petty tyranny of the hinterland.
