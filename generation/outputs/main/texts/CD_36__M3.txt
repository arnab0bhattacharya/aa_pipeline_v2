If any solitary mortal in the whole distracted city of Paris, in the year one thousand seven hundred and ninety-three, possessed a profounder veneration for the infallible, the unyielding, the inexorable, and the magnificently tyrannical nature of Time than Master Étienne Broucard, ancient clockmaker of the Rue de la Cossonnerie, that mortal has escaped the diligent enquiries of history. For Master Broucard did not merely sell clocks; he worshipped them. He ministered to their iron ailments, fed their hungry brass stomachs with the oil of patience, and listened to their ticking heartbeats with the grave devotion of a patriarch attending to his infants. He kept the great, bronzed, and bewilderingly complex clocks of the City Hall running through every change of government—a statement which, in those days of roaring tumbrils and constantly shifting authority, was no trifling boast. Kingdoms had fallen, emperors had been whittled down to citizens, and citizens had been elevated to executioners, yet Master Broucard’s pendulums had never ceased their majestic, impartial swing.

The workshop of Master Broucard was a crowded, chattering, and hyperactive metropolis of brass and steel, where ten thousand ticking, whirring, and chiming mechanisms engaged in a perpetual and discordant conversation. There were fat Dutch clocks that panted like overfed aldermen; tall, gaunt grandfather clocks that coughed and wheezed with the asthmatic gravity of retired notaries; and shrill little mantel clocks that clicked and clattered with the vain, breathless chatter of old washerwomen. It was into this gleaming, restless, noisy jungle of metal that young Guillot Marin, his apprentice, peered with his sharp, ambitious eyes, perpetually dazzled by the spinning brass gears that danced like enchanted acrobats in the candlelight.

On a raw, damp afternoon, when the fog from the river crept through the city streets like a famished and searching ghost, the bell above the workshop door jangled with a harsh, discordant cry, and Citizen Marchaud—a Deputy of the Revolutionary Committee, wearing his tri-colour cockade as if it were a third, and exceedingly belligerent, eye—swept into the crowded room. He was a man who carried his chin as though it were a bayonet, and his boots, heavy and muddied, struck the wooden floor with the rhythmic, threatening thump of a marching drum.

“Citizen Broucard!” barked the Deputy, his voice cleaving the ticking air like a guillotine blade cleaving a neck. “The National Convention has decreed it. The old, superstitious, priest-ridden months are dead. We are now in the era of Reason. You will ascend to the City Hall, and you will adjust the great public clocks to the new Republican Calendar. Year One, month of Brumaire. You will set them back, you will set them forward, you will pull the sun from the heavens and reset it, if Reason demands it!”

Master Broucard, a small, white-haired, and remarkably upright man, with spectacles that magnified his eyes to the size of twin moons, looked at the Deputy. He did not tremble. He did not quail. He merely looked, with the vast, unblinking patience of a ticking pendulum, at the revolutionary official.

“Citizen Deputy,” said Master Broucard, polishing a small, delicate silver cog with a rag that was black with age and wisdom, “a clock is a mechanical witness to the truth. It is not a politician.”

“Truth!” snorted Citizen Marchaud, striking a fat Dutch clock with his gloved hand, causing it to wheeze in protest. “The truth is the Republic! The truth is what we decree it to be! You will set the clocks, Citizen, or you will find your head making the acquaintance of a basket. I give you until tomorrow’s dawn.”

With that, the Deputy turned on his heavy heel and marched out, leaving the door swinging, and the ten thousand clocks of the Rue de la Cossonnerie ticking with a furious, agitated, and seemingly terrified speed.

Young Guillot Marin, whose heart had been beating at a pace no sensible timepiece would ever sanction, crept from the shadowy corner where he had been sorting mainsprings. His face was as pale as the fog outside, and his hands shook like a broken escapement.

“Master!” whispered Guillot, his voice cracking with the delicious terror of the young. “You cannot refuse him! They will surely take your head! They will wrap it in the very chains of the City Hall clock! You must go! You must take your tools, and you must set the dials to Brumaire, or whatever mad month they wish, and let Reason be served!”

Master Broucard set down the silver cog. He looked at his apprentice with an expression of immense, sorrowful, and theatrical pity. He slowly removed his spectacles, breathed upon them with a long, weary sigh, and polished them with the corner of his sleeve.

“Sit down, Guillot,” said Master Broucard, pointing a long, oil-stained finger at a three-legged stool. “You are a fool. But you are a young fool, and there is perhaps time to cure the affliction. You speak of Reason as though it were a turnip, to be planted in whatever soil the current magistrate sees fit to manure. You speak of setting the dials as though it were a matter of turning a hand to point at a painted fleur-de-lis or a painted cap of liberty. But I ask you, boy—what does a clock measure?”

“Minutes, Master,” stammered Guillot, trembling upon his stool. “Hours. Days.”

“It measures the passage of human life, Guillot,” said Master Broucard, his voice dropping to a solemn, resonant rumble that seemed to draw the very ticking of the room into a hushed, attentive silence. “It measures the steady, inevitable, and incorruptible march of existence. When I forge a gear, I forge a promise. When I align a pendulum, I align a covenant with the universe. A clock does not care if it is the year of our Lord, or the year of the Republic. It does not care if a king sits upon a throne, or if a merchant sits upon a barrel. It says, ‘The sun moves, the earth turns, the heart beats, and time passes.’ If I take the great clock of the City Hall, and I force its honest, mathematical hands to lie—if I make it strike ten when the shadows say nine—I am not merely altering a machine, Guillot. I am murdering Time. I am putting a knife to the throat of Truth, and telling the citizens of Paris that a decree can erase the sun!”

The old clockmaker’s eyes flashed with a magnificent, theatrical indignation. He seized a heavy brass pendulum bob from the workbench and brandished it as though it were a holy relic.

“They can take my head, Guillot. They can boil it, they can pickle it, they can place it upon a pike outside the Tuileries, and I dare say it will make a very handsome and philosophical spectacle for the passing crowds. But I will not, while I have breath in my body and oil in my oil-can, bend the honest mechanism of a timepiece to the vain, whimsical, and shifting doctrines of men who think they can legislate the heavens! A clock is a mechanical witness to the truth, and I am its keeper. If the Republic wishes to change the calendar, let the Republic change the stars! Let the Republic decree that the earth shall spin backwards! Let the Republic arrest the sun in its orbit and drag it before the Revolutionary Tribunal! But until they do, my clocks shall tick the honest, old, priest-ridden, superstitious, and unimpeachably true time!”

Guillot Marin stared at his master, his fear entirely swallowed by a vast, overwhelming awe. The ten thousand clocks in the workshop seemed to have caught the old man’s fervour; they ticked louder, bolder, with a defiant, clattering rhythm that sounded like the tramp of a celestial army.

“And so, Master?” Guillot whispered, his eyes wide. “What will you do?”

Master Broucard replaced the pendulum bob upon the bench with a gentle, reverent click. He put on his spectacles, picked up his small silver cog, and resumed his polishing.

“I shall go to the workshop, Guillot,” said Master Broucard, with the calm, unshakeable serenity of a man who has made his peace with eternity. “I shall oil the escapement of the great City Hall clock. I shall adjust the pendulum nut by a fraction of a thread, for it has been gaining two seconds a day, which is a fault I cannot abide. And I shall leave the dial exactly as it is. For if a man must die, let him die in the service of the only honest mechanic left in Paris. Let him die for the Truth, which, unlike the Republic, does not change its coat every Tuesday.”

And so, as the raw fog pressed its ghostly face against the windows of the Rue de la Cossonnerie, and the revolutionary drums beat their sullen, threatening tattoo in the distant streets, Master Broucard polished his silver cog, and the ten thousand clocks ticked on, faithfully, indifferently, and magnificently, towards the dawn of a new, and very perilous, day.
