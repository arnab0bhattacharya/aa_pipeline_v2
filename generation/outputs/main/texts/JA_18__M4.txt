The village of Ashcombe Parva had long enjoyed the blessing of a clergyman whose wife possessed a very active benevolence and an inflexible conviction of her own judgement in the affairs of others. Mrs. Ellingham was not a woman to leave her neighbours to the dull management of their own happiness; she considered it her peculiar province to arrange their comforts, and she had, in the course of twelve years, disposed of three curates, two schoolmistresses, and a widow of uncertain temper, all to her own entire satisfaction, and, as she was pleased to observe, to the general good.

It was therefore with a lively sense of duty that she observed the arrival of Miss Harriet Danvers at Ashcombe Hall. Miss Danvers was the squire's niece, a young woman of two-and-twenty, with a fortune of six thousand pounds, a placid countenance, and a manner so uniformly civil that it seemed designed to discourage all intimacy while it forbade all complaint. She had come to keep her uncle company, and to be, as Mrs. Ellingham expressed it, settled in life.

The village doctor, Mr. Francis Lydgate, was a young man of good family, sober habits, and a very moderate income, who had the misfortune to be universally esteemed. He was esteemed by the farmers for his skill with their cattle, by the ladies for his manner of bowing, and by Mrs. Ellingham for his being the only unmarried gentleman within five miles who was not either too old, too poor, or too foolish to be improved by matrimony. She had long destined him for some deserving woman; and Miss Danvers appeared to her the very instrument of providence.

The scheme was formed in a moment, and pursued with all the ardour of a woman who has never been thwarted. Mrs. Ellingham called at the Hall on Tuesday; she called again on Thursday; by Saturday she had contrived a tea-party at which the doctor and Miss Danvers should be seated next to each other, and by the following week she had established a system of small manoeuvres which would have done credit to a general.

“My dear Miss Danvers,” said she, one afternoon, having secured that young lady in a corner of the drawing-room, “I am sure you must find the country very dull after London. You want society—you want the cheerful interchange of sentiment which is so necessary to a young mind. Mr. Lydgate is so obliging as to say that he is quite at your service for a game of backgammon, whenever you are weary of your book.”

“You are very kind,” replied Miss Danvers, “but I am seldom weary of my book, and I never play backgammon.”

“Ah! you say so now, but you will find the evenings long. Mr. Lydgate is a most agreeable young man; he has read a great deal, and has a very pretty taste in music. He tells me he is passionately fond of a duet.”

“I am sorry for it,” said Miss Danvers, “for I have no voice at all.”

Mrs. Ellingham smiled, as one who knows better. “Modesty is a charming quality, my dear, but it must not be carried too far. I have heard you hum a tune at church, and I am sure you could sing very well if you chose.”

Miss Danvers made no reply, and Mrs. Ellingham, feeling that she had planted a seed, went to find Mr. Lydgate, whom she discovered in the hall, examining the squire's new horse.

“My dear sir,” said she, “I am delighted to see you. I have been telling Miss Danvers of your musical talents, and she is quite impatient to hear you play. She is a little shy, as young ladies are, but I assure you she is very sensible of your merits.”

“I am much obliged to her,” said Mr. Lydgate, with a bow, “but I never play before strangers.”

“Strangers! La! Mr. Lydgate, you have known her these three weeks. You are too modest. I shall bring her to the vicarage to-morrow, and you shall play to us both.”

“I am engaged to-morrow,” said Mr. Lydgate, “and the day after, and indeed every evening this week. I have a great many patients.”

“Patients! Nonsense! You must not let your patients run away with your politeness. I shall expect you at six.”

Mr. Lydgate bowed again, and said nothing; but as he rode home he resolved to be very busy indeed.

The following week produced a series of those small events which Mrs. Ellingham considered as decisive proofs of a growing attachment. She met Mr. Lydgate in the lane, and told him that Miss Danvers had spoken of him with great esteem. She met Miss Danvers at the milliner's, and assured her that Mr. Lydgate had declared her to be the most sensible woman in the parish. She invited them both to dinner, and placed them at opposite corners of the table, that they might have the pleasure of looking at each other. She contrived that they should walk home together, and then followed at a distance, to enjoy the sight of their felicity.

The walk home was, in truth, a very silent one. Mr. Lydgate offered his arm, which Miss Danvers accepted with a civil inclination of her head; and they proceeded for some minutes without a word.

“I believe,” said Mr. Lydgate at last, “that Mrs. Ellingham is very kind.”

“Very kind indeed,” replied Miss Danvers.

“She has a great deal of good nature.”

“A great deal.”

Another pause.

“I am afraid,” said Mr. Lydgate, “that she is a little too—that is, she is very anxious for the happiness of her neighbours.”

“She is very anxious,” said Miss Danvers, with a slight emphasis.

Mr. Lydgate looked at her, and thought he perceived a smile lurking at the corner of her mouth. “I have sometimes fancied,” said he, “that her anxiety is not always—that it is possible to be too anxious.”

“I have fancied so too,” said Miss Danvers.

They walked on a little farther, and Mr. Lydgate began to feel that he was not alone in his sufferings. “I have a great respect for Mrs. Ellingham,” said he, “but I have no talent for—for the sort of thing she is pleased to imagine.”

“Nor I,” said Miss Danvers. “I have no talent for being disposed of.”

“You are very good to say so,” cried Mr. Lydgate, “for I have been afraid that I was the only person in the parish who was not enchanted with her schemes. I am sure I wish her every happiness, but I cannot think that she has any right to make me happy in her own way.”

“She is very kind,” said Miss Danvers, “but I have no wish to be married.”

“Nor I,” said Mr. Lydgate, with an earnestness that made her smile outright. “I have a great deal to do, and very little to do it with. I cannot afford a wife.”

“And I,” said Miss Danvers, “have a great deal to read, and very little patience for company. I cannot afford a husband.”

They looked at each other, and for the first time since their acquaintance began, they understood one another perfectly.

“Then we are agreed,” said Mr. Lydgate, “that we are the two most ungrateful people in Ashcombe Parva.”

“We are agreed,” said Miss Danvers, “that we are the two most persecuted.”

From that evening their intercourse assumed a new character. They met with the frankness of fellow-sufferers, and conversed with the ease of persons who have no design upon each other. They agreed to be always civil to Mrs. Ellingham, and always to be engaged when she proposed a party. They agreed to praise each other to her in the warmest terms, that she might think her scheme prospering, and leave them in peace. They agreed, in short, to unite only in their efforts to escape her kindness.

Mrs. Ellingham, however, was not to be deceived. She observed that they talked to each other with great freedom, and she concluded that they were on the point of an explanation. She observed that they avoided her, and she concluded that they were shy. She observed that they never sat together, and she concluded that they were prudent. She therefore redoubled her efforts, and was more than ever convinced of her own sagacity, when she met them one morning walking together in the shrubbery, and heard Mr. Lydgate say, with great animation, “I assure you, madam, I have the highest respect for you.”

“And I for you,” replied Miss Danvers.

“I am the happiest of women,” cried Mrs. Ellingham, advancing from behind a laurel. “I knew it would be so. I knew you could not resist. I shall write to your uncle immediately.”

“Write to my uncle!” cried Miss Danvers. “For what?”

“For his consent, my dear. You cannot suppose that I would allow you to be married without his consent.”

“Married!” said Mr. Lydgate. “I have no intention of being married.”

“No intention! La! Mr. Lydgate, you are very modest. But I have seen it all this fortnight. You have been walking together, and talking together, and praising each other, and now you say you have no intention. I am not to be deceived.”

“I assure you, madam,” said Mr. Lydgate, “that I have the greatest respect for Miss Danvers, but I have no wish to marry.”

“And I,” said Miss Danvers, “have the greatest respect for Mr. Lydgate, but I have no wish to marry.”

Mrs. Ellingham looked from one to the other in astonishment. “No wish to marry! What can you mean? You are the most ungrateful pair I ever knew. I have taken more pains with you than with any two people in the parish. I have invited you, and seated you, and walked you, and talked you, and now you tell me you have no wish to marry. I am sure I never heard anything so provoking.”

“We are very sorry to provoke you,” said Mr. Lydgate, “but we cannot marry to oblige you.”

“Cannot! You can if you will. There is no difficulty. You have a house, and she has a fortune. You are made for each other.”

“We are made for something else,” said Miss Danvers, “I hope.”

Mrs. Ellingham was too much offended to reply. She walked away with great dignity, and for a week she was silent. But her silence was more terrible than her speech. She looked at them with reproachful eyes, and sighed whenever they met, and once she was heard to say, “I wash my hands of them.”

The parish, however, did not wash its hands of them. The farmers’ wives began to talk, and the milliner began to nod, and the curate began to smile, and Mr. Lydgate found that his patients were less inclined to be ill. He therefore resolved to be very busy indeed, and Miss Danvers resolved to read very hard, and they both resolved to be very civil to Mrs. Ellingham, and to avoid her as much as possible.

In time, Mrs. Ellingham recovered her spirits, and found a new object for her benevolence in the curate, who was a young man of five-and-twenty, with a very small stipend and a very large family.
