The station stood on a low spit of sand, a mere finger of the mainland thrust out into the shallow, brownish water of the strait, and consisted of two houses, a ramshackle wooden godown, and a flagstaff that was never used. Behind it the jungle rose, dark and impenetrable, a wall of vegetable life that seemed to breathe a damp and heavy silence upon the clearing. To the north, across the water, lay the faint, blue outline of the other islands, remote and indifferent. The heat was a constant presence, a weight upon the shoulders, and the light, whether the fierce, white glare of noon or the sullen, coppery glow of the late afternoon, had a quality of hopelessness in it, as if it illuminated a world forgotten by time.

Here lived Mr. Elias Gedge, a trader, and the Reverend Octavius Prout, a missionary of a small and struggling society. They were the sole white men on the island, and their relationship was one of those strange, unnatural growths which flourish in the barren soil of isolation. They despised each other with a perfect and unwearying contempt. Mr. Gedge, a thick-set, silent man with a face the colour and texture of a dried leaf, regarded the missionary as a meddlesome fool, a purveyor of impossible comforts to the natives, who thereby grew lazy and insolent and neglected the honest business of gathering rattans and gutta-percha. The Reverend Prout, a lean, tall, and vehement person, whose pale eyes burned with a steady and uncharitable flame, saw in the trader a man of wrath, a trafficker in the souls of men, whose very presence was a scandal and a stumbling-block to the work of the Kingdom.

Their days were passed in a profound and mutual silence, broken only by the necessary civilities of a shared existence. They took their meals apart. They sat on their respective verandahs in the long evenings, watching the fireflies stitch the darkness with threads of cold light, and each was acutely, almost painfully, conscious of the other’s presence. It was a companionship of the damned, a partnership in a purgatory of their own making.

It was into this stagnant and bitter peace that the first intimation of disaster came. The supply boat, a small, wallowing schooner that plied between the island and the distant port of Labuan, was overdue. It was a thing that happened. The winds were fickle, the currents treacherous, and the captain a man of an easy and procrastinating habit. But the days passed, and then the weeks, and the horizon remained an empty, glittering line of blue. A new and profounder silence settled upon the station. The silence of their mutual dislike was now underscored by the greater, more menacing silence of the sea.

Their stores, never plentiful, began to dwindle. The flour went first, then the salt pork, and finally the sugar. They were reduced to a diet of rice, dried fish, and the bitter, dark coffee of the islands. They ate it in their separate houses, but the consciousness of the other’s hunger, of the other’s silent, calculating scrutiny of their common predicament, was a new and corrosive element in their hatred. It was no longer a matter of principle; it had become a question of survival.

One evening, Gedge, who had been standing by the water’s edge, his hands deep in his pockets, staring at the empty sea, turned and walked slowly towards the missionary’s verandah. Prout was seated there in a rattan chair, a book open on his knees, though he was not reading. He looked up, his pale eyes wary.

“The boat is not coming,” Gedge said. His voice was flat, without inflection, as if he were stating a fact of no particular consequence, such as the colour of the sky or the height of the tide.

Prout closed the book with a deliberate and careful movement. “I have observed the fact,” he replied, his voice thin and dry. “It is a visitation. A chastisement, perhaps, for the laxity of our respective flocks. Or for our own.”

Gedge gave a short, ugly laugh. “Visitation or no, we must eat. The rice will not last for ever. There are the natives on the other side of the point. They have yams and plantains. They have a few pigs.”

“And they have their souls, Mr. Gedge,” the missionary said, with a flash of his old fire. “Souls which you have done your best to imperil by your trade in opium and your traffic in their simple wants. I will not have you bartering the last shreds of our Christian decency for a handful of their food.”

“Decency?” Gedge echoed, and there was a sudden, dangerous quiet in his tone. “You talk to me of decency, you, who sit here and pray while your belly is as empty as a drum. You will not have me barter? Then you will starve. And I will starve with you. Is that your Christian charity? To die in a state of grace, with your guts sticking to your backbone, and the natives you came to save dancing on your grave?”

The missionary’s face had grown very pale. He rose from his chair, his tall figure trembling with a passion that was not all spiritual. “You are a blasphemer, sir, and a fool. You understand nothing. You see only the flesh and the devil’s own handiwork. You do not see the hand of God in this trial.”

“I see the hand of an idle sea-captain,” Gedge retorted, “and the emptiness of my own store-cupboard. I am a practical man, Mr. Prout. I deal in facts. The fact is that we are two white men on a God-forsaken island with nothing to eat. The fact is that I can get food from the natives, and you cannot, or will not. And the fact is that you would rather see us both rot than have me succeed where your prayers have failed.”

He turned on his heel and walked away, leaving the missionary standing rigid and speechless on the verandah. That night, Gedge did not sleep. He lay in his bed, listening to the familiar sounds of the night, the whine of mosquitoes, the distant, mournful cry of a bird, the lap of the water against the sand. And for the first time, he felt a sensation that was not hatred, but a cold and creeping fear. It was not the fear of starvation, of which he had a healthy and practical dread, but a fear of the man in the other house. He saw him, in his mind’s eye, not as a rival or a fool, but as a thing of unyielding and implacable principle, a man who would let the world burn to prove a point of doctrine. And in that moment, Gedge understood that he was alone with a fanatic, and that the jungle and the sea were less to be feared than the pale, burning eyes of the Reverend Octavius Prout.

The weeks that followed were a slow descent into a new and more terrible intimacy. They spoke no more of the boat, or of food, or of God. They spoke hardly at all. But their silences were now charged with a different electricity. They watched each other. Gedge, from his verandah, would see Prout emerge from his house at dawn, gaunt and hollow-eyed, to pace the clearing with a slow, mechanical stride, his lips moving soundlessly. Prout, from his window, would observe Gedge squatting by the fire, methodically boiling his ration of rice, his face a mask of sullen endurance. They were like two prisoners in a cell, each the other’s jailer and the other’s only hope of a kind of company.

One afternoon, Gedge, driven by a hunger that was a physical pain, took his old fowling-piece and went into the jungle. He returned at dusk, exhausted and empty-handed, his clothes torn by thorns. As he came into the clearing, he saw the missionary standing by the flagstaff, his hands clasped behind his back. Prout did not turn.

“There is nothing,” Gedge said, his voice a hoarse whisper. “Not a bird, not a monkey. The jungle is dead.”

The missionary turned slowly. His face was skull-like in the fading light. “The jungle is not dead, Mr. Gedge,” he said, and his voice had a strange, hollow resonance. “It is merely empty. As we are empty. As your life has been empty. It is a mirror.”

Gedge felt a chill that had nothing to do with the evening air. He wanted to strike the man, to break that pale, accusing mask, but he lacked the strength. He simply stood there, swaying slightly, and stared.

“I have been thinking,” the missionary continued, his gaze fixed on something beyond Gedge, something in the darkening line of the trees. “We have been set here, you and I, to work out a great truth. You with your ledgers and your rattans, I with my prayers and my tracts. And we have both failed. We have failed because we were not alone. We were always two. And now, in this extremity, we are more than ever two, and the truth is hidden from us.”

He paused, and a strange, almost beatific smile touched his lips. “But the truth is simple. It is only when we are one that we can be saved. Not one in spirit, Mr. Gedge, no. One in the flesh. One in the acceptance of our common, sinful, perishing humanity. You must come to my house tonight. We will share what we have. We will eat the last of the rice together. And we will pray.”

Gedge stared at him, his mind a blank. The offer was so unexpected, so monstrously out of character, that he could not comprehend it. It was a trap, he thought, a final, subtle cruelty. But as he looked into the missionary’s eyes, he saw no mockery there, only a profound and terrifying earnestness. He saw a man who had been broken by the silence and the hunger, and who had rebuilt himself, in the crucible of his own despair, into something new and strange and perhaps more dangerous than before.

He did not answer. He turned and walked back to his own house, his heart beating a slow, heavy tattoo against his ribs. The offer hung in the air between them, a challenge and a threat. To accept it was to surrender something he could not name; to refuse it was to embrace the slow, certain death of the body and, perhaps, of the soul. He sat in the darkness of his room, listening to the sea, and the silence of the island seemed to press in upon him, vast and without pity, waiting for his decision.
