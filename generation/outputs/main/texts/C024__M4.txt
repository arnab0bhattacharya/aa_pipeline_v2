The carrier’s cart set him down at the crossroads where the Ipswich road met the lane into Ashwell Thorpe, and for a moment he stood alone in the ringing quiet, his portmanteau at his feet, and listened to a silence he had forgotten the shape of. In New South Wales there had always been some sound—the lowing of cattle, the crack of a stockwhip, the dry rustle of the gums—but here the quiet was a wet, green quiet, broken only by the far-off complaint of a pewit and the tick of a hedge-sparrow in the thorn. The air smelled of damp earth and wood-smoke and something older still, the sweetish rot of last autumn’s leaves. He was thirty-one years old, and he had come home a rich man.

His name was Daniel Sparrow, and he had left Ashwell Thorpe twelve years before with nothing but a bundle and a hunger that would not let him sleep. Now he wore a good broadcloth coat and a watch-chain of Australian gold, and the boots on his feet had cost more than the whole of his father’s cottage. He had imagined this return a thousand times, in the long hot nights on the diggings and later in the cramped Sydney counting-house where he had learned to turn a profit from wool and land. He had imagined the looks on their faces. He had not imagined this—the emptiness of the lane, the closed doors, the way the village seemed to draw its skirts aside from him as he walked.

He came first to the cottage where his mother had lived, and found it empty, the thatch gone to moss, the windows boarded. A woman he did not know—a Mrs. Pettit, she said, from the next parish—told him over her gate that old Mrs. Sparrow had died four winters past, and that the cottage had stood empty since. “You’ll be the son,” she said, and looked at his coat and his chain, and her mouth went thin. “They said you’d made a fortune. They said you’d forgot us.” He did not answer. He walked on.

At the Bell and Anchor he found his oldest friend, Tom Ruddick, sitting alone in the corner with a pot of small beer and a face like a closed fist. Tom had been his fellow poacher, his fellow runaway, the boy who had held the sack while Daniel took the eggs from the keeper’s pheasant-nest. Now Tom was a widower with three children and a cough that shook his shoulders, and he looked up at Daniel with eyes that were not glad. “You came back,” he said, as though it were a fault. “I heard you were rich. I heard you’d a house in Sydney with a marble floor.” Daniel sat down and tried to talk of old times, of the night they had been chased across the water-meadow by the keeper’s dog, of the stolen apples and the cider they had drunk behind the church. Tom listened, and drank, and said little, and at last he said, “It’s all very well for you to laugh at it now. You got out. I stayed. My Mary’s dead, and my boy’s in the workhouse at Bury, and you sit there with your gold chain and your fine stories.” He stood up, and his chair scraped the flagstones. “I’d be ashamed to be you, Daniel Sparrow, if I had the time for it.” And he went out into the rain.

Daniel sat for a long while in the empty taproom. He thought of the letters he had not written, the money he had not sent, the years he had spent in the bright hard light of another country, telling himself he would come back when he was rich enough to be forgiven. He had not understood that forgiveness is not a thing you can buy with a watch-chain.

He walked out of the village then, along the lane that led to the common, and it was there, at the edge of the green where the schoolhouse stood, that he saw the light in the window. It was a small light, a candle behind a curtain, and it drew him the way a fire draws a man out of the snow. He knocked, and after a moment the door opened, and there stood Miss Honour Gedge, who had taught him his letters when he was six years old and she was a young woman with a stern face and a cane she never used.

She was old now—sixty at least—and her hair was white under her cap, but her eyes were the same, grey and clear and unafraid. She looked at him for a long moment, and then she said, “Daniel Sparrow. You’ve grown.” And she stepped aside and let him in.

Her parlour was small and warm, with a fire in the grate and a kettle on the hob and shelves of books that she had read so often the spines were cracked. She made tea, and set out a plate of seed-cake, and she did not ask him about Australia or his fortune or his watch-chain. She asked him if he remembered the poem she had made him learn, the one about the daffodils, and when he said he did, she made him say it, and he stumbled over the words and she corrected him, and for a moment he was six years old again, and the world was simple.

“They don’t like me here,” he said at last. “Tom Ruddick as good as called me a traitor. The rest look at me as though I’d brought the plague.”

Miss Gedge poured more tea. “Tom Ruddick is a bitter man, and bitterness is a kind of grief,” she said. “He lost his wife, and his son, and his hope, and you come back with all three. You cannot blame him for not knowing what to do with you.” She looked at him over the rim of her cup. “And you cannot blame the village for being suspicious of a man who left and did not write. They are not ashamed of you, Daniel. They are ashamed of themselves for having loved you and been forgotten.”

He was silent for a long time. The fire crackled. The wind pressed against the window.

“I wanted to come back,” he said. “I wanted to come back and be the boy they knew. But that boy is dead, isn’t he? I killed him in Sydney.”

Miss Gedge set down her cup. “The boy is not dead,” she said. “He is sitting in my parlour, drinking my tea, and he still cannot say ‘daffodil’ without putting the emphasis on the wrong syllable. You have grown, Daniel, and you have made money, and you have learned things I cannot teach you. But you are still the boy who sat at that desk and learned to read. That is the boy I am glad to see. The rest of them will come round, in time, or they will not. That is their business, not yours.”

She rose and went to the shelf and took down a book, a small worn volume of Cowper’s poems, and she put it into his hands. “You gave me nothing when you left,” she said. “You owe me nothing now. But if you want to be forgiven, Daniel, you must first forgive yourself, and then you must stay. Not for a week. Not for a season. Stay until they are tired of being angry with you. Stay until Tom Ruddick lets you buy him a pint. Stay until the village remembers that you are one of us.”

He looked at the book in his hands, and then at the old woman who had taught him to read, and he felt something loosen in his chest that had been tight for twelve years. Outside, the rain had stopped, and the moon was coming up over the common, and the village of Ashwell Thorpe lay quiet and dark and waiting. He did not know if he would stay. But he knew, for the first time since he had stepped off the carrier’s cart, that he could.
