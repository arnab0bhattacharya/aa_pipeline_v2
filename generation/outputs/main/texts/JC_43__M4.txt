The affair began, as such affairs will, not with a proposition but with a silence—a silence which Señor Esteban Varga, chief of the customs guard at the port of San Cristóbal de la Ribera, found himself observing across the width of his own small office, and which he could not, for all his twenty-two years of service, bring himself to break. The man who sat opposite him, one Ignacio Ríos, a ship’s broker of doubtful antecedents and a smile of great sweetness, had laid upon the table a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. He had not opened it. He had merely placed his brown hand upon it, and waited, and the waiting had in it something of the quality of the river fogs that came up at evening—a soft, pervasive, obscuring thing that entered every corner of the room and made the lamp upon the desk burn with a diminished and uncertain flame.

“You will understand, señor,” Ríos said at last, “that I speak as a friend of your family. The señora’s health—it is not what it was. A man in your position, with your responsibilities, with your—how shall I say?—your scruples, which are well known and everywhere respected, must find the world a hard place. The world does not respect scruples, señor. The world respects only results.”

Varga did not answer. He was a man of middle years, lean and dry, with the face of one who had spent his life peering into the sun-glare of the anchorage, and his eyes, which were of a pale, faded blue, had in them the permanent squint of the man who reads manifests by a swinging lantern. He had been honest all his life. It was not a virtue he had chosen; it was rather a condition, like the colour of his hair or the shape of his hands, and it had made him poor, and the poverty had been patient and uncomplaining, and his wife, María, had borne it without reproach until the illness had come, and then the poverty had ceased to be a condition and had become a weight.

“The ship is the *Estrella del Sur*,” Ríos continued, in the tone of one discussing a matter of no consequence. “She sails at dawn. Her papers are in order—I have seen to that—but her cargo is not. It is a small thing, señor. A few cases. Some bales. Nothing that would trouble the conscience of a saint. And the owners, they are men of substance, men who understand that a public servant of your merit is not paid according to his merit. They wish to make a—a gesture. A recognition. It is not a bribe, señor. It is an acknowledgment of your years of faithful service.”

He unrolled the oilcloth. The gold lay in neat stacks, and the lamplight, which had been so uncertain, seemed to gather itself and leap upon the metal with a kind of eagerness, so that the room was suddenly full of a yellow, shifting radiance, and the shadows in the corners drew back as if abashed. Varga looked at the gold. He did not touch it. He looked at it for a long time, and in his mind there was no calculation, no weighing of consequences, but only a great and weary wonder that the thing should have come to him at last, after all these years, and that it should have come in this form, so quiet, so reasonable, so like the small, sad transactions of ordinary life.

“How much?” he asked, and his voice was strange to him, dry and thin.

“Enough,” said Ríos, “to take the señora to the capital. Enough for the doctors. Enough for the voyage, and the hotel, and the care. Enough to make an old man’s last years easy. And more, señor. Much more.”

Varga rose and went to the window. The night outside was black and soft, and the harbour lay below him, a darkness within a darkness, broken here and there by the riding lights of the ships, which swung and dipped like the souls of drowned men beckoning from the deep. He could hear the water lapping against the piles of the wharf, a small, persistent sound, and the distant creak of a hawser, and the low, melancholy note of a steamer’s whistle from beyond the point. The air was heavy with the smell of coffee and hides and the faint, sweetish odour of the river, and it seemed to him that the whole night was waiting, breathless and patient, for him to speak.

“I have been an honest man,” he said, not turning.

“It is precisely that, señor,” Ríos replied, “which makes the offer worthy of you. A dishonest man would have demanded more.”

Varga did not hear him. He was thinking of María, of the way she lay in the shuttered room, her face turned to the wall, her breathing so light that sometimes he would stop in the doorway and listen, and the fear would come up in him like a wave, cold and suffocating. He was thinking of the doctor, who had said, with the fatalism of his race, “The capital, señor. Perhaps. Who can say? But here—” and had shrugged, and the shrug had said everything. He was thinking of the gold, which lay behind him on the table, and which was not, after all, a fortune, but only a little ease, a little hope, a little time.

“The *Estrella del Sur*,” he said at last, “she is a clean ship. Her master is a German. He will not take risks.”

“The master is a German,” Ríos agreed, “but the owners are of San Cristóbal, and they are not Germans. They are practical men. They know that a ship may sail with her papers in order and her cargo—incomplete. The German will not ask. He will sign the manifest and go. It is a matter of a few hours, señor. A few hours, and the ship is over the horizon, and the gold is in your hands, and the señora is on her way to the capital.”

Varga turned from the window. The lamp upon the desk had begun to smoke, and the flame was guttering, and the gold in the oilcloth had lost its lustre and lay dull and heavy, like something dead. He looked at the man who sat there, at the sweet and patient smile, at the brown hand resting upon the table, and he felt a sudden and profound disgust, not for Ríos, but for himself, for the world, for the whole weary business of living, which demanded of a man that he should choose between his honesty and his wife’s life, and called the choice a moral one.

“And if I refuse?” he asked.

Ríos spread his hands. “Then I go to another. There are others, señor. There are always others. But I would not go to another. I would go to you. Because I know you, and I respect you, and I know that you will not refuse.”

“You know too much,” Varga said.

“I know the world, señor. That is all.”

There was a long silence. The lamp smoked and stank, and the harbour lights swung and dipped, and the water lapped against the piles, and somewhere in the town a dog began to bark, a sharp, insistent sound that seemed to come from a great distance, as if it belonged to another world, a world of simple things and clear choices, a world that had no gold in oilcloth and no sick wife in a shuttered room. Varga went to the desk and turned down the wick, and the flame leaped up, bright and steady, and the gold shone again with its yellow, shifting radiance, and he looked at it and knew that he would take it.

He did not say so. He did not say anything. He merely stood there, with his hand upon the lamp, and looked at the gold, and Ríos, who was a man of perception, rose and wrapped the oilcloth about the money and placed it in the drawer of the desk, and then he bowed, and went out, and the door closed behind him with a soft and final click.

Varga sat down in his chair. He did not open the drawer. He did not look at it. He sat with his hands upon his knees and stared at the wall, and the wall was white and bare and had upon it a small stain, shaped like a leaf, which he had never noticed before. He thought of the *Estrella del Sur*, lying out there in the darkness, her hatches open, her crew waiting, and he thought of the dawn, which was coming, and of the moment when the ship would slip her moorings and stand out to sea, and of the moment when he would go home and look at María and say nothing, and of the moment when the gold would be spent and the illness would return and there would be no more gold, and he knew that he had crossed a line from which there is no returning, and that the man who had been Esteban Varga, chief of the customs guard, honest all his life and poor all his life, was now someone else, someone he did not know, and the knowledge was not bitter, as he had expected, but only dull and heavy and very tired.

He sat there until the first grey light began to show in the window, and then he rose and went out, and the harbour was full of mist, and the ships were ghostly and indistinct, and the *Estrella del Sur* was already moving, a dark shape sliding through the grey, and he watched her until she was gone, and then he turned and walked slowly homeward through the empty streets, and the town was silent, and the morning was coming up, and he did not look back.
