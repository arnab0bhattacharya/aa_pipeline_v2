The summons had come upon me as a thunderclap from a serene sky, and even now, as I stood within the great hall of the newly-raised edifice, its echo reverberated through the chambers of my heart with a disquiet that mocked the outward pomp and jubilee. The building itself was a monument to a prosperity so sudden and so vast that it seemed to shame the slow, grey antiquity of the town that cradled it; its walls of fresh-hewn stone rose with a confident and gleaming whiteness, its windows, tall and many-paned, caught the pale, reluctant light of the Lancashire morning, and the great clock in the central tower, a masterpiece of brass and polished steel, ticked with a precision that felt to me like the very pulse of the age—an age of iron, of engines, of unrelenting forward motion, where the past was but a shadow to be trodden underfoot. And at the heart of all this splendour stood the man, Mr. Josiah Hartley, the benefactor, the founder, the idol of the hour, his broad, ruddy countenance wreathed in smiles that seemed to me, in my fevered apprehension, to hold a secret and a mockery.

My name is Thomas Ashworth, and I was, by the strange and cruel alchemy of fate, the grandson of the man whom this same Josiah Hartley had ruined. My grandfather, a weaver of honest, if narrow, means, had taken the young and penniless Hartley into his employ, had taught him the mysteries of the loom, had trusted him with the secrets of his trade; and Hartley, with a cunning that was the more monstrous for its patient dissimulation, had used that trust to steal his designs, to undercut his prices, and to build the first foundation of his colossal fortune upon the broken fortunes of his master. My grandfather had died, not of want, for his pride forbade him to beg, but of a broken heart, a slow and silent decay that ate away his spirit as the canker eats the rose. The story had been dinned into my ears from childhood, a bitter inheritance of shame and sorrow, and I had grown up with the name of Hartley as a synonym for all that was treacherous and base.

And now, by a refinement of cruelty that I could not fathom, this very man had sought me out. He had learned of my position as the schoolmaster of the town, of the small repute I had gained for learning and for the earnestness with which I endeavoured to instil into the minds of my scholars a love of truth and honour; and he had sent for me, with a show of condescension that galled my soul, to request that I would deliver the address at the opening of his new school—a school which he designed, he said, as a lasting monument to his own benevolence, and as a means of educating the children of the poor, of which he had himself once been the humblest. The invitation had been couched in terms so flattering, so full of a seeming respect for my abilities, that I had been for a moment deceived; but the truth had flashed upon me with a sickening certainty. He wished to parade his victim before the world, to show how completely he had triumphed, to make the grandson of the man he had destroyed the mouthpiece of his own praises. It was a subtle and a devilish revenge.

Yet I had accepted. Why, I scarcely knew, save that some dark and irresistible fascination drew me towards the man, some wild hope that in his presence I might read the secret of his guilt, might pierce the armour of his self-complacency, and find, beneath the gilded surface, the worm of remorse that I was certain must gnaw at his heart. I had prepared my address with a feverish diligence, choosing my words with a care that was almost agonizing, seeking to weave into the fabric of my oratory a thread of truth so fine, so delicate, that it should escape the notice of the vulgar crowd, yet sting the conscience of the guilty man. I would speak of the sacredness of early trust, of the meanness of ingratitude, of the eternal laws of justice that, though they may sleep, can never die.

The hour was now at hand. The hall was filled with the magnates of the town, with the gentry of the surrounding country, with the clergy and the magistrates, all arrayed in their bravery, all eager to do homage to the idol of the day. The air was heavy with the scent of hot-house flowers and the murmur of obsequious voices. I stood upon the platform, pale and trembling, my manuscript in my hand, and I felt the gaze of a thousand eyes fixed upon me, and, above all, the gaze of that one man, who sat in the place of honour, his countenance beaming with a paternal benignity that seemed to me the very mask of hell.

He rose to introduce me. His voice was rich, full, and sonorous, the voice of a man who had been accustomed to command, and who had learned to use his tongue as an instrument of power. He spoke of his own humble origin, of the struggles of his youth, of the debt he owed to the kindly patronage he had received—a debt, he said, which he had ever sought to repay by a life devoted to the service of his fellow-men. He spoke of the school as the crowning work of his life, as a thank-offering to the Providence that had prospered him. And then he turned to me, and his eye met mine, and for a moment—a moment that seemed an eternity—I saw something flicker in its depths, something that was not benevolence, something that was cold and hard and watchful. He spoke my name, and the name of my grandfather, and a murmur of sympathy ran through the assembly, for the old story was known, though the truth of it was not. He spoke of the friendship that had once bound our families, of the happy days when he had worked at my grandfather's loom, of the sorrow he had felt at his death, of his earnest desire to atone for the past by his kindness to the grandson. It was a masterly performance; the tears stood in the eyes of many a soft-hearted matron, and I saw even the stern old magistrate in the front row blow his nose with a great and sonorous trumpeting.

Then I rose. My heart was beating as though it would burst its bonds; my limbs trembled beneath me; a mist swam before my eyes. I looked at the sea of faces, at the glittering array of wealth and fashion, at the man who had wrought my family's ruin, and who now sat there, serene and smiling, the very incarnation of successful villainy. And I felt a sudden and terrible conviction that I could not do it. I could not stand there and speak smooth words of praise and gratitude; I could not lend my voice to the chorus of adulation; I could not be the accomplice of his hypocrisy. A wild and desperate courage seized me. I laid aside my manuscript. I would speak from the fullness of my heart.

"Mr. Hartley," I began, and my voice, though low, was heard in the profound silence that had fallen upon the assembly, "and you, my friends and fellow-townsmen, who have honoured me by your presence this day, I am but ill-fitted for the task that has been assigned to me. I am a schoolmaster, and my trade is with words; but there are moments when words seem but a hollow mockery, when the heart is too full for utterance, and the tongue cleaves to the roof of the mouth. Such a moment is this. I look around me, and I see the evidences of a prosperity that is the wonder of the age; I see a man who has risen from the humblest station to the highest, who has amassed a fortune that might ransom a king, and who has devoted a portion of that fortune to the noble purpose of educating the children of the poor. It is a splendid spectacle; it is one that should stir the heart of every lover of his kind. But I would ask you, my friends, to consider what is the foundation upon which this prosperity is built? Is it the solid rock of integrity, of honour, of fair dealing? Or is it the shifting sand of expediency, of self-interest, of a success that has been purchased at the price of a fellow-creature's ruin?"

A shudder ran through the assembly. I saw the smile freeze upon Hartley's lips. I saw the faces of the great men of the town turn pale and grave. I heard a low, angry murmur, and I knew that I had gone too far. But I was reckless now; the pent-up passion of years had burst its barriers, and I could not, if I would, have checked the torrent of my speech.

"I speak," I continued, "of a man who was once poor and friendless, and who found a home and a welcome in the house of one who trusted him, who loved him, who treated him as a son. I speak of a man who repaid that trust with treachery, that love with hatred, that hospitality with a foul and deliberate robbery. I speak of my grandfather, who died of a broken heart, and of the man who broke it. That man is Josiah Hartley, the benefactor of this town, the founder of this school, the idol of this hour. And I stand here, the grandson of his victim, to denounce him in the presence of you all as a hypocrite, a traitor, and a thief!"

The words rang out like a pistol-shot. For a moment there was a silence so profound that I could hear the ticking of the great clock in the tower, and the beating of my own heart. Then the storm broke. A cry of horror and indignation rose from the assembly; the women shrieked; the men started to their feet; some rushed towards the platform as though to seize me, others surrounded Hartley, who had risen, and who stood, pale as death, his lips moving as if in speech, but no sound issuing from them. I saw his eyes fixed upon me with a look of implacable hatred, and I knew that I had made an enemy who would never forgive, who would never forget. But I was conscious only of a wild and exulting joy, a sense of relief so intense that it was almost pain. I had spoken the truth; I had done my duty; I had vindicated the memory of my grandfather, and I had torn the mask from the face of the hypocrite. What mattered it that I had ruined my own prospects, that I had incurred the enmity of the powerful, that I had perhaps brought upon myself the vengeance of a man whose resources were boundless? I had been true to myself, and that was more than all the world beside.

I turned and left the platform, and as I passed through the crowd, I heard a voice—I knew not whose—cry out, "Shame! Shame!" and another, "The man is mad!" and another, "He has spoken the truth!" And I went out into the grey and misty morning, and I breathed the free air, and I felt that I was a man, and not a slave.
