The gas-jet above Elias Thorne’s bench hissed a low, steady note, the only sound in the damp Clerkenwell cellar save for the familiar ticking of a hundred imprisoned seconds. Rain lashed the cobblestones of Saffron Passage above, filtering down through the iron grating as a fine, cold mist. Elias held a pair of newly crafted brass tweezers, his left eye squinting through a jeweler’s loupe at the intricate escapement of a lady’s half-hunter. He had been a watchmaker in this subterranean warren for thirty years, servicing the respectable tradesmen of the city, and in all that time, the rhythm of his life had been governed by the predictable, ordered progression of gears. 

The heavy oak door at the top of the stairs groaned. Elias did not look up. He heard the heavy tread of boots descending, the scrape of a match striking, and the acrid scent of a cheap cheroot polluting the sanctity of his workshop. When the footsteps halted at the bottom step, Elias finally lowered his tweezers and removed the loupe. 

The man standing in the gloom was tall, wrapped in a dark, sodden greatcoat that dripped steadily onto Elias’s scrubbed floorboards. His face was obscured by the brim of a dripping wideawake hat, but the gaslight caught the pale, unforgiving line of his jaw and the glint of a heavy gold signet ring on his right hand. 

“Mr. Thorne,” the man said. It was not a question. The voice was low, roughened by London soot, carrying the dull vowels of the East End dressed in the consonants of forced civility. 

“I am he,” Elias replied, dabbing his thin fingers on a stained linen rag. “The shop is closed. If you require a watch cleaned, you may call upon my boy in the morning.”

“I haven’t come for a cleaning.” The stranger stepped into the room, his heavy boots reverberating against the hollow floor. From the depths of his greatcoat, he produced an object wrapped in a piece of chamois leather. He placed it on the bench, pushing it toward Elias with a blunt, calloused finger. 

Elias picked up the chamois bundle. The weight of it was immediately wrong for a pocket watch—too heavy, too dense. He unfolded the leather. Inside lay a mechanism of breathtaking, terrible ingenuity. It was not cased in silver or gold, but housed in a crude, blackened steel frame, clearly built to be secreted within a larger, hollowed-out object. There was no watch face, no hands to mark the passing minutes. Instead, there were two mainsprings of unusually thick gauge, wound tight and locked by a complex series of levers. At the heart of the device sat a cylinder studded with sharp, protruding pins, aligned perfectly above a pair of heavy, sharpened strikers. 

Elias did not need to see it in motion to know its purpose. He had spent his life studying the release of tension. The thick mainsprings, the delayed release levers, the pin-cylinder, and the strikers—this was not a device to measure time. It was a device to consume it. It was an infernal machine, designed to be hidden within a parcel, a gift, or a hollowed-out book, ticking silently until the slow unwinding of the springs released the strikers into a percussion cap or a vial of acid. It was a bomb, crafted to murder from afar. 

The silence in the cellar stretched, broken only by the persistent ticking of the watches on the shelves. Elias looked up at the stranger, his heart knocking a ragged rhythm against his ribs. 

“It’s broken,” the stranger said, exhaling a plume of smoke. “The cylinder jams. The pins don’t align with the strikers when the secondary spring unwinds. I need it functioning by tomorrow eve.”

Elias stared at the device, his mind rapidly mapping the mechanical failure. The pin-cylinder’s arbor was slightly bent, likely from being dropped, causing it to bind against its bridge. It would take perhaps two hours to dismantle, straighten, and reassemble. It was a trivial repair for a man of his skill. 

“I do not deal in such things,” Elias said, his voice barely above a whisper. He pushed the chamois back across the bench. 

The stranger did not take it. Instead, he reached into his coat and withdrew a small canvas purse. He loosened the drawstring and tipped the contents onto the bench. Gold sovereigns spilled across the scarred wood, clinking softly against the brass tools. Elias counted them instinctively. There were twenty pounds. It was more money than he saw in six months of repairing broken escapements and replacing cracked crystals. 

“You’re the best in Clerkenwell, Thorne,” the stranger said, his tone flat. “I’m told you can fix anything with gears. The money is yours, whether you do the job or not. But I’ll expect the machine tomorrow night, in perfect working order.”

Elias looked from the gold to the device. The stranger turned and began to climb the stairs, his heavy boots echoing in the narrow stairwell. The door at the top slammed shut, leaving Elias alone with the hissing gas-jet, the ticking shelves, and the silence. 

He sat heavily on his stool, the damp of the cellar seeping into his bones. Twenty pounds. It was a fortune. It would pay off the landlord, buy new tools, perhaps even move him above ground to a proper storefront on the Strand. But the device sitting on his bench was a promise of death. He did not know the target—perhaps a ruthless magistrate, perhaps a rival in the criminal underworld, perhaps a politician. It did not matter. His hands would be the ones to set the pins aligning with the strikers. 

Elias considered his options. He could refuse, leave the money, and throw the device into the River Fleet. But the stranger knew where he lived, where he worked. If the machine failed to operate tomorrow night, Elias had no doubt that men with less mechanical inclinations would return to Saffron Passage. He could go to the police. But the police would ask questions. They would confiscate his tools, shut down his shop, and drag him into the light of a courtroom. He would be an informant, a target for every cutthroat in the city. And even then, the stranger would likely vanish, only to find another, less scrupulous craftsman. 

Elias picked up a pair of pliers. The metal was cold against his skin. He looked at the sovereigns scattered across the bench, gleaming softly in the gaslight. They represented life, comfort, survival. He looked at the infernal machine. It represented the exact opposite. 

For a long time, he sat motionless, listening to the rain. Then, with a slow, deliberate motion, he swept the gold sovereigns into his palm and dropped them into his coat pocket. He pulled the chamois bundle toward him. 

He lit a smaller spirit lamp on his bench and selected a fine screwdriver. With practiced ease, he began to dismantle the device. He removed the bridge, exposing the jammed pin-cylinder. The arbor was indeed bent, the metal fatigued. He carefully lifted the cylinder out and examined it under the loupe. 

He could fix it. He could make it perfect. He could ensure that when the mainspring unwound, the pins would rotate smoothly, dropping the strikers with flawless precision. But as he looked at the sharp little pins, an idea began to form in his mind, a quiet rebellion only a watchmaker could devise. 

Elias picked up a tiny file. He would repair the mechanism, yes, but he would alter the timing of the secondary lever. He would file down the escape tooth by a fraction of a millimeter. To the untrained eye, the device would look and function perfectly. But when the spring finally unwound, the tension would release a fraction of a second early. The cylinder would rotate, but the pins would slip past the strikers, missing them entirely. The mechanism would click, uselessly, harmlessly, into the void. 

It would not explode. It would simply fail. 

Elias smiled, a thin, tight expression that did not reach his eyes. He would take the stranger’s gold, and he would deliver the machine. But he would also deliver a silent prayer for whoever was meant to receive that parcel. He set to work, the quiet ticking of the watches on the shelves keeping time with the steady, careful rhythm of his file. In the morning, the rain would stop, the gas-jet would be extinguished, and the machine would be returned, a perfect, flawless, useless instrument of mercy.
