It was upon a raw and gusty evening in the late autumn of 1893 that my friend, Mr. Julian Croft, the mining engineer, made his way to my rooms in Truro. The rain was falling in a fine, persistent drizzle, and the wind, coming up from the Atlantic, had the cold, salt tang of the sea about it. Croft was a man of precise habits, and to see him thus, with the damp still clinging to his heavy tweed coat and a look of unusual animation in his keen grey eyes, was a matter of some surprise to me.

“Watson,” he began, without preamble, for he had no time for the niceties of social intercourse when a problem was upon him, “I have come across a matter at Wheal Annelise which I find myself unable to explain. It is a puzzle of the most singular kind, and it has a flavour of the uncanny which I confess I do not like.”

I begged him to be seated and to tell me his story. He took the chair opposite the fire, stretched out his long legs to the warmth, and began his account.

“As you know, Sir Giles Trelawny has engaged me to survey the old Annelise workings. The mine has been sealed for twenty years, ever since the great collapse in the lower adit which cost three men their lives. The old man, Sir Giles’s father, would not have it reopened. But the son is of a different mould. With the price of tin what it is, he believes there is a fortune still to be made, and he has sent for me to see if it is to be got at without undue risk.”

“This morning, I took my lamp and descended into the main shaft. The air was foul, as one would expect, and the timbers were rotten in places. But my work took me into the old workings, the galleries that were abandoned long before the final disaster. It was in one of these—a narrow passage which the miners called the ‘Serpent’s Run’—that I made my first discovery.”

He paused, taking a pipe from his pocket and beginning to fill it with a methodical care that I knew was characteristic of him when he was marshalling his thoughts.

“The Serpent’s Run was sealed, you understand. A wall of stone and mortar had been built across its entrance after a minor fall. The mortar is old and crumbling, but the seal is unbroken. No one has passed through that wall for two decades. And yet, upon the rough stone floor, no more than ten feet beyond it, I found this.”

From his waistcoat pocket, he produced a small object and laid it upon my mantelpiece. It was a nub of candle wax, perhaps an inch long, and of a peculiar pale yellow colour.

“Tallow?” I suggested.

“No,” said he, with a sharp shake of his head. “Beeswax. And of a quality I have not seen in this county. The common miner uses a tallow dip or a cheap candle. This is something else entirely. Moreover, the floor around it was disturbed. The dust, which lies an inch thick in those galleries, had been swept aside by the passage of feet. Not many, I should say, but more than one. And the tracks were fresh.”

I must have looked my astonishment, for he leaned forward, his eyes gleaming in the firelight.

“That is not all, Watson. My curiosity being thoroughly aroused, I determined to make a closer inspection of the Serpent’s Run. I found a place where the old sealing wall was weakest, a small aperture near the roof, and with some difficulty I squeezed through. The air within was better than I had any right to expect, for there is evidently a draught, which suggests another means of ingress. I followed the gallery for some two hundred yards. It is a treacherous place, with loose rock overhead and a drop into a flooded stope on one side. To negotiate it in the dark would be impossible.”

“And there was light?”

“There was evidence of it. I found more of the wax upon the ledges, and once, on a timber prop, the black smear of a lamp-glass. But it was at the very end of the run, where it opens out into a small chamber, that I found the thing which has brought me to you tonight.”

He took a long draw upon his pipe before continuing.

“The chamber is perhaps thirty feet across. In the centre is the stump of an old winch, and all about are the signs of the ancient workings. But leaning against the far wall, in perfect condition and utterly out of place, was a ladder. It is a fine piece of work, Watson, made of seasoned ash with rungs of iron, and it is as new as the day it was made. I have spent the better part of a week in the mines of this district, and I can tell you there is not a ladder like it in Cornwall. It is a professional tool, not the sort of makeshift affair a man would cobble together for himself.”

“You believe the mine is being worked?” I asked, the conclusion being obvious.

“I am certain of it,” he replied. “Someone is in Wheal Annelise. They are using a method of entry unknown to Sir Giles or his agents. They are working a section of the mine which has been sealed for twenty years, and they are doing so with a quiet efficiency that speaks of skill and purpose. They are not common thieves, for a thief would not leave such a valuable ladder unattended. They are miners. Good ones.”

“But for what purpose? The mine is legally the property of Sir Giles Trelawny.”

“Precisely. And that is what makes the matter so delicate. If I report this to Sir Giles, he will send his bailiffs down, and there will be a confrontation. If these men are desperate, it could end in bloodshed. On the other hand, if they have discovered a rich pocket of ore, Sir Giles has a right to it.”

He rose from his chair and began to pace the room, his hands clasped behind his back.

“No,” he said, after a moment. “The direct approach is the wrong one. We must observe before we act. I propose to go back tonight, after dark. I shall take a good lamp and my revolver. I mean to find their point of entry and discover who these phantom miners are and what it is they are after. It is a risk, I do not deny it, but it is a calculated one. Will you accompany me, Watson? A second pair of eyes is always an advantage, and I confess I should be glad of your company.”

The proposition was not without its dangers, but the mystery had taken a firm hold upon my imagination. To see my friend Croft in action was always a study in methodical courage, and the prospect was more than I could resist.

“I shall come, of course,” said I.

“Good man!” he exclaimed, clapping me on the shoulder. “We shall meet at the crossroads above the mine at eleven o’clock. Bring a stout stick and wear your oldest clothes. We may have some climbing to do.”

With that, he took his leave, disappearing into the wet Cornish night as suddenly as he had arrived. I sat for some time by the fire, the yellow nub of beeswax on my mantelpiece serving as a silent testament to the strange affair. The wind howled about the eaves, and I could not help but feel that we were on the verge of an adventure far more perilous than a simple inspection of a disused tin mine. The quiet efficiency of these unknown miners, the strange ladder, the sealed gallery—these were pieces of a puzzle whose final shape I could not begin to guess, but which I felt certain would prove to be both bizarre and dangerous.
