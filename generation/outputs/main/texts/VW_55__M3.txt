The light fell in broad, slanting planes across the counters, cutting the gloom of the department store into sharp, glittering wedges. It illuminated the folds of the silks—pale blues, greens like the depth of a stagnant pond, and sudden, arterial reds—so that they seemed to pulse, to breathe with a life quite separate from the fine ladies who prodded them with gloved fingers. Edith stood behind the pyramid of folded cashmere, her hands moving automatically, smoothing the edges, pressing the fibres flat. The hum of Oxford Street pressed against the plate-glass windows, a muffled, ceaseless roar that was the bass note to the chatter inside. Voices floated, detached; a woman asking for something in a shade of fawn, another complaining of the quality of the stitching; and Edith heard them, and answered them, but her mind was somewhere else entirely. It was in the dusty, resinous air of the Parish Hall, where the gramophone had stuck on a single phrase, repeating it over and over until the notes seemed to have entered her very blood, and she had been dancing with a young man whose name was Oliver. Oliver. The syllables dropped into the mechanical clatter of the shop floor like a stone into a deep well, sending ripples outward, disturbing the neat arrangement of her thoughts.

He had held her very lightly, as if she were made of glass, and his eyes, which were a sort of grey-brown, like the pebbles on the beach at Margate before the tide comes in, had looked past her shoulder into the dim corner of the room. She had not known what he was thinking. One never knew what they were thinking; they moved in a different element, behind a pane of glass, smiling, saying words that sounded right but signified nothing. “Do you often go to the Palais?” he had asked, and she had said, “Sometimes,” which was a lie, for she never went, hating the noise and the smell of stale beer, but she had wanted to seem to him a person of the world, someone who danced frequently, who was accustomed to this sort of thing. And now, standing in the shop, folding the same cashmere for the hundredth time, she felt the heat of his hand on her back still, a phantom pressure that unsettled her, making her clumsy, so that she dropped a bolt of ribbon and Mrs. Gomm clicked her tongue from behind the till.

The hours passed in a succession of meaningless transactions. A lady bought four yards of velvet; a girl, hardly older than Edith herself, hesitated over a pair of silk stockings, touching them with a reverence that was almost religious. Edith watched her fingers stroke the sheer, cool fabric, and thought how strange it was that a mere covering for the legs should hold such power, such promise of transformation. The girl bought them, eventually, and went away smiling, clutching her parcel as if it contained the key to some enchanted kingdom. And Edith, left alone again, felt the flatness of her own existence settle upon her like a layer of dust.

At half past twelve she went to the staff room. It was a bleak room, high up in the building, with windows that looked out over the rooftops, a forest of chimney pots and slate, stretching away to where the haze of the city blurred the edges of the sky. The air smelled of boiled cabbage and wet coats. Girls sat in clusters on wooden chairs, eating sandwiches from paper bags, their voices rising and falling in a rhythm that was almost music, a discordant, chattering music that filled the spaces between the bites. Edith sat by the window, eating her bread and cheese slowly, tasting nothing. The cheese was hard and sharp, the bread slightly stale, but she ate it mechanically, her eyes fixed on the panorama of roofs, where a pigeon was strutting along a parapet, its breast iridescent in the thin winter sun. She thought of Oliver again. He worked in an office, he had said, somewhere near Chancery Lane; he was a clerk, or something of the sort. She imagined him bending over a desk, writing in a ledger, his grey-brown eyes narrowed in concentration, and the picture was so vivid that it seemed to her she could see the very grain of the wood, the dark smear of ink on his cuff. She wondered if he ever thought of her. Probably not. Men did not, she supposed, think of girls they had danced with once, at a parish hall where the gramophone stuck. They had other things to occupy them—important things, ledgers and figures and the grave business of commerce. She was merely a shadow that had passed across the floor of an evening, a face that had floated, for a moment, in the shifting crowd.

She went back to the counter. The afternoon dragged. The light changed, the slanting planes becoming steeper, more golden, then fading to a dull, sallow grey. The shop filled with a different sort of customer now; tired women, coming in from the street with harassed faces, dragging children by the hand. The silks seemed to lose their lustre, becoming limp and lifeless in the fading day. Edith’s feet ached. She had a blister on her left heel, and every time she shifted her weight she felt the sharp, hot sting of it, a tiny, persistent reminder of her own bodily weariness. She wanted to be out in the street, walking, feeling the cold air on her face, the hard pavement under her feet. She wanted to be moving, going somewhere, away from the smell of the shop, the chatter, the endless folding and unfolding of fabrics.

At last the hour came. She pinned on her hat, a small, dark felt hat that she had trimmed herself with a scrap of velvet, and went out through the side door into the side street. The air hit her like a wave, cold, sharp, tinged with the smell of exhaust fumes and roasted chestnuts. She walked quickly, her shoes clicking on the pavement, merging with the stream of workers pouring homeward. The buses loomed past, red and cumbersome, their windows misted, the faces inside blurred and indistinct, like faces in a dream. She walked along Oxford Street, past the illuminated shop windows, where the mannequins stood in their glass cases, frozen in attitudes of impossible grace, their painted smiles fixed and empty. The lights were coming on now, strings of bulbs outlining the facades of the buildings, turning the street into a canyon of glittering, shifting light. She felt a sort of exhilaration, a release from the stagnation of the day. She was free, for a few hours at least; free to walk, to think, to be herself, whoever that was.

And then, quite suddenly, she saw him. He was standing under the awning of a tobacconist’s shop, on the opposite side of the road, and he was not alone. There was a girl with him, a girl in a green coat with a fur collar, who was laughing, her head thrown back, her teeth showing white in the light from the shop window. Oliver was looking down at her, and he was smiling too, a smile that Edith had never seen on his face before; a smile that was intimate, private, a smile that seemed to enclose the two of them in a bubble of shared understanding, impenetrable to the outside world. He said something, and the girl laughed again, and took his arm, and they moved off together into the crowd, their figures dissolving, merging with the stream of other figures, until they were lost in the general blur of movement and colour and light.

Edith stood still on the pavement. The crowd jostled her; a man swore as he brushed past; a bus went by, showering her with a fine mist of dirty water from the gutter. She did not notice. She was looking at the spot where he had been standing, the empty pavement under the awning, the glowing window of the tobacconist’s shop with its rows of bright packets and its jars of pipe tobacco. The light seemed to have gone out of the street, or rather, it was still there, the same lights, the same buses, the same stream of hurrying people, but it had changed; it was merely light now, illumination, without meaning or magic. The cashmere, the silks, the girl with the silk stockings, the pigeon on the parapet—all of it seemed suddenly absurd, a silly, pointless masquerade. She began to walk again, slowly, her feet heavy, the blister on her heel burning with every step. The street stretched out before her, long and grey and indifferent, and she walked into it, carrying her disappointment like a stone in her chest, a small, hard, irreducible weight that nothing, she felt, could ever dislodge.
