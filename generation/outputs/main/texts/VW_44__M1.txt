The fog had a taste of iron and old water, a taste of the river and the soot that had settled on London for a century. It pressed against the glass of the cab, and when the driver’s voice came through the hatch—“Number 17, ma’am, that’ll be it”—she felt the vibration in the soles of her shoes rather than heard the words. Élise adjusted the black veil over her face; the netting was rough against her nose, smelling of French perfume and the mothballs of a Parisian trunk. She paid the man in English coins that felt light and flimsy in her palm, unlike the heavy, certain gold of the francs she was used to. She watched him drive away, the red taillight dissolving into the yellow murk, leaving her standing on the pavement of the house where she had been born, a stranger in the geography of her own childhood.

The door opened before she could knock. It was Helen, standing in the hall with the light behind her, a silhouette that had grown thicker, more solid, in the twenty years of absence. Helen did not embrace her; she took the hatbox and the small case, her hands efficient and dry, and said, “You’ve missed the worst of the train, at least. Mother passed in the night. It was very quiet.” The words were stones dropped into a well, falling with a dull thud that did not seem to reach the bottom. Élise stepped over the threshold. The smell hit her first—not the stale air of a sickroom, but the smell of floor polish, of boiled cabbage from the basement, of a house that had been scrubbed to hide the fact of death. It was a smell of her youth, and it made her feel faintly sick, as if she were ten years old again and being scolded for muddy boots.

In the drawing room, the curtains were half-drawn, and the afternoon light filtered through the lace in grey ribbons that lay across the carpet. The room was smaller than she remembered, the furniture crowded together, pushing against the walls as if to escape. Ada was there, sitting by the cold fireplace, her hands folded in her lap, her face a mask of pale endurance. She looked up when Élise entered, and her eyes, blue and watery, narrowed slightly.

“You look very... French,” Ada said. She did not mean it as a compliment; she meant the cut of the coat, the way Élise stood, the slight tilt of the head that suggested a language spoken behind the eyes. “Did you have a dreadful crossing?”

“The sea was calm,” Élise said. Her voice sounded high and thin in the low-ceilinged room. She felt the English words forming in her mouth like foreign objects, heavy and clumsy. In Paris, her voice was a tool, a stream of melody that flowed easily; here, it was a series of distinct, hard sounds. She wanted to say that the crossing was nothing, that the boat had rocked like a cradle and the gulls had screamed over the grey water, but the thought dissolved before it reached her tongue. “I am sorry about Mother.”

“Of course,” Helen said, coming in with a tray of tea. The china clattered. “We are all sorry. But one must carry on. The solicitor will be here at four. He wants the papers from the bureau.” Helen poured the tea with a heavy hand, the milk splashing into the cups. She looked at Élise’s gloves. “You can take those off now, you know. There’s no one to impress.”

Élise peeled the gloves from her fingers. Her hands looked white and frail against the dark wood of the table. She looked at her sisters, sitting in their black crepe, solid as oak trees, rooted in this damp soil. They were English to the bone, she realized; they belonged to the heavy furniture, to the soot in the chimney, to the grey light that seemed to be a permanent state of being. And she? She was a ghost drifting through the rooms, a memory of a girl who had run across these floorboards, now replaced by a woman who thought in another tongue. She picked up the teacup. The tea was strong and bitter, the taste of England, and she drank it slowly, feeling the warmth spread through her chest, a small fire in a cold house.

She remembered a summer, perhaps thirty years ago, when the sun had actually shone on the garden and the grass had been dry and yellow. She and Ada had made a tent out of the tablecloths. But looking at the garden now, through the tall window, she saw only wet slate and the skeletal remains of the rose bushes, shivering in the draft that crept under the door. The past was a country she could not visit; it had been repopulated by strangers.

“Henri sends his regards,” she said suddenly. The name hung in the air, sounding ridiculous, too soft and round for this room.

“Does he?” Ada asked, stirring her sugar. “I expect he’s busy with the vineyards. Or is it the shop now? I can never remember.”

“The shop,” Élise said. “He is selling the last of the winter truffles.” She saw the dark, rich earth of the Périgord, the baskets of black diamonds, the smell of the soil and the cold wind coming down from the hills. It was a vivid, burning image, brighter than the room she was sitting in. “It has been a hard winter there too.”

“Well,” Helen said, setting her cup down with a decisive click. “London is London. One doesn’t expect truffles.” She stood up and walked to the window, looking out at the street where a woman was dragging a pram through the slush. “I suppose you’ll want to see the bedrooms before the solicitor comes. To see if anything’s been disturbed.”

Élise rose. She felt stiff, as if her bones had calcified in the damp air. She followed Helen up the stairs, her hand trailing along the banister. The wood was smooth, worn away by generations of hands. She looked at the wall, where the wallpaper was peeling slightly at the seam. She had a sudden, overwhelming urge to touch the tear, to peel it back and see what was underneath—perhaps a different colour, a different time, a version of herself that had never left. But she kept her hand moving, climbing the stairs, step by step, into the darkness of the upper floor. The silence of the house pressed down on her, thick and suffocating, and she realized with a sharp pang that she was an exile here, not because she had left, but because she had stayed away too long to ever truly return.
