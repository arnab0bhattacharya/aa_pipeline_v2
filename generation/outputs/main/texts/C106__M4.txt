The rain had not stopped for three days, and the sound of it on the slate roof of the cottage was a constant, drumming reminder of the world’s indifference. Ellen stood at the small window, her breath fogging the cold glass, and watched the water sluice down the cobbled lane, turning it into a shallow, muddy river. It was a fitting eve for a wedding she did not want.

Behind her, the fire in the hearth spat and hissed, its warmth a reproach. On the table, laid out with a care that felt like a mockery, was her wedding dress. It was a fine thing, of a deep blue wool, with a lace collar that had cost more than her father earned in a season. A gift from Mr. Ashworth. *Her husband-to-be.* The words felt foreign, like a stone in her mouth. Mr. Ashworth, with his soft hands and his soft voice, his talk of wool prices and the new mill he was building in the valley below. He was fifty if he was a day, twenty years her senior, and as dull as the pewter plates on her mother’s dresser. But he was kind, and he was rich.

Her father, Thomas, sat in his worn armchair, staring into the flames. The lines on his face seemed deeper tonight, etched by a worry that had become a permanent resident in their home. The failure of the smallholding, the sickness that had taken their few sheep, the debt to the bank in Harrowgate—it was a litany of misfortune that had led them to this. To her.

“He is a good man, Ellen,” her father said, his voice raspy. He did not look at her. “He’ll provide for you. You’ll never want for a thing.”

“I want for nothing now, Father,” she replied, her voice quiet and flat. It was a lie, and they both knew it. She wanted for love. She wanted for a life of her own choosing, a life that did not feel like a transaction.

He sighed, a heavy, defeated sound. “We owe him a great deal. The money for the doctor when your mother was ill… the loan to keep the roof over our heads. Without him…” He trailed off, leaving the sentence to hang in the smoky air. Gratitude. It was a heavy cloak, and they were wrapping it around her shoulders, pinning it with duty.

Ellen turned from the window. She looked at her mother, who was busy folding linens, her hands moving with a frantic, nervous energy. Her mother had not met her eyes all evening. She was a woman who had married for love, a love that had weathered every storm, and Ellen knew the silent ache in her heart. She was giving her daughter away to save them all, and the shame of it was a physical presence in the room.

“I should like to walk,” Ellen announced suddenly.

“In this weather?” her mother protested, her hands stilling. “You’ll catch your death.”

“I need some air. I won’t be long.”

Before they could argue further, she had pulled her thick wool cloak from its hook and slipped out of the door. The cold hit her like a slap, a bracing, honest shock after the stifling warmth of the cottage. The rain was a fine, needle-like drizzle now, and the wind carried the peaty scent of the moors. She pulled her hood up and began to walk, her boots sinking into the mud with a soft, sucking sound.

She did not walk towards the village, with its huddle of stone houses and the church where she was to be bound on the morrow. Instead, she turned up the lane, towards the open fell. The path was a familiar one, a sheep track that wound its way past the last cottage and up onto the high, wild land. The world was grey and green, the skeletal branches of the hawthorn trees clawing at a sky the colour of old lead. The wind moaned across the hills, a lonely, elemental sound.

She thought of Mr. Ashworth. She thought of his house, a grand, square-built place of dark stone, with more rooms than they had furniture to fill. She thought of the life that awaited her there. A life of quiet order, of managing servants, of sitting at the head of a long, polished table. He would be generous. She would have new gowns, books from Leeds, perhaps even a pianoforte. He would not be unkind. He would pat her hand and talk of his business, and she would listen and nod, and the years would stretch out before her, flat and featureless as the moorland in the fog.

Was gratitude enough? Her father’s voice echoed in her mind. It was a practical question, a question of survival. And what was love, after all? A girlish fancy? A fleeting passion that burned bright and then died, leaving only ashes? Her parents had love, and it had not saved them from the crushing weight of debt and disappointment. Perhaps Mr. Ashworth’s dullness was a kind of safety. A placid sea after a lifetime of storms.

She reached the top of the rise and stopped, her breath catching in her throat. Below her, the valley was a patchwork of dark fields and grey stone walls. The river was a silver ribbon, and the village was a cluster of huddled roofs, smoke rising from the chimneys in thin, grey columns. Further down, she could see the great, raw scar on the land where Mr. Ashworth’s new mill was being built, its skeleton of timber and stone a testament to his industry. He was building a future. He was offering her a place within it.

A memory surfaced, unbidden. A summer’s day, years ago, when she was a girl of sixteen. She had been walking on this very path, and she had met John Renshaw, the blacksmith’s son. He had been carrying a lamb, a tiny, bleating thing he’d found abandoned on the fell. His hands, so strong and sure at the forge, had been impossibly gentle as he cradled the creature. He had smiled at her, a slow, easy smile that had made her heart turn over. They had walked together for a while, not speaking, just sharing the warmth of the sun and the quiet companionship. It was a moment, nothing more. He had gone to Manchester the following year to seek his fortune, and she had not seen him since. But the memory of that smile, of that simple, uncomplicated feeling, was a sharp, sweet pain in her chest. It was a reminder of a different kind of life, a life that might have been.

But John Renshaw was a ghost, a what-if. Mr. Ashworth was real. His offer was real. The debt was real.

She stood there for a long time, the rain soaking through her cloak, the wind chilling her to the bone. The choice was laid out before her, stark and simple. On one side, the cold, hard reality of her family’s need, and a life of comfort bought with her own acquiescence. On the other, a vague, uncertain future, a romantic dream that had no substance, no power to save anyone. She thought of her father’s defeated posture, her mother’s averted eyes. She was not just marrying a man; she was salvaging her family. Her life was the currency.

A strange sense of calm settled over her. It was not resignation, not exactly. It was the quiet clarity of a decision made. She had been looking for a reason to say no, a sign, a grand passion to sweep her off her feet and carry her away from this duty. But there was no sign. There was only the rain, the wind, and the quiet, insistent voice of her own conscience.

She turned and began the slow walk back down the path. The lights of the village were beginning to twinkle in the gathering dusk, small, brave beacons against the encroaching dark. The smell of woodsmoke grew stronger. As she approached her home, she saw the lamp glowing in the window, a warm, yellow square in the grey afternoon. Her father was standing at the door, his figure a dark silhouette against the light. He was waiting for her.

She paused at the gate, her hand on the cold, wet wood. She could still feel the wildness of the fell on her face, the scent of the rain and the peat. She could still feel the ghost of a blacksmith’s smile. But it was time to come in from the cold.

She pushed the gate open, the hinges creaking a familiar welcome. Her father stepped forward, his face a mask of anxiety. She met his eyes, and for the first time that day, she did not look away. She gave him a small, tired smile.

“It’s cold, Father,” she said, her voice steady. “Let’s go inside.”
