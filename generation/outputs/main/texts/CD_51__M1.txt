In that amphibious region where the river begins to lose its respectable riverhood and put on the wild and salty manners of the sea, and where the land, in a manner of speaking, is not so much solid ground as it is a temporary arrangement of mud and opinion, there stood the ferry-house of Old Silas Grumm. It was a house that seemed to have been built by the tide itself, of stout timbers that knew the taste of brine, and a roof of mossy tiles that clung together for warmth against the perpetual damp. Here, upon a spit of land that the map-makers called Plover’s Reach, but which the local boatmen knew as the Devil’s Elbow for its treacherous currents, lived Old Silas and his daughter, Prudence.

Old Silas was a man whose ambition was as small and as firmly fixed as a limpet upon a rock; it was to be left alone. He desired a quiet life, a life measured out in the gentle slop of the tide against his piles, the cry of the gulls, and the steady, predictable income derived from ferrying honest souls across the treacherous water to the opposite shore at Gravesend. He was a man of few words, and those few were usually concerned with the state of the weather, the price of tar, or the untrustworthiness of the wind. His daughter, Prudence, was the one bright and articulate thing in his watery world, a girl whose laughter could be heard across the water on a still day, and whose sharp eyes missed nothing that moved upon the estuary.

Now, into this quiet life had come two suitors, who between them represented the two great opposing forces of that watery kingdom: the Law and the Lack of It. The first was Mr. Alaric Vane, an officer of His Majesty’s Customs and Excise. Mr. Vane was a man composed entirely of straight lines and official pronouncements. His uniform was always immaculate, his cravat a starched white barricade against the world’s slovenliness, and his speech was peppered with such words as ‘regulation,’ ‘contraband,’ and ‘the King’s revenue,’ which he pronounced as if they were sacred texts. He courted Prudence with the same methodical precision with which he filled out his ledgers, offering her not passion, but a prospectus of a respectable future, a life of order, punctuality, and a small but certain pension in his declining years. ‘Miss Grumm,’ he would say, his voice as dry as a ship’s biscuit, ‘I propose to you a partnership of mutual benefit and civic virtue. Consider the advantages.’

The second suitor was a creature of a vastly different sort, a man who seemed to have been fashioned from the fog and the moonlight by some fanciful and mischievous spirit. He was known only as Jasper, though the excise men on the coast had other, less printable names for him. He was a smuggler, the dashing and elusive captain of a swift lugger called the *Silent Nightshade*, a vessel that could appear from nowhere and vanish into the mist like a phantom. Jasper courted Prudence with wild tales of the French coast, with gifts of silk that shimmered like a butterfly’s wing, and with a dangerous, twinkling charm that was as intoxicating as the brandy he sometimes brought ashore. ‘Pru,’ he would murmur, leaning against the doorpost with the easy grace of a cat, ‘what is a life of ledgers and wet feet when a whole world of moonlight and adventure is waiting out there on the water?’

Poor Old Silas, caught between these two, was a man perpetually on the verge of a headache. Mr. Vane represented authority, and therefore trouble. Jasper represented adventure, and therefore even greater trouble. All Silas wanted was to ferry the baker’s boy and the doctor’s apprentice and to be left in peace. He would watch the two men with a look of profound exhaustion, as if they were two opposing tides threatening to tear his small world asunder.

The crisis, when it came, did so on a night of such profound and suffocating fog that the world seemed to have shrunk to the dimensions of the ferry-house and its small, creaking pier. The fog rolled in from the sea in great, silent, grey waves, muffling all sound, swallowing the lights of Gravesend, and turning the familiar estuary into a monstrous and unknown wilderness. It was a fog in which a man might lose not only his way, but his very sense of self. It was on such a night that both Mr. Vane and Jasper had, by different routes and for very different reasons, an urgent and undeniable need of Old Silas’s ferry.

The first to arrive was Mr. Vane, his official boat having been rendered useless by the impenetrable gloom. He hammered upon the door, his knuckles raw with cold and impatience. ‘Grumm!’ he cried, his voice tight with an excitement that was utterly foreign to it. ‘A matter of the utmost urgency! A vessel—the *Nightshade*, I am certain of it!—has been sighted making for the Devil’s Elbow. I must cross. The King’s revenue is at stake!’

Hardly had he spoken, his words still hanging in the damp air like ghosts, when a second figure materialized out of the fog as if he were a part of it. It was Jasper, his dark coat beaded with moisture, his face pale and urgent. ‘Silas,’ he said, his voice a low and desperate whisper. ‘My girl, you must help me. My mother lies ill at Higham, past Gravesend. A fever. They say she may not see the morning. I must get to her.’

The two men stood face to face in the lamplight that spilled from the open door. The King’s officer and the King’s enemy, the hunter and the hunted, each with his own compelling story, each with his eyes fixed on the old ferryman who held their fates in his gnarled hands. Prudence stood behind her father, her face a mask of conflicting emotions—pity for the one, terror for the other, and a dawning horror at the predicament. Old Silas looked from the rigid, righteous face of the customs officer to the pale, pleading face of the smuggler. He looked at the churning, invisible water beyond his door, a water that held both a desperate voyage and a desperate escape. He let out a great, weary sigh that seemed to carry the weight of all the fog in the estuary.

‘The water’s the same for both of ye,’ he said at last, his voice a low rumble. ‘And my boat’ll carry ye both. But I’ll have no trouble in it. Whatever ye are to each other on the far side, ye are passengers here. And passengers pay.’ And with that, he turned to fetch his lantern and his pole, leaving the two men to stare at each other across the chasm of their professions, while his daughter watched them, and wondered which of these two paths through the fog would prove, in the end, to be the truest.
