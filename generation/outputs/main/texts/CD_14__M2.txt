The rain in Islington, in the autumn of 1843, did not so much fall as it conspired with the mud, the soot, and the general melancholy of the season to encase the world in a damp, grey shroud that seemed to absorb the light and leave only the shadows standing. It was a day fit for the extinguishing of hopes, or the kindling of fires, but certainly not for the quiet, uneventful passage of time, which had been the sole ambition of Mr. Josiah Thwack for the better part of a decade. The villa in which Mr. Thwack resided, a structure of modest pretensions situated on the edge of a lane that smelled perpetually of damp wool and unwashed laundry, was a house of many rooms, each one a monument to the stagnation of its inhabitants. Here, in the drawing room, with its curtains drawn tight against the gloom and its furniture polished to a mirror-like shine that reflected only the dust, the great event of the evening was about to unfold, though no one present suspected its magnitude.

Mr. Thwack was a man of forty-five, or perhaps fifty; it was difficult to tell, for his face had the peculiarly blurred, unfinished quality of a sketch that has been erased and re-drawn too many times. He was a clerk of no small reputation in the office of the late Mr. Quin, a position he held with the tenacity of a barnacle on a rock, fearing that if he let go, he would be swept away into the abyss of unemployment and want. He was engaged to Miss Clara Vane, a young woman of twenty-two, who had been engaged to him for seven years. Seven years! A duration in which empires rise and fall, in which stars burn out and new ones are born, and yet, in the domestic sphere of the Thwack household, it had amounted to nothing more than a prolonged period of waiting for Mr. Thwack’s prospects to improve.

Miss Clara sat by the window, her face pale as the paper on which she had been writing letters she would never send, her fingers white-knuckled around the hem of her dress. She looked, in that dim light, like a statue of patience that had finally begun to crack. Beside her, seated in a chair that creaked with every movement of his heavy limbs, was Uncle Barnaby, a man of immense girth and even greater obstinacy, who subsisted on a diet of porter and spite, and who regarded the world with the suspicion of a cat who has been offered a fish but is afraid of the water.

It was Clara who broke the silence. She had been silent for years, but the silence had grown heavy, like a leaden blanket, until it threatened to suffocate her.

"Josiah," she said, her voice steady, though her hands trembled. "I have waited. I have waited through the snows of '36, and the fevers of '38, and the long, dreary winters that followed. I have waited for your promotion, for your salary to rise, for your uncle to die, or for you to find the courage to make a life for yourself. I have waited until I have forgotten what it is to be young, and to be loved, and to be free."

Mr. Thwack blinked, as if a fly had landed on his nose. "Clara, my dear," he began, in that wheezy, apologetic tone of his, "one must be patient. Prospects are fickle. The times are hard. My uncle, though he is not a man of great wealth, is not a man of great poverty. We must not be hasty."

"Hasty?" Clara stood up. The chair scraped loudly against the floor, a sound like a groan from the earth itself. "Hasty! After seven years! I am not asking for a castle, Josiah. I am asking for a husband. I am asking for a future. If you cannot give me a future, then I will not have a present."

Uncle Barnaby, who had been dozing with his eyes half-closed and his mouth slightly open, opened one eye. It was a small, beady eye, like a button sewn onto a patchwork coat, but it held a sharp, piercing intelligence that was often missed by those who looked only at his bulk. He listened, his breath rasping in his chest, his fingers drumming a slow, irregular rhythm on the arm of his chair.

Mr. Thwack looked at Clara, then at his uncle, then at the fire, where a log was burning with a sullen, red glow. "Clara," he said, clearing his throat, "I have a solution. A perfect one. My uncle’s health is... delicate. We all know that. If he were to... to pass on, his estate, though modest, would secure our position. I would be in a position to offer you the home, the comfort, the security you deserve. All I ask is a little more time. A very little more time. Once the uncle is gone, we will marry."

The room fell silent. The rain pattered against the glass. The fire crackled.

Clara looked at her fiancé, her eyes wide with a mixture of horror and disbelief. "You would marry me on the condition of your uncle’s death?" she whispered. "You would trade my happiness for a few pounds and a better office?"

"It is not a trade, my dear," Mr. Thwack stammered, his face turning the color of a beet. "It is a... a practical arrangement. The world is not all poetry and romance, Clara. It is ledgers and balances. And Uncle Barnaby is..."

"He is alive," said a voice from the corner of the room.

It was Uncle Barnaby. He had risen from his chair, a movement that seemed impossible for a man of his size, yet he stood there, rigid and upright, his face set in an expression of such profound, malevolent determination that it could have been carved from granite. His eyes were fixed on Mr. Thwack, and in them burned a fire that was not of this world.

"You want me to die?" Barnaby asked, his voice low and gravelly. "You want me to expire, so that you may have your wife and your money? Is that it, Josiah? Is that the depth of your devotion? To my death?"

Mr. Thwack stammered, "I... it was not my intention... I meant... the timing..."

"Timing!" Barnaby roared, the sound shaking the dust from the chandelier. "You mean to time my death! You mean to schedule my departure from this world like a train on the Great Western! Very well, then! I shall defy you! I shall defy the clock! I shall defy the very laws of nature! If you want me to die, then I shall live! I shall live forever! I shall eat until I burst, I shall sleep until I am cold, but I shall not die! Not while you draw breath! Not while you stand there, waiting for my corpse to pay for your wedding!"

Clara looked from one to the other, her face a mask of frozen horror. "Uncle," she said, weakly, "that is... that is impossible."

"Impossible!" Barnaby sneered, pointing a thick, fleshy finger at her. "You think you know what is possible? You think you know the limits of a man’s will? I am a man of principle, Clara. And my principle is this: I shall outlive him. I shall outlive you. I shall outlive the sun itself, if I have to! I shall sit in this very chair, and I shall eat my oysters, and I shall drink my port, and I shall smile at every one of you, and I shall not die! I shall be the ghost that refuses to leave the room! I shall be the debt that cannot be paid! I shall be the shadow that stretches across the light!"

Mr. Thwack fell into his chair, his face white as chalk. "Barnaby," he whispered. "Please. Let us not be dramatic."

"Dramatic!" Barnaby shouted, his voice echoing in the small, crowded room. "You have made a drama of your life, Josiah! You have made a farce of your love! And I shall be the punchline! I shall be the joke that never ends! I shall live! I shall live! I shall live!"

And so, it was decided. Not by law, nor by contract, but by the sheer, implacable force of a man’s spite. Uncle Barnaby would not die. He would live, he said, with a grin that showed his yellowed teeth, a grin that was as wide and as terrible as the moon in a full sky. He would live, and he would watch, and he would wait, and he would make sure that Mr. Josiah Thwack knew, every single day, that his prospects had not improved, and that his future was as long and as uncertain as the night.

Clara turned to the window, and looked out at the rain. The rain had stopped, but the streets were still wet, reflecting the gaslights in long, wavering streaks. She looked at her hands, and then at the fire, and then at the two men who had made her life a cage. She did not speak. She did not move. She simply stood there, in the middle of the room, in the middle of the silence, and she felt, for the first time in seven years, that the air was cold, and that the ground beneath her feet was solid, and that she was alone.

Outside, the London fog rolled in, thick and grey, swallowing the lamps, the houses, the streets, and the people, until there was nothing left but the darkness, and the sound of the wind, and the ticking of the clock, which ticked on, and on, and on, indifferent to the hopes and the fears of the small, frail creatures who lived within its reach. The clock did not care for Mr. Thwack’s prospects. It did not care for Clara’s patience. It did not care for Uncle Barnaby’s spite. It simply measured the time, second by second, minute by minute, hour by hour, and it was patient, and it was true, and it was the only thing in the room that was not waiting for something to change.
