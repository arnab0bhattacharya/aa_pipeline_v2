The smoke of the West Riding hung over the valley of Netherfield like a shroud of grey wool, thick enough to taste upon the tongue. Within the great stone house of Fieldhead, which sat aloof upon the hillside amidst its scanty pines, the air was still, but the silence was of that oppressive kind which precedes a storm. I stood at the window, watching the chimneys of the mill below belch forth their black exhalations, and my heart beat with a heavy, irregular throb.

Miss Adela Vane, the heiress of Fieldhead, sat by the fire. She was a woman of great beauty and greater wealth, possessed of that pale, statuesque calm which the world so often mistakes for virtue, but which I knew to be the armour of an indifferent spirit. The firelight played upon her silk gown, turning it to liquid gold, while I, in my plain merino, felt like a shadow cast upon the wall. I was her companion, the niece of the rector of the parish, dependent upon her caprice for my daily bread, yet possessed of a spirit that refused to be bought, even by the kindness she occasionally lavished upon me.

"You are restless, Eleanor," said Adela, without looking up from the letter she was idly perusing. "Is the wind rising?"

"The wind is rising in my soul, Adela," I replied, turning to face her. "And it blows from the mill. It carries the sound of hammers that are falling silent."

At this, she laid the letter down. Her eyes, large and dark, fixed upon me with a look of inquiry, but there was no sympathy in them, only a cold curiosity. "You speak of Mr. Thorne’s troubles again. You have wearied me with them this week past. Business is a tide, Eleanor; it ebbs and flows. He is a manufacturer; he must take the rough with the smooth."

"It is not a tide, Adela; it is a shipwreck," I cried, my voice rising despite my efforts at restraint. "You know it as well as I. The bank in Leeds has called in its loan. Unless he finds capital—unless he finds it now—the looms of Thorne and Son will be sold at auction, and the hundreds of souls who depend upon him for bread will be cast out to starve."

Adela shrugged her white shoulders. "Capital is not a commodity to be plucked like blackberries from a hedge. If he has failed to manage his affairs, it is his own lack of foresight. I cannot help every struggling tradesman who makes a bad bargain."

"No," I said, stepping closer to her, my hands clenched at my sides. "You cannot help every tradesman. But you can help him. You know you can. Your fortune is vast—larger than you can ever spend. A loan, a mere fraction of your income, would set him free. He is an honest man, Adela; a man of genius and industry. To see him crushed because of a temporary lack of gold seems to me a sin against justice."

A flush rose to her pale cheeks, not of shame, but of anger. She rose from her chair, tall and commanding. "You speak boldly, Eleanor. You forget your place. But since you speak of sin, let me tell you of another. It is a sin to presume to dictate the disposal of another’s wealth. My money is mine. I did not inherit it to play the saviour to every distressed manufacturer in the district. Moreover, you speak of Mr. Thorne with a heat that I find indelicate. Is it only the poor workers you pity, Eleanor? Or is it the master?"

The blow struck home, stinging my pride and my conscience alike. I felt the blood rush to my face, but I did not quail. "I pity the man who is unjustly broken," I retorted. "And I despise the woman who has the power to save him and will not, out of cold-blooded pride. You fear, perhaps, that he might misunderstand your charity? That he might think you care for him?"

"I fear nothing," she said, her voice dropping to a dangerous quiet. "Least of all the opinion of a man who is ruined. But I will tell you this, Eleanor: I will not throw my money into a bottomless pit to gratify your romantic notions. If Mr. Thorne is to be saved, let him save himself. Let him find a wife with a dowry, perhaps, though I know of none in this district foolish enough to wed a beggar."

She paced the room, the silk of her dress rustling like dry leaves. The wind outside rattled the window frames, and a sudden gust drove the rain against the glass with a sound like scattering shot. I felt a surge of emotion so fierce it threatened to choke me. I knew then that the friendship we had nursed, however unequal, was dead. It had died in the fire of her indifference.

"You are cruel, Adela," I whispered. "Cruel as the grave."
