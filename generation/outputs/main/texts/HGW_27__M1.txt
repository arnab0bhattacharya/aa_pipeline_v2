The city of Luminos had abolished the night. For a century and a half, the great artificial suns, suspended on their pylons above the rooftops, had poured down a steady, unwavering, and perfectly pleasant daylight. There were no shadows in Luminos, only soft, graded areas of lesser illumination. The citizens had evolved accordingly; their circadian rhythms were a thing of historical curiosity, their sleep patterns governed by civic ordinance and personal habit rather than the clumsy rotation of the planet. It was a city of perpetual afternoon, a triumph of order over the chaotic, inconvenient business of cosmic mechanics.

Mara, a junior technician in the Department of Solar Radiance, was a creature of this order. Her world was one of lumens, spectrums, and photometric stability. She was not a dreamer but a practical young woman with a talent for finding the flaw in a beautifully calibrated system. It was this talent that led her to the anomaly. It began as a whisper in the data streams, a flicker so minute it was dismissed by her superiors as instrumental drift. But Mara saw a pattern. It was not a random fluctuation but a slow, rhythmic decay, like the fading pulse of a dying animal.

She ran the diagnostics on Sun-Pylon Alpha-7, the great lamp that illuminated the city’s central civic plaza. Its output was down by a fraction of a percentage point. Then Sun-Beta-3 over the residential arcologies. Then Gamma-12 over the industrial sector. A fraction here, a fraction there. Individually, they were nothing. Collectively, they were a harbinger. Mara took her findings to the Chief Illuminator, a man named Valerius, whose placid face seemed to have been polished by the very artificial light he commanded.

“Decay, my dear Mara?” he said, peering at her charts with a mild, paternalistic amusement. “Impossible. The system is self-correcting. The Pylons are built to last for millennia.”

“The self-correction protocols are overcompensating,” she insisted, her voice tight with a frustration she rarely felt. “They are drawing more power to maintain the illusion of stability, but the core emitters are degrading. It’s a fatigue effect. The crystals are… tiring.”

“Crystals do not tire, Mara. They are inert. This is fanciful talk.” He dismissed her with a wave of his hand, the gesture catching the light and casting a perfectly smooth, grey shadow on the wall behind him. “Go back to your data. Perhaps recalibrate your sensors. You are a fine technician, but you lack a certain… confidence in the permanence of our achievements.”

Mara left his office, the Chief’s placid certainty a bitter taste in her mouth. The permanence of their achievements. It was the foundational faith of Luminos. But the data did not share this faith. The data was a cold, hard, and relentless prophet of doom. For the next week, she worked secretly, bypassing the official channels and delving into the raw, unfiltered telemetry from the city’s power grid. What she found was terrifying. The degradation was accelerating. The system was not just tiring; it was beginning to fail.

The first public sign came on a Tuesday afternoon. The light in the Grand Concourse, usually a brilliant, shadowless white, took on a faint but definite blue cast. It was subtle, like the difference between good porcelain and cheap crockery, but the citizens of Luminos noticed. People paused in their walking, looking up at the sky-pylons with a vague unease. Conversations faltered. A child began to cry, pointing at the strange new colour of the light. The city, for the first time in a century and a half, felt… wrong.

Mara watched from her window, her heart hammering against her ribs. This was it. The beginning. She knew what would come next. The reports began to filter in. Headaches. Nausea. A strange, collective anxiety. The doctors were baffled, prescribing rest and light-therapy, which of course made things worse. The social order, so dependent on the unwavering perfection of its environment, began to fray. Arguments broke out in the public squares. The gentle hum of the city was replaced by a nervous, irritable clamour.

Then, the Great Dimming began. The lights did not go out. That would have been too simple, too merciful. Instead, they faded, slowly, over the course of several hours. The brilliant white of noon became the yellow of a late afternoon, then the orange of a sunset the city had never known. The shadows, long suppressed, began to creep out from the corners of buildings, stretching and deepening like living things. The people of Luminos panicked. They huddled in their homes, turning on every domestic lamp, every appliance that gave off the smallest glimmer of light. But the municipal grid was failing, and the domestic systems were tied to it. One by one, the little lights winked out.

Mara knew she had to get to the central control nexus, a place she had only ever seen in schematics. The city streets were a scene of mounting terror. People were running, screaming, colliding with things they could no longer see. The orange glow from the sky was now so weak it was barely a suggestion of light. Mara felt a primal fear she had never known, a terror born not of the darkness itself, but of the utter and complete unpreparedness for it. Her people were like deep-sea creatures suddenly dragged to the surface; they were being killed by the very medium they had conquered.

She reached the nexus, a vast, silent chamber at the heart of the Department. The main console was dark. Emergency power, a thing of legend, was nowhere to be found. The air was thick with the smell of ozone and hot metal. The system was dying. There was no way to restart it. The crystals were shattered, the pylons inert. The grand project of Luminos was over.

But as her eyes adjusted to the profound gloom, she noticed something. A faint, milky luminescence was filtering down from the great dome above the nexus. It was not the hard, electric glare of the artificial suns. It was soft, diffuse, and silver. She looked up. Through the thick plasteel of the dome, she saw them. Points of light. Thousands of them. Scattered across a blackness so deep and so vast it seemed to have no end. They did not burn; they glittered. They did not illuminate; they presided.

It was the stars. The real stars.

Awe, pure and absolute, washed away Mara’s fear. This was not an ending. It was a return. Below her, in the city, the panic was beginning to subside, replaced by a stunned, trembling silence. For the first time in their lives, the people of Luminos were looking up. They were seeing the universe as it truly was: immense, mysterious, and gloriously, terrifyingly dark. And in that darkness, they were finding, one by one, a light that was not made by man. They were learning to see in the dark.
