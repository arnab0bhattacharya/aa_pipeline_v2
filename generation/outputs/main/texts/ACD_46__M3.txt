It was upon a raw November afternoon in 1894 that I found myself seated opposite Sir Aldous Trevena in the oak-panelled study of Varley Grange, a solid Georgian pile situated in a sheltered hollow of the Sussex Downs. I had travelled down from London at the urgent request of my friend, Mr. Martin Hewitt, the investigator, who was already established in the house. Sir Aldous, a hale but anxious man of seventy, paced the Turkey carpet with the restless energy of a caged animal, while Hewitt sat by the fire, his clean-shaven, good-humoured face belying the sharp attention of his eyes.

"The matter stands thus, Hewitt," Sir Aldous said, pausing to thrust his hands deep into his pockets. "My brother Lionel left England for the Cape in the spring of 1867. He was a wild, headstrong youth, and a quarrel over a woman parted us. He invested what little he had in a trading venture near the Diamond Fields, and was reportedly lost, along with his entire party, in the Bushman country. For twenty-seven years I have believed him dead. Last Tuesday, a man arrived at this house claiming to be his son."

"And you, Sir Aldous, had no inkling of a marriage or a child?" Hewitt asked, his tone mild and conversational.

"None whatever. Lionel was but four-and-twenty when he sailed. Yet this claimant, who gives his name as Arthur Trevena, knows a remarkable amount of our family history. He knew of the silver snuff-box presented to my grandfather by the Duke, which I keep in that cabinet. He even knew the pet name my brother had for me in his boyhood. It is most disconcerting."

Hewitt turned to me. "You will observe, Doctor, the value of small details. A man may forge a pedigree, but the accidental secrets of a nursery are not easily come by."

At that moment the door opened, and a tall, gaunt woman of middle age entered. She was Mrs. Mallow, the housekeeper, who had served the Trevena family for thirty years. Her face was drawn and hard with resolution.

"Begging your pardon, Sir Aldous," she said, "but I must speak. This man is an impostor. I cared for Master Lionel from the time he was in short coats. I packed his trunk for the Cape myself. He had no wife, sir, and no child. I would have known. The young master was not one for secrets; he told me everything, even his foolishness regarding that Miss Carstairs. I will not see the estate, and the good name of this family, fall into the hands of a designing rogue."

"You are very positive, Mrs. Mallow," said Hewitt.

"I am positive, sir, because I have eyes. This claimant has Master Lionel’s eyes."

Sir Aldous started violently. "You said he was an impostor!"

"So he is, in his pretensions," the housekeeper replied, her voice trembling slightly. "But those eyes were Master Lionel’s. I cannot get past them."

"There is the point for our investigation," said Hewitt, rising briskly. "We shall see the gentleman. Sir Aldous, if you will be so good as to allow me to conduct the interview. Doctor, your observational skills shall be our second pair of eyes."

We moved to the morning-room, where the claimant awaited us. He was a man of perhaps thirty, sunburnt to a deep brown, with a rough, weather-beaten look about him that spoke of a colonial life. He wore a well-cut but much-worn travelling suit of grey tweed. He rose as we entered, his bearing easy and assured.

"Sir Aldous," he began, but Hewitt stepped forward.

"One moment, Mr. Trevena. Sir Aldous has placed the matter of your recognition in my hands. You will, I am sure, appreciate the necessity of satisfying certain formalities before the family solicitors are instructed to alter the will."

The claimant’s brow darkened for an instant, but he recovered himself. "Naturally. Ask what you will."

"You claim to be the son of Lionel Trevena, by a wife married at the Cape," Hewitt said. "Pray, what was your mother’s maiden name?"

"Anna de Vries, of Graaff-Reinet. She died when I was twelve."

Hewitt nodded, producing a notebook. "You knew of the silver snuff-box. How did you come by that knowledge?"

"My father spoke often of his old home," the man replied readily. "He told me of the box, and of the inscription inside it."

"Ah, the inscription," said Hewitt. "Most interesting. And what, sir, was the inscription?"

The claimant hesitated for a fraction of a second. "‘To Trevena, from his friend, the Duke.’"

Hewitt closed his notebook with a snap. "Sir Aldous, would you be so kind as to open the snuff-box in the cabinet?"

The baronet did so, and handed the box to Hewitt. There was no inscription within. It bore only the maker’s mark and the crest.

"A clever guess," Hewitt observed, "but a guess nonetheless. You have been misinformed, Mr. Trevena, or you have invented the detail to lend colour to your narrative."

The man’s face flushed. "My father’s memory may have failed him in small things. It is the larger truths that matter."

"The larger truths," Hewitt repeated. "Then let us turn to a larger truth. Your father, you say, was lost in the Bushman country. Yet you are here. How did you survive, and how did you make your way to England?"

"I was taken in by a Boer family, who raised me as their own. It was only upon my father’s death-bed that he revealed his true name to me."

"A most romantic tale," said Hewitt, "and one that is, unfortunately for you, entirely false. I have here a telegram received this morning from my agent in Cape Town." He drew a paper from his pocket. "It establishes, beyond doubt, that one Arthur Penhaligon, formerly of Brixton, sailed for the Cape in 1892, and was employed as a clerk in a store in Kimberley. He returned to England a fortnight ago. This Arthur Penhaligon is, I think, yourself."

The claimant’s jaw dropped. He looked from Hewitt to Sir Aldous, and then, with a sudden movement, he turned and wrenched open the window. Before any of us could prevent him, he had vaulted over the sill and disappeared into the rhododendron-bordered drive.

"Ahead of the law by a good ten minutes," said Hewitt, shutting the window. "The local constable will be notified. But come, Sir Aldous, there is a more interesting problem to resolve. Mrs. Mallow’s testimony regarding the eyes."

We returned to the study, where the housekeeper awaited us, her face pale.

"Mrs. Mallow," said Hewitt, "you said the claimant had Lionel Trevena’s eyes. You were perfectly right. He has."

"Then he is—" Sir Aldous began.

"He is not Lionel’s son. But he is of Trevena blood. I observed, upon the back of the man’s neck, a small, crescent-shaped scar, partially hidden by his collar. You have a similar mark, Sir Aldous, upon your own wrist, as I noted when you handed me the snuff-box. It is a hereditary trait, is it not?"

Sir Aldous stared. "It is a mark that has appeared in the Trevena family for generations. My father had it."

"And your brother Lionel had it also?"

"He did."

"Then the explanation is simple, and far less sinister than we feared. This man is not the son of Lionel Trevena, but he may well be the son of some other member of your family, one who emigrated to the Cape in an earlier generation. The eyes and the mark are the evidences of blood. He heard of Sir Aldous’s wealth, and of the missing Lionel, and sought to advance his own claim by substituting Lionel for his true father. His knowledge of family secrets was gathered, I suspect, from old letters or papers in the possession of his mother or grandmother, perhaps a domestic servant in the Trevena household."

Mrs. Mallow gasped. "There was a kitchen-girl, sir, a Dorcas Penny, who went out to the Cape in the fifties. She was always one to listen at doors."

"There is your source," said Hewitt. "The man is a Trevena, but an illegitimate and collateral branch. His claim to the baronetcy is void, but his blood is genuine. I would suggest, Sir Aldous, that when he is apprehended, you deal with him leniently. He is a rogue, but he is a rogue with your family’s eyes."

Sir Aldous sank into his chair, his face working with emotion. "I am greatly obliged to you, Hewitt. The matter is cleared. The will stands."

"And the housekeeper’s eyes were better than the claimant’s lies," Hewitt added, with a smile. "It is always the small things, the accidental things, that betray the carefully planned. A scar, a housekeeper’s memory, a missing inscription—these are the true evidences. The impostor constructed a grand romance, and forgot the snuff-box."
