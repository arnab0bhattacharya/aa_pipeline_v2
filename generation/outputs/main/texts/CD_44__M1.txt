The fog had crept into the very marrow of the city that November morning, a damp, grey, clinging thing, more substance than vapour, which smothered the cries of the street-vendors and softened the hard edges of the world. It was a fog fit for a ghost, or a ghost of a memory; and Mr. Jeremiah Pym, a man whose past was a country to which he could no longer travel, felt its chill upon him more keenly than on his skin. He stood in the waiting-room of the Court of Queen’s Bench, a place of polished wicker benches and nervous coughing, and felt as out of place as a rose in a coal-scuttle. His hands, large and knotted with a lifetime of holding reins, were clasped in his lap, a monument to a trade he no longer practised.

He was summoned here to give evidence in the matter of *Pike v. The Metropolitan Stage-Coach Company*, a case concerning a dreadful accident in Cheapside some three months prior, in which a little girl, no bigger than a doll, had been crushed beneath the wheels of a ponderous omnibus. Pym was not the driver of that omnibus; he was a witness, a man of the street who had, by the merest chance, been passing on the opposite pavement. But his name had been put forward by a sharp-eyed solicitor who had discovered a peculiar and inconvenient fact about Mr. Jeremiah Pym: that he had once, in the days of his youth, driven the carriage of a French nobleman.

This fact, a relic of a vanished world, was the key to the whole affair. The defence, a team of hard-faced lawyers with wit as sharp as their starched collars, argued that the coachman was not to blame; that the child had darted from the kerb in a heedless manner, impossible to foresee or avoid. The plaintiff’s counsel, a young man with more fire in his belly than sense in his head, had seized upon Pym. He wished to establish that a driver trained in the swift, precise, and dare one say, *continental* style of driving, would have had the skill to swerve, to check, to do *something* to avert the tragedy. Pym, therefore, was to be a living, breathing exhibit of superior horsemanship, a ghost from the aristocratic stables of a fallen France, summoned to condemn an English coachman by the standards of a dead lord.

The door to the court was thrown open, and a bailiff with a face like a winter turnip barked his name. “Pym! Jeremiah Pym! Step this way, and look lively!” Pym rose, his joints protesting with a sound like a rusty hinge. As he walked towards the great, dark maw of the courtroom, he was no longer in London. He was twenty years old again, perched high on the box of a lacquered berlin, the air crisp with the promise of a winter’s day outside Versailles. He could feel the supple leather of the reins in his hands, the powerful, rhythmic sway of the horses, and beside him, the faint, ever-present scent of lavender and snuff that belonged to the Comte de Valois.

“Now, Mr. Pym,” began the barrister, a man whose voice was a finely tuned instrument of persuasion and indignation, “you were in Cheapside on the morning of the fourteenth of August, were you not?”

“I was, sir. I was.”

“And you saw the whole lamentable event unfold before you?”

“I saw the ‘bus, sir. And I saw the little one. It was… very quick.”

“Quick! Indeed! Now, tell the jury, Mr. Pym, of your experience. You were for many years the principal coachman to a nobleman, were you not? A French nobleman?”

Here it came. The bridge to the past. “I was, sir. To the Comte de Valois. In Paris, and at his château.”

“And the Comte de Valois,” the barrister continued, warming to his theme, “was a man who demanded the very highest standard of skill and vigilance from his servants, was he not? A man for whom a moment’s inattention could mean disaster?”

Pym’s mind drifted again. He saw the Comte’s long, pale fingers tapping impatiently on the carriage window. He heard the clipped, impatient French—*“Vite, Pym! Vite! Ne soyez pas une tortue!”* (Quick, Pym! Quick! Do not be a turtle!)—a phrase he had understood more by its tone than its meaning. He remembered the narrow, crowded streets of Paris before the Terror, a maelstrom of carts, sedan chairs, and hawkers, where a driver needed the eyes of a hawk and the instincts of a fox. “He was… particular, sir. He liked to be swift.”

“Swift and safe! Is that not the mark of a master coachman?” the barrister declaimed, turning to the jury with a triumphant flourish. “And in your expert opinion, formed over years of such exacting service, could the driver of that omnibus, with ordinary skill and care, have avoided striking that child?”

This was the question. The whole weight of the proceeding rested upon it. Pym looked at the driver of the omnibus, a stout, red-faced man named Cobb, who sat wringing his hat in his hands, his eyes fixed on the floor. He looked a decent enough fellow. A Londoner. Not a man of Versailles, but a man of the Seven Dials. Pym thought of the Comte de Valois, who had lost his head in the Place de la Révolution for the crime of being a Comte de Valois. He thought of the little girl, whose name he had heard whispered in the court—Eliza. He thought of the fog, which had been thick that morning, too. So thick.

“Mr. Pym?” the barrister prompted, his voice losing its silkiness. “The court awaits your opinion.”

The silence in the room was a heavy, suffocating blanket. Pym could feel the eyes of the jury upon him, the judge’s gaze from his high perch, the desperate hope in the plaintiff’s solicitor. He was a bridge between two worlds, and both sides wished to pull him down.

He cleared his throat, the sound unnaturally loud. “It is… hard to say, sir.”

“Hard to say?” the barrister echoed, incredulous. “A man of your experience? With the training of the French nobility in your hands?”

“The French way is one way,” Pym said slowly, the words coming from a place deep within him, a place of dust and memory. “The London way is another. In Paris, the Comte, he… he wished for speed. He did not much care for the folk in the street. ‘They will move,’ he would say. ‘They are not fools.’ But here… here it is different. The streets are… thicker. The people are not so quick to move. They have their own business. A London coachman, he must… he must think for everyone. For the horses, for the ‘bus, for the fools, for the children. It is not the same skill. It is a different kind of seeing.”

He paused, looking at the jury. “I drove for the Comte de Valois. But I have lived in London for thirty years. And in the fog… well, sir. In the fog, we are all just as blind as one another. I cannot say what another man might have done. I can only say what I saw. And I saw a little girl, and a great wheel, and a fog that swallowed everything up.”

The barrister stared at him, his case dissolving before his eyes. He had wanted a sword, and Pym had given him a puddle. The judge made a note. The jury looked thoughtful. And Jeremiah Pym, his evidence given, was dismissed. As he walked out of the court and back into the grey London afternoon, he felt a strange relief. He had not been a ghost from France, nor a condemnation of an Englishman. He had been, simply and only, himself: Jeremiah Pym, a man who had driven many miles, and remembered that all roads, in the end, lead to the same uncertain place. The fog, if anything, seemed a little lighter now.
