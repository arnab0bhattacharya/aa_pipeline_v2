The shop was a narrow, dusky place, squeezed between a marine-store and the shuttered front of a small saddler’s that had long since given up the ghost, and it bore upon its grimy window the legend, in letters of faded gold, of *E. Hartmann, Watchmaker & Jeweller*, though the jewellery was a thing of the past and the watches were mostly old, and the man himself was old, and the whole establishment seemed to partake of a patient and dusty decay, as though it were a specimen preserved in the spirits of its own stagnant air. The light that found its way into the workshop from the street was of the colour of weak tea, and it fell upon a counter of blackened oak, upon glass cases where a few silver watches ticked with a faint, asthmatic insistence, and upon the figure of the watchmaker himself, a man of middle stature, with a grey, impassive face and hands that were remarkable for their delicacy, for their cleanliness, and for a certain stillness of habit that seemed to have been communicated to them by the very nature of their employment. He was a Swiss by birth, of that sober, patient, and somewhat cynical race of craftsmen who have made the measuring of time a kind of moral discipline, and who look upon the world of men with the quiet, disillusioned eyes of those who know that all things, from the movements of empires to the oscillations of a balance-wheel, are subject to the same inexorable laws of friction and decay.

It was an evening in late October, and the fog, which had been threatening all day, had at last come down upon the streets with a soft, pervasive insistence, muffling the footsteps of the rare passers-by, blurring the gas-lamps into vague and sorrowful halos, and lending to the interior of the shop a peculiar intimacy, as of a place cut off from the world, a little island of order and precision in the midst of a vast and shapeless confusion. Hartmann had lit the gas-jet above his work-bench, and was engaged, with a magnifying glass fixed in his eye, in the delicate task of adjusting the hair-spring of a lady’s watch, a small, frivolous, and exasperating thing, when the door opened, and a man entered.

He was a man of no particular age, of no particular appearance, and yet there was about him something that caused Hartmann to lay down his tools and to raise his head with a slow and deliberate movement, as though he were conscious of a subtle but definite interruption in the rhythm of his thoughts. The stranger was dressed in a dark, heavy overcoat, buttoned to the throat, and his hat, which he did not remove, was pulled low over his forehead. His face was pale, and his eyes, which were of a light, uncertain grey, moved restlessly about the shop, taking in the dusty cases, the ticking watches, the old man at his bench, and then returning to the watchmaker’s face with a kind of furtive and yet determined scrutiny. He carried a small parcel, wrapped in brown paper, which he held before him with both hands, as a man might hold some object of a dangerous or questionable nature.

“You are Herr Hartmann?” he said, at last, and his voice was low and hurried, with a slight huskiness, as though he had been running, or as though he were labouring under some strong emotion which he was endeavouring to suppress.

“I am Hartmann,” the watchmaker replied, quietly. “What can I do for you?”

The man advanced to the counter, and, with a movement that was almost furtive, laid the parcel upon it. “I have a piece of work,” he said, “which I am told you can do. It is a matter of some delicacy, and I am willing to pay well for it. But it must be done at once—to-night—and it must be done in strict confidence.”

Hartmann looked at the parcel, and then at the man. “I do not,” he said, slowly, “undertake work that I do not understand. And I do not,” he added, with a faint, dry smile, “undertake work that requires to be done in the dark.”

The man hesitated. He glanced over his shoulder at the door, as though he feared that some one might be listening, and then, with a quick, nervous movement, he unwrapped the parcel upon the counter.

The object which it contained was a small, compact mechanism of brass and steel, of a workmanship that was evidently of the highest order, but of a form that was not that of any watch or clock that Hartmann had ever seen. It consisted of a cylinder, about six inches in length, fitted with a number of delicate screws and springs, and terminating at one end in a small, blunt-nosed hammer, and at the other in a kind of trigger, which was protected by a guard of polished steel. Hartmann looked at it for a long time in silence, and his face, which had been impassive before, became very still and grave. He did not touch it. He merely looked at it, as a man might look at a snake which he finds in his path, and then he raised his eyes to the stranger’s face.

“You wish me,” he said, “to repair this?”

“It is out of order,” the man said, hurriedly. “The spring—the mainspring, I think you call it—is broken. I want it replaced. I want it done to-night. I will pay you any sum you name.”

Hartmann was silent for a moment. The gas-jet hissed faintly above his head, and from the street came the muffled sound of a passing cart, and then the silence again, heavy and oppressive. “You know what this is?” he said, at last.

The man’s eyes shifted. “It is a—a machine,” he said, vaguely. “A machine of my own invention. For—for mining purposes.”

“For mining purposes,” Hartmann repeated, without any expression. “I see. And you wish me to repair it—to-night—in strict confidence.”

“Yes. I will pay you in advance.” The man fumbled in his pocket, and produced a handful of gold coins, which he laid upon the counter. They were sovereigns, and they glinted dully in the gaslight. “There are ten pounds there,” he said. “It is yours, if you will do the work. And I will bring you more to-morrow.”

Hartmann looked at the gold, and then at the machine, and then at the man. He was a watchmaker, a man of peace, a man whose whole life had been spent in the cultivation of a minute and scrupulous precision, and he was conscious, as he stood there, of a sudden and profound distaste, not for the stranger, who was nothing to him, but for the thing which lay upon his counter, and for the whole dark and sinister world of violence and secrecy of which it was the symbol. He had lived long enough in London to know that such instruments were not made for the mining of coal or the blasting of rock, and he had a swift and vivid vision of a dark street, of a sudden flash, of a man lying dead upon the stones, and of himself, the watchmaker, sitting in his shop, with the knowledge of it in his heart and the price of it in his pocket.

“I cannot do it,” he said, quietly.

The man started. “What do you mean?” he said, and his voice was suddenly harsh and threatening. “You are a watchmaker, are you not? You repair clocks and watches. This is a machine. It is all the same.”

“It is not the same,” Hartmann said, with a patience that was not without a certain dignity. “I repair watches. I do not repair—this. I do not know what it is, and I do not wish to know. You will take it away.”

The man’s face flushed, and then grew pale. He leaned across the counter, and his eyes, which had been furtive, became suddenly fixed and hard. “You refuse?” he said, in a low voice. “You refuse to do a simple piece of work for which you are well paid? Do you know who I am?”

“I do not know who you are,” Hartmann said, “and I do not wish to know. I am a watchmaker. I have my own work to do. You will oblige me by taking this away.”

For a moment the man stood staring at him, and there was in his face a struggle of rage and fear, and then, with a sudden, violent movement, he swept the machine and the gold back into the brown paper, and crushed the parcel in his hands. “You will regret this,” he said, thickly. “You will regret it. You think you are safe here, in your little shop, with your clocks and your watches, but you are not so safe as you think. You have made an enemy to-night, Herr Hartmann, and you may find that an enemy is a dangerous thing.”

He turned and went out, slamming the door behind him, and Hartmann stood for a long time motionless, listening to the sound of his footsteps dying away in the fog. Then he sat down again at his bench, and took up the lady’s watch, and put the magnifying glass to his eye. But his hand was not quite steady, and the hair-spring, delicate as it was, seemed to have grown suddenly stubborn and unmanageable, and he laid it down again with a sigh. He was not a brave man, in the ordinary sense of the word, and he knew that he had made an enemy, and he knew that in the dark and tangled ways of the great city, an enemy might be a very dangerous thing. But he knew also, with a quiet and profound certainty, that he had done what he had to do, and that no amount of gold, and no threat of violence, could have made him do otherwise. He was a watchmaker, and his whole life had been a lesson in the value of order, of precision, of the small, exact, and honest adjustments by which alone the world is kept going. He had refused to meddle with the machinery of death, and he sat there, in the dim light of his little shop, with the fog pressing against the window, and the old watches ticking faintly around him, and he was conscious of a strange and quiet peace, as though he had, in some obscure way, justified his existence, and had kept faith with the delicate and honourable craft to which he had devoted his life.
