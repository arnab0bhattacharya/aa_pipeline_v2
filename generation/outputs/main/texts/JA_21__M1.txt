Miss Eleanor Vale had been engaged to Mr. Edmund Croft for two years, a period of time which, in the estimation of her mother, was long enough to have produced at least one wedding and a reasonable prospect of an heir, but which, in the private estimation of Eleanor herself, had produced little more than a settled and most unromantic familiarity. The engagement had begun in a season of general approval. Mr. Croft was a gentleman of unexceptionable character, a younger son with a respectable profession in the law, and his attachment to Eleanor was of so steady and undeviating a nature as to defy any question of its sincerity. He was waiting, as the phrase went, for preferment—a living in the Church, a partnership, or some other mark of advancement that would allow him to support a wife in the manner to which she was accustomed. In the meantime, he was content to wait, and to visit at Hartwell Park with a regularity that had long ceased to be a matter of comment.

It was this very contentment, this unshakeable patience, that had become the source of Eleanor’s most secret and disturbing doubts. Mr. Croft was good. He was, in fact, so thoroughly good as to be quite without flavour. His conversation, though perfectly sensible, seldom rose above the topics of the county assizes, the health of the local clergyman, or the agricultural prospects of the coming season. He admired Eleanor’s proficiency on the pianoforte, but with the same calm approbation he might have extended to a well-managed dairy. He was devoted to her, but his devotion was a placid, sunlit lake, whereas Eleanor, in her heart of hearts, had begun to suspect she was built for the sea. She did not love him. The thought, once admitted, was as cold and clear as a winter morning. She esteemed him, she respected him, she felt a genuine affection for him as one might for a reliable and well-bred spaniel, but the spark, the fire, the elevating passion that poets wrote of and heroines in novels seemed to experience as a matter of course, was entirely absent.

Her family, of course, saw nothing amiss. Her mother, Mrs. Vale, a woman whose views of matrimony were founded upon the solid principles of income and consequence, considered the match a triumph of prudence. Her father, Sir John, a baronet of small fortune and smaller ambition, was satisfied that his daughter would be well-cared for. Her younger sister, Georgiana, who had read too many novels from the circulating library, thought Mr. Croft a capital fellow, if a little dull, and assumed that Eleanor’s heart was safely and happily engaged. To them, Eleanor’s occasional silences and thoughtful air were merely the signs of a bride-to-be’s happy contemplation. They could not have been further from the truth.

The crisis, when it came, was precipitated by a letter. It arrived on a Tuesday morning, bearing the frank of a naval officer, and was addressed to Mr. Croft at the village inn, where he had taken rooms for his visit. Mr. Croft, having a natural desire to share his good fortune with his future wife, brought the letter to Hartwell Park directly. He found Eleanor in the morning-room, and with a face of such uncharacteristic animation that she felt a pang of alarm.

“Miss Vale,” he began, his voice trembling slightly with an emotion she had never before heard in it, “I am the happiest of men! My cousin, Captain Croft, has been made a baronet! And he, having no son and no prospect of one, has been so kind—so exceedingly kind—as to declare me his heir. The estate is in Kent. It is a most respectable property. Eleanor,” he said, using her Christian name for perhaps the third time in two years, “our long period of waiting is at an end. I can now speak with certainty. We may be married as soon as you please.”

He held out the letter to her, his hand trembling. Eleanor took it mechanically. She saw the bold, decisive script, heard the triumphant words in her mind, and felt not a thrill of joy, but a profound and chilling certainty. The final barrier to their union had been removed. The path was clear. The wedding, which had for so long been a distant and abstract concept, was now imminent and real. And in that moment of perfect clarity, she knew she could not marry him. To do so would be to accept a life of quiet, respectable emptiness, to betray not only her own unformed feelings but the very possibility of a love she had not yet found. It would be a lie of the most fundamental and enduring kind.

“Eleanor?” Mr. Croft prompted gently, misinterpreting her silence for an excess of happy feeling. “You are overwhelmed. I understand it perfectly.”

She looked up at him, at his kind, plain, hopeful face, and felt a terrible pity for him. This was the hardest part. It was not her own disappointment she feared, but his. “Mr. Croft,” she said, and the return to formality was a deliberate, painful cruelty. “I am sensible of the honour you do me. I am sensible of your great goodness. But I cannot—I must not—accept you.”

The light in his eyes did not so much die as become fixed, like that of a candle in a sudden draught. “I do not understand you.”

“I have been mistaken,” she continued, forcing the words out, each one a stone in her throat. “My own heart has been a stranger to me. I believed my affection to be of the right kind, but I find I was wrong. It is not of a nature to make you happy, and it would be a grievous wrong to you to pretend otherwise.”

He stood very still, the letter still in his hand. “Is there another?”

The question was so direct, so devoid of the social circumlocution she had expected, that it startled her. “No. There is no one else.” This, at least, was the truth. There was only a vast, terrifying emptiness where love ought to be.

He gave a short, humourless laugh. “So it is merely that you do not love me. After two years. I see.” He folded the letter with great precision and placed it in his pocket. “I had believed my character to be well-known to you. I had not thought it subject to such… revision. You have been very frank, Miss Vale. I thank you for it.” The politeness of his words was a far greater condemnation than any reproach. He bowed stiffly, and without another look, he left the room.

The scene that followed with her family was, as Eleanor had anticipated, one of profound disapprobation. Her mother declared it an act of folly bordering on insanity. Her father, bewildered, could not comprehend what more a daughter could want than a good man with a secure estate. Only Georgiana, after the first shock, ventured to suggest that Eleanor had perhaps been too hasty, that affection might grow. But Eleanor knew it would not. She had faced her family’s displeasure and broken a good man’s heart. She was alone, and the future was an unmapped country. Yet as she sat by the window, watching the familiar Hampshire landscape, she felt not despair, but a strange and quiet sense of release. The truth, however painful, had been spoken. And in the midst of the ruin she had wrought, that was a comfort of a most singular kind.
