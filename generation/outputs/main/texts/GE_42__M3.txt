In the flat fenland village of Great Averill, where the black soil stretches away to meet a pale winter sky with an aspect of monotonous endurance, the character of the inhabitants is shaped as much by the resistance of the clay as by the doctrines preached from the pulpit. Among those who had wrested a meagre subsistence from this unyielding earth was Jonas Thicket, a labourer who had broken his constitution by long years of ditching and hedging before finally surrendering his breath to a marsh fever. He left behind him his wife, Martha, and a heritage of pride so fierce and unyielding that it seemed to compensate in the moral economy of her nature for the absence of material inheritance. Martha Thicket was a woman whose poverty was clad in a garment of such unimpeachable cleanliness and rigid self-respect that it repelled the very charity it might otherwise have invoked. She had been known to turn away from her door the overseer of the parish, Mr. Gravel, with a look that would have made a less thick-skinned gentleman feel the full indignity of offering bread in exchange for the surrender of independence; and this resolute rejection of all parochial assistance had woven itself into the village consciousness as an inseparable part of her character.

But pride, however heroic in its aspect, is a poor conductor against the damp cold of a Fenland January, and the same frost which had settled in the furrows of the fields found its way into the humble cottage where Martha lay upon her hard bed, stricken by a rheumatic fever. She had been sinking for three days, her only fire a few sods of turf that had smouldered out, when the attention of Mrs. Eleanor Halesworth, the rector's wife, was drawn to the absence of the widow's familiar figure. Between Mrs. Halesworth and Martha Thicket there had existed these five years past a state of mutual alienation, having its root in a severe misunderstanding concerning the distribution of a flannel petticoat. The rector's wife, a woman whose benevolence was somewhat obstructed by an exacting standard of doctrinal fitness and a consciousness of social station, had called upon Martha to present a gift of cast-off garments, which she had done with a graciousness that implied a considerable distance between the giver and the receiver. Martha, whose pride was the more sensitive because it was the sole possession she had to defend, had received the gift with a coldness that was indistinguishable from contempt, and had afterward been heard to say that she was not a pauper, to wear the leavings of the gentry. Mrs. Halesworth, who felt that her intentions had been as misguidedly pure as those of any philanthropist burdened by the infelicities of human vanity, had retorted by washing her hands of Martha Thicket altogether, concluding that there was no gratitude in a nature so stubbornly ungracious.

It was with something more than surprise, then, that Mrs. Halesworth, having walked across the frost-hardened ruts to the cottage on a Tuesday afternoon to inquire after a neighbouring family, found the door ajar and the interior pervaded by an air of desolate neglect. She entered, impelled by that instinct of succour which is often stronger than the resentments of the ego, and beheld Martha lying on the narrow bed, her face pinched and sharp, her breath coming in short, painful gasps beneath the scanty covering. The fire was a heap of grey ash, and a freezing mist seemed to rise from the stone floor itself.

The sight acted upon Mrs. Halesworth with a force that entirely dissolved the pettier accretions of their quarrel. Here was a fellow-creature, and a woman of stubborn virtue, perishing in the very shadow of the church whose bounty she had disdained. The rector's wife did not pause to weigh the moral consequences of her next actions; she took off her own thick cloak and laid it over the thin blanket, and then, going to the door, she called sharply to a boy who was passing on the lane.

"Run thee to the Rectory, lad," she said, in a voice that brooked no delay, "and bid them send Bessy hither with hot bricks and a posset, and a bundle of faggots from the wood-shed. Look sharp, now, or I'll know the reason why."

The boy, who was well accustomed to the tone of authority, fled with a clatter of hobnailed boots, and Mrs. Halesworth returned to the bedside. She chafed Martha's cold hands, feeling beneath her fingers the hard ridges of toil that had never known a moment's respite, and as she did so, a profound penitence took hold of her spirit—a feeling that was not merely the impulse of pity, but a clear, analytic perception of the wrong she had done in imputing pride to a woman whose only crime had been an unconquerable determination to preserve her human dignity against the encroachments of a charity that demanded submission in return.

Martha opened her eyes, which were bright with the fever, and recognized the face bending over her. A faint flush crept over her wan cheeks, whether of shame or resentment it would have been hard to tell.

"Mrs. Halesworth," she said, in a voice that seemed to come from a great distance, "you shouldn't. I can do without the parish."

"I am not the parish, Martha," said Mrs. Halesworth, with a gentleness that surprised even herself, for it was a tone she had not used since the days before her husband's presentation to the living. "I am only a woman who has been a fool, and who sees another woman in want of a fire."

Martha was silent, for the effort of speaking had exhausted her, and she lay watching the rector's wife with the wary, half-trusting look of a creature that has been hunted and is unsure whether the hand extended to it carries food or a snare. Mrs. Halesworth, meanwhile, set about restoring the fire with her own hands, breaking up the faggots the boy had brought and kindling them with a skill that spoke of a childhood spent in a less elevated station. The flames caught, and a ruddy glow spread through the room, driving back the shadows and the damp, and as the warmth penetrated to the bed, Martha's rigid features relaxed by a barely perceptible degree.

When Bessy arrived with the posset and the hot bricks, Mrs. Halesworth herself held the cup to Martha's lips, supporting her head with a firm, gentle hand.

"Drink it, Martha," she said. "It is not parish gruel, nor yet the leavings of the gentry. It is only good ale and herbs, such as my mother used to make in Leicestershire when I was a girl."

Martha drank, and as the warmth spread through her, she turned her face away and wept—the first tears she had shed since her husband's death, drawn from her not by her own suffering, but by the unexpected gentleness of a hand she had believed to be raised only in judgment. Mrs. Halesworth, seeing this, sat down on the edge of the bed and took the widow's hand in hers, and for a long while neither spoke, for they understood that the reconciliation between them was of a kind too deep for words, being founded on the recognition of a common human frailty.

"I was a proud woman, Mrs. Halesworth," Martha said at last, in a tremulous whisper. "But it's a hard thing to be beholden."

"It is a harder thing to be alone, Martha," replied the rector's wife, with a gravity that acknowledged her own share in the guilt of their separation. "And I was a proud woman too, in my way—proud of giving what I should have been glad to share."

The fire burned steadily now, casting a warm light over the two women, and outside the cottage the fenland lay quiet under its mantle of frost, indifferent to the small drama of reconciliation that was being enacted within, yet not untouched by the larger movement of human fellowship that makes the endurance of such landscapes possible. Mrs. Halesworth stayed until the evening, nursing Martha with a patience that grew more natural with each hour, and when she left, it was with the understanding that she would return on the morrow—not as a benefactress, but as a neighbour.
