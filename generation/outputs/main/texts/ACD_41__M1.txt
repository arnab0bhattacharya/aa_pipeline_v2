“It is a singular thing,” observed my companion, as he stood with his back to the fire and surveyed the lady who had taken a seat opposite us, “how often the most trifling detail, a mere chance word or an unconsidered action, will serve to unlock a situation which appears, upon the surface, to be utterly impenetrable. You tell us, Miss Vane, that your father, Mr. Josiah Vane, received a parcel some three weeks ago. Let us begin there. Describe it to me.”

Miss Eleanor Vane was a young woman of perhaps five-and-twenty, handsome in a severe and resolute fashion. Her dark travelling-dress was of good but plain material, and her gloved hands were clasped tightly in her lap, betraying a nervous energy which her composed features sought to conceal.

“It was a small, brown-paper parcel, Mr. Thornton,” she said, her voice steady. “It was addressed in a rough hand to my father at our home in Bayswater. The postmark was smudged, but the paper had an odd, dry smell, like dust and sun-baked earth. Inside was a lump of quartz and gold—a nugget, my father called it—and a scrap of coarse canvas. Upon the canvas were four initials, stitched in blue thread: J.V., T.C., M.D., and H.F.”

“And your father’s reaction?”

“It was remarkable. He went quite white. He dismissed the parlour-maid who had brought it and locked the door. He would not show the contents to me or to my brother. He only said that it was a matter from a past he had buried and that it must not concern us. But from that hour, he was a changed man. He became restless, his sleep was broken, and he began to carry a revolver in the pocket of his morning-coat. He has not left the house without it since.”

My own notes, which I had been scribbling in my own fashion, contained the word ‘Nugget’, followed by ‘Four Initials’ and ‘Revolver’. It was a promising, if bewildering, start. My companion, the private investigator Mr. Julian Thornton, merely steepled his long fingers and gazed at the pattern on the carpet.

“The canvas,” he said at last. “Was it new?”

“No. It was old, weathered, and stiff. It looked as though it had been cut from a sail or a tent.”

“And the initials. Were they professionally done?”

“Quite the reverse. They were rough, clumsy stitches, as if made with a sailmaker’s needle and twine by one unaccustomed to such work.”

Thornton nodded slowly. “Good. A professional would have used a fine thread and a steady hand. This is a message, not a decoration. It is a reminder, sent from one man to another. The nugget is the key. It is not merely a piece of gold; it is a symbol. It speaks of a shared past, a common discovery. Your father, I take it, made his fortune in the Australian goldfields?”

“He did. He went out to Ballarat in ’54 as a young man and returned twenty years later a wealthy man. He rarely speaks of that time.”

“He will speak of it now,” said Thornton, with a quiet confidence that was a feature of his method. “Now, as to his disappearance. He left the house yesterday evening at seven o’clock for his customary walk in Kensington Gardens. He was in his usual attire, but for a heavy grey ulster overcoat against the chill. He took the revolver. And he has not returned. The police, I presume, have been informed?”

“They have, Mr. Thornton. A constable came this morning, made a note of the particulars, and said that a man of his age and habits would doubtless turn up in a day or two. They seemed to think he had merely gone off on some private errand.”

“The official mind is a wonderfully placid instrument,” remarked Thornton, with a dry smile. “It is content to wait for events to explain themselves. We, I trust, are of a more impatient disposition. Come, Watson. Let us take a hansom to Bayswater. The scene of the action, Miss Vane, is always the most eloquent of witnesses.”

The Vane residence in Porchester Terrace was a handsome, four-storeyed house of white stucco, typical of the prosperous merchants of the district. The butler, a solemn man named Hemmings, let us in. The air in the hall was heavy with the scent of beeswax and a faint, lingering aroma of my father’s favourite shag tobacco. Thornton’s eyes, sharp and grey, swept over the hall-stand, the polished floor, the heavy curtains.

“Your father’s study, if you please, Miss Vane,” he said. “And then we shall see the parcel.”

The study was a large, book-lined room. A fine Persian rug covered the floor before a mahogany desk. Thornton moved about it with a cat-like tread, his gaze missing nothing. He paused by the window, which overlooked a small, walled garden.

“The latch is fastened,” he observed. “And the dust on the sill is undisturbed. He did not leave by this route. Miss Vane, you say he was a creature of habit?”

“Extremely so. His walk, his newspaper, his pipe at the same hour each evening.”

“Then he was intercepted. Or he went to a pre-arranged meeting. Let us see the object of all this trouble.”

Miss Vane produced the parcel from her father’s desk. Thornton handled the nugget, a rough, yellow lump still flecked with white quartz, as a connoisseur might handle a rare gem. Then he examined the scrap of canvas. His forefinger traced the crude stitching.

“J.V.,” he murmured. “Josiah Vane. Your father. But who are T.C., M.D., and H.F.? They are the key to this entire affair. A quartet, then. Four men in a remote and dangerous place, making a discovery. A rich one, judging by this sample. And now, one of them sends a reminder. Why? For money? For silence? For revenge?”

He fell silent, his gaze fixed on the nugget. I knew the signs of his intense concentration. He was building a theory, testing its strength against the facts.

“Miss Vane,” he said suddenly, “your father’s boots. The ones he wore for his walk. Are they available?”

A look of surprise crossed her face, but she complied. The boots were brought—stout, well-worn leather brogues. Thornton took one and examined the sole with a small pocket lens.

“Hemmings,” he called to the butler, who stood respectfully by the door. “You are familiar with Mr. Vane’s tread. Is this the impression he would make?”

The butler bent over the boot. “Yes, sir. The master has a slight outward roll to his left foot, sir. You can see it in the wear on the heel.”

“Excellent,” said Thornton. “A most useful observation. Now, we must return to Kensington Gardens. The constable on duty last night is our next port of call.”

We found the man, a stout and rather bored-looking individual named Perkins, at the gate nearest to the Vane residence. Thornton, with a manner of such quiet authority that the man at once became attentive, questioned him closely.

“Did you see a gentleman in a grey ulster and a bowler hat leave the park after seven o’clock last night?”

“Many such, sir.”

“Did any of them appear agitated? Did any of them speak to anyone?”

Perkins scratched his chin. “Come to think of it, sir, there was one. A tall, thin cove in a long coat, like a sea-faring man. He was loitering by the gate for the best part of an hour. Our gentleman came out just as the lamps were being lit. This other fellow fell in beside him. They didn’t stop to chat, just walked off together towards the Bayswater Road. The merchant looked a bit grim, I thought, but he didn’t seem afraid.”

“A sea-faring man,” mused Thornton. “Sun-browned? Weather-beaten?”

“That’s him, sir. Looked as if he’d spent half his life on a deck.”

We thanked the constable and walked on in silence for some minutes. The November dusk was closing in, and the gas lamps began to cast long, wavering shadows across the pavement.

“It takes shape, Watson,” said Thornton at last, breaking his reverie. “It becomes coherent. Four men in Australia. A rich find. But such things are often attended by violence, by betrayal. Perhaps one man was left for dead. Perhaps one took more than his share. Twenty years is a long time for a grievance to fester, but it is not too long. The parcel was a warning. Vane knew what it meant. He took his revolver not for defence against a footpad, but for a battle he knew was coming. He went to meet this man, T.C., or M.D., or H.F., not in fear, but in grim resolution. And he has not returned. Therefore, the meeting did not end as he had hoped.”

“But where is he, Thornton? If there has been foul play, where is the body? Where is the struggle?”

“Ah,” said my companion, a flicker of triumph in his eyes. “That is the final, and most telling, point. You heard what the constable said. They walked away together. There was no struggle in the park. The answer, my dear fellow, lies not in the park, but in the destination. A man with a sea-faring aspect, a man from the Australian past… he would not be staying at the Savoy. He would be near the docks, or in some lodging-house where questions are not asked. And he would need a quiet place to conclude an old account. I fancy, Watson, that a visit to the shipping offices and the more obscure hotels of Rotherhithe is in order. The name of the man we seek is likely to be found on the passenger list of a vessel recently arrived from Melbourne. And I have a shrewd suspicion that the initials on that canvas will match the name on the manifest.”
