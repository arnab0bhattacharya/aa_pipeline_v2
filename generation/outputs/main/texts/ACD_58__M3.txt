It was upon a raw November evening, with a damp fog creeping up from the Regent’s Canal and settling in a greasy film over the lamps of Upper Street, that the singular affair of the leaden coffin at Islington first came to the attention of my friend Mr. Aldous Croft. We had been sitting in our lodgings at number 14, Thornhaugh Street, he with a volume of Petronius balanced upon his knee, and I with the evening paper, when our landlady announced that a gentleman desired an immediate consultation.

Croft laid aside his book and regarded our visitor with that intent, questioning gaze which I had learned to recognize as the prelude to a most exacting scrutiny. The man who entered was tall, gaunt, and dressed in a suit of funereal black which had seen considerable service. His hands were large and remarkably clean, bearing the unmistakable softness of one accustomed to handling fine fabrics and polished wood rather than coarse labour. His face was long and melancholy, the face of a man whose trade kept him perpetually in the shadow of grief, yet his grey eyes were restless and troubled in a manner that had nothing to do with his professional melancholy.

"Mr. Silas Grindle, of Grindle and Phipps, undertakers, of Liverpool Road," said he, producing a card from a case that was worn at the corners. "I crave your pardon for the intrusion, sir, but a matter has arisen in my business which passes the bounds of my experience and touches, I fear, upon something darker than any honest burial."

"Pray be seated, Mr. Grindle," said Croft, with that quiet courtesy which put every sort of man at his ease. "Dr. Forsythe will bear me out when I say that nothing which concerns the proper observance of our last rites is trivial to me. You speak of a burial. I gather it was not altogether regular."

"It was regular in every outward particular, sir, and that is precisely what alarms me." The undertaker sat forward in his chair, his large hands clasped between his knees. "The facts are shortly these. On Tuesday last I received an order from a Mr. Joshua Lassiter, of 9, Cloudesley Square, for the funeral of his sister, a Miss Arabella Lassiter, who had died, so the certificate stated, of heart failure. The arrangements were made with every appearance of propriety. Mr. Lassiter desired a private funeral, no viewing, no flowers, and the interment to take place with all dispatch in the family vault at St. Andrew’s, Hornsey. I thought it somewhat hasty, but grief takes strange forms, and I am not the man to press questions upon the bereaved."

Croft nodded. "You are a man of professional tact, I perceive. But something occurred which your tact could not accommodate."

"It was the coffin, sir. I am forty-seven years of age, and I have carried more coffins to more graves than I care to number. I know the weight of a coffin and its occupant as surely as a butcher knows the weight of a joint. Miss Arabella Lassiter was, by all accounts, a small woman, rather wasted by her long illness. The coffin I supplied was of polished elm, standard size, and I should judge the total weight at not more than eleven stone. Yet when my men and I lifted it to convey it to the hearse, it was all we could do to manage it. I should say it weighed a full sixteen stone, perhaps more."

"Sixteen stone," repeated Croft, his fingers tapping softly upon the arm of his chair. "A discrepancy of five stone. That is considerable."

"Considerable, sir! It was monstrous. My assistant, a young fellow named Bodkin, remarked upon it at once. I bade him hold his tongue, for the family was within earshot. But I made it my business to examine the coffin privately before the lid was finally screwed down. The plate bore the name and dates correctly. The lining was undisturbed. There was no sign of any foreign object within the shell. Yet the weight was there, a dead, oppressive weight that no empty coffin and one slight woman could account for."

"And the funeral proceeded?"

"It did, sir. Mr. Lassiter was urgent upon the point. He stood in the hallway, a tall, pale man with a nervous manner and a habit of pulling at his cuffs, and he pressed for the burial to be concluded that very day. I represented to him that the vault required preparation and that my men were not at liberty. He offered a considerable sum over and above my charges if the thing could be managed by Thursday. I consented. The coffin was conveyed to St. Andrew’s on Thursday afternoon and placed in the vault, but the sealing of the vault was deferred until to-day, Saturday, owing to some difficulty with the mason."

"And the vault stands open at this moment?"

"It does, sir. And that is what brought me to you. I have not slept these two nights. I am a respectable man, Mr. Croft, and my name is above my door. But I cannot seal that vault with a clear conscience until I know what lies within that coffin."

Croft rose from his chair and went to the window, where he stood for a full minute staring out into the fog. When he turned back, his face had taken on that expression of concentrated energy which I had learned to associate with the approach of a crisis.

"You have done perfectly right to come to me, Mr. Grindle. The matter is, as you suspected, a grave one. I shall want two things from you: the precise dimensions of the coffin, and the name of the mason who is to seal the vault. Dr. Forsythe and I will meet you at St. Andrew’s at eleven o’clock to-night. Bring your assistant Bodkin, and a lantern. And, Mr. Grindle, not a word of this to Mr. Lassiter."

The undertaker promised and withdrew. Croft turned to me with a gleam in his eye. "Well, Forsythe, what do you make of it?"

"The extra weight suggests that something was placed in the coffin beside the body," said I.

"Undoubtedly. But what? Not lead, for lead would have altered the balance in a manner Mr. Grindle, an experienced man, would have detected at once as a lining. No, the weight was distributed as though it were part of the occupant. Five stone, Forsythe! What weighs five stone, is compact, and would be concealed within a coffin without altering its outward appearance?"

I confessed I could not say.

"Then we must go and see," said he, reaching for his ulster. "But I have my suspicions, and if they are correct, Mr. Joshua Lassiter will have more to answer for than an irregular burial."

We met Grindle and his assistant at the lych-gate of St. Andrew’s shortly before eleven. The night was thick and cold, and the churchyard was a place of deep shadows and dripping yews. The mason, a taciturn Welshman named Evans, led us to the vault, a substantial structure of Portland stone at the eastern boundary of the grounds. The iron gate stood open, and within, by the light of two lanterns, we could see the coffin resting upon a stone shelf.

Croft knelt beside it and ran his hands over the elm with the delicacy of a surgeon. He produced a small screwdriver from his pocket and, with a few swift turns, removed the brass plate from the lid. Beneath it, where there should have been nothing but plain wood, there was a second plate, of dull, heavy metal.

"Platinum," said Croft quietly. "Worth, at current rates, something in the neighbourhood of eight hundred pounds. There is your five stone, Mr. Grindle. Miss Arabella Lassiter, it would seem, was not the only party travelling to her long home."

The undertaker stared, his face ghastly in the lantern light. "But how came it there, sir? And who—"

"That, I think, is the work of Mr. Lassiter’s own hands. He is a clerk, I believe, at the assay offices of the Royal Mint. A man with access to refined metals and a pressing need to dispose of them before an audit. His sister’s death provided the opportunity, and the sealed coffin the means. He counted upon the sanctity of the grave to protect his theft, and upon your professional discretion to ask no questions."

"But the real Miss Arabella—is she truly dead?" I asked.

"Oh, yes," said Croft, replacing the plate with practised care. "The death was genuine enough. It is the burial that is fraudulent. Come, Forsythe. We have enough here to interest Inspector Brayfield of the City police. Mr. Grindle, you may seal your vault in the morning. But I fancy Mr. Lassiter will not be present to see it done."
