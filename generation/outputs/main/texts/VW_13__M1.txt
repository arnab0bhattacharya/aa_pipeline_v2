The wind, coming off the sea, had a salt and a chalky grit to it, and it lifted the edges of their skirts and pulled at the ribbons of their hats as they walked along the shingle. It was the same wind, it was the same sea, and the light fell upon them both with an impartial, watery brilliance, yet to each sister it seemed to strike a different chord. For Beatrice, who was married, the light was a kind of harsh interrogation, laying bare the fine lines at the corners of her eyes, the tired set of her mouth, the whole weary edifice of her life. For Helen, who was not, it was merely light, a clean, cold substance in which one could see for miles, to the smudge of a distant sail, to the very edge of the world.

The children, Edmund and little Clara, were a few yards ahead, a small riot of energy against the grey and dun of the beach. Edmund was flinging stones at the waves, his thin arms a blur, and Clara was gathering shells into a pinafore already stiff with salt, her concentration absolute. To Beatrice, they were the sum of her existence and the evidence of her exhaustion. She watched her son’s fierce, joyous assault upon the sea and felt a pang, not of love, but of a profound and weary recognition. She was that boy’s mother; she would be that boy’s mother until the sea itself wore the cliffs away. The thought was a stone in her own stomach. She looked at Helen, who was watching Clara, a small, quiet smile on her lips. Helen’s face, unlined and clear, seemed to hold no such weight. It was a face upon which the world had not yet written its accounts. And a sudden, sharp envy, like the cry of the gulls overhead, pierced Beatrice’s calm. It was not for Helen’s freedom, not for her unburdened days, but for the very stillness of her, for the self that remained, unwritten, unsacrificed, whole. She envied her sister everything.

And Helen, turning from the child to her sister, felt a different current. She saw the lines of weariness on Beatrice’s face and thought only of their depth, of the life that had carved them. She saw the way Beatrice’s hand rested, instinctively, on the back of Clara’s neck as the little girl stumbled on a loose stone, a gesture so swift and sure it was like a second heartbeat. It was this that Helen envied. Not the children themselves, for she loved them with a clear, uncomplicated affection, but the state of being so utterly necessary to another. She saw in her sister a purpose so profound it was almost a gravity, a force that held this small, noisy world together. Her own life, by contrast, seemed a series of pleasant, but weightless, observations. She had her books, her watercolours, her quiet rooms in Bloomsbury. She had a self, yes, but it was a self that answered to no one, that left no wake. It was a boat, she thought, with no anchor, and she envied her sister the glorious, terrible weight of the anchor that was her family. She envied her sister everything.

They turned back towards the town, the wind now at their backs, pushing them along. The pier stretched out its skeletal arm, and the little coloured lights of the town began to prick the gathering dusk. “They’ll want ices,” Beatrice said, the words torn from her by the wind, and Helen nodded, her throat suddenly tight. In the narrow, sandy lane that led up from the sea, the wind’s voice fell to a whisper. Edmund ran ahead, his shouts echoing off the flint walls of the cottages. Beatrice felt a sudden, irrational desire to run after him, to be as light and as thoughtless as he was, to shed the years like a heavy coat. The envy was a physical thing now, a dull ache behind her ribs. To be Helen, to walk into an evening that held no duties, no small, hot hands to wash, no stories to be told, no marriage to be maintained in the quiet, airless rooms of the house—it was a paradise she could only imagine. And she hated herself for imagining it.

Helen, walking beside her, saw the slump of her sister’s shoulders and felt a wave of pity so strong it was almost love. She thought of her own empty room waiting for her in London, the single lamp, the book left face down on the table. It was a life of such peace, and such a profound and aching solitude. To be Beatrice, to walk into a house full of noise and life, to be the fixed point around which these small planets revolved, to have a claim upon the future so solid and real as those two children—this was the substance of life itself. Her own life was a shadow-play. She reached out and took Beatrice’s arm, and for a moment they walked thus, bound together. “It has been a good week,” Helen said, her voice low.

“Yes,” Beatrice replied, and the single word held the whole of it: the fatigue, the love, the envy, the peace. The wind had dropped. The tide was coming in, its gentle, rhythmic shushing the only sound now, a sound that promised nothing and offered everything, and seemed to speak for them both.
