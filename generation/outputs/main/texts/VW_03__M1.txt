The light in the lecture hall was a strange, dusty gold, a light that seemed to have substance, that fell not just upon the rows of faces but into the very air between them, illuminating the slow drift of motes. It was a light quite unlike the clean, watery light of the mornings in their Somerset house, the light that fell on the scrubbed flagstones and the ordered jars in the surgery. Here, everything was softened, made ambiguous. And he was speaking, Dr. Alistair Finch, her brother, his voice—so familiar in its clipped, impatient cadences as he called for more hot water or complained of the vicar’s rheumatism—was now a public instrument. It filled the hushed room, laying out the principles of antisepsis with a clarity that seemed to belong not to the man who left his muddy boots in the hall, but to this other creature, this man of science, standing before a screen of diagrams that were like strange, beautiful constellations.

Beside him, or rather, just behind and to the side, sat Eleanor. She felt herself to be a part of his shadow, a necessary but unobtrusive appendage. She had come to arrange his papers, to see that his shirts were starched, to be the quiet, competent fact of his life made portable. But the light of this strange Parisian gold seemed to catch her too. When a gentleman in a dark suit leaned across the aisle during the interval to ask Alistair a question, his eyes flickered to her, and he smiled. “Your wife is a great support to you, I am sure, Doctor,” he said, not to Alistair, but to the air between them. And Eleanor felt a curious little lurch, a silent, internal turning-over. She did not correct him. She merely smiled back, a small, closed smile, and in that instant she felt herself to be a new person. She was not Eleanor Finch, the spinster sister, the keeper of keys and accounts. She was *Madame Finch*. The title was a cloak, and she drew it around her, feeling its unexpected warmth.

At the dinner that evening, in a room blazing with electric light that made the silver and the crystal glitter with a hard, insistent brilliance, the transformation was complete. The name cards, printed in a fine, elegant hand, read *Docteur et Madame Finch*. She watched the words as if they belonged to strangers. The man on her right, a French physician with a neat grey beard and eyes that held a kind of weary intelligence, did not ask her about her brother’s work. He asked her about the light. “You find our electric light harsh, perhaps?” he said, his English careful and accented. “It is new, you see. It does not have the soul of a candle. It shows everything, and perhaps it is better that some things are left to the shadows.”

And because he was looking at her, really looking at her, and because the wine had made the room seem to hum and sway with a life of its own, she spoke. She spoke of the candles in their parish church, how they made the stone saints seem to breathe, and of the single oil lamp in their drawing-room at home, which threw such long, dancing shadows that the furniture seemed to creep and change its shape in the evenings. She spoke of the way morning light in the country was a slow, grey thing that seeped in under the doors before it filled the room. She spoke of these things, these small, domestic particulars, and the Frenchman, whose name was Dubois, listened with a profound and serious attention that she had never known. He did not interrupt. He did not glance at Alistair to see if he would confirm her observations. He simply listened, his head tilted slightly, as if she were telling him something of great scientific importance. When she finished, he nodded slowly. “Yes,” he said. “The soul of a candle. It is very true.”

She felt a warmth spread through her that had nothing to do with the wine. It was the warmth of being seen, of being heard. At home, when she spoke of the way the frost lay on the yew hedge, Alistair would nod, but his mind would be on the case he was writing up, on the new sterilising agent he had ordered. Her words were a kind of pleasant background noise to the more important music of his thoughts. But here, in this glittering, foreign room, with her new name like a jewel at her throat, her words were the music. She looked down the table at her brother. He was deep in conversation with a German professor, his hands moving expressively as he described some new technique. He was magnificent, in his way, a sun around which these lesser planets orbited. But for the first time, she did not feel herself to be merely a satellite. She felt she had a light of her own, a small, steady candle-flame, newly lit.

Later, walking back to the hotel through streets that were still alive with the clatter of carriages and the murmur of voices, the gas lamps casting pools of liquid amber on the pavement, she was silent. The city was a great, breathing creature, and she was walking in its veins. Alistair was talking, his voice full of a restless energy. “A remarkable man, that Dubois. Did you hear his paper on the treatment of gangrene? Absolutely revolutionary. I must get a copy of his monograph.” He was still in the lecture hall, still on the platform. He had not noticed the small, quiet revolution that had taken place at the dinner table.

She looked at him, at the familiar, dear, impatient line of his jaw, and felt a sudden, piercing tenderness mixed with a strange new distance. He had brought her here to be his shadow, and in doing so, had accidentally allowed her to step into the light. It was a light she knew would be extinguished the moment they reached the quiet of their own house, the moment she became Eleanor again, the moment the name on the doorbell read simply *Dr. A. Finch*. But for now, the light was hers. She held the memory of Dubois’s listening face close, a secret, warm thing in the cool Parisian night. She was Madame Finch, for one night more, and the city, with all its brilliant, indifferent light, had seen her.
