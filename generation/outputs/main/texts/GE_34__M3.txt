It is a common error in the minds of those who have never been deeply tethered to a particular patch of earth to suppose that the return to a familiar landscape must necessarily soothe the human spirit. We are apt to imagine that the sight of native hills and the scent of habitual grasses act as a gentle narcotic upon the wounds of memory. But the soul is not a vessel to be filled by mere scenery; it brings its own inward atmosphere to colour the outward day, and the very familiarity of an old home may but serve to throw the alienation of a changed self into more merciless relief. So it was with Stephen Holloway when, on a pale March evening in the year 1816, he came back over the brow of Edale Edge to look down upon the grey stone rooftops of Thornfield Farm.

He had gone away five years ago, a raw and thoughtless youth, to follow the drums in the Peninsula, carrying with him little but a head full of vague heroics and a heart stung by the petulant pride of his sweetheart, Janet Bellamy. He had returned a man bronzed and scarred, holding within his chest a silence that had gathered like sediment through long years of marching and bloodshed, and carrying, too, a half-acknowledged hope that he might find Janet weeping for his return. But the news had met him at the village inn: Janet was married, and to his own elder brother, Robert.

The arrangement of their domestic life at Thornfield was a practical compromise born of necessity and a certain stubborn familial endurance. Robert, a broad-shouldered, slow-moving man, whose passions were chiefly invested in the rotation of root crops and the maintenance of dry-stone walls, had taken the farm on his shoulders after their father’s stroke. He had offered Stephen a labourer’s place, not with any effusive generosity, but with that blunt, unspoken recognition of kinship which belongs to men of the northern dales, who feel their duties too heavily to phrase them lightly. Stephen had accepted, and lived under the same roof, eating at the same oaken board, occupying the narrow chamber next to the newly wedded pair.

If Stephen’s inward conflict was a dark and boiling current, Janet’s was a quieter, more subtle torture, akin to the slow erosion of a limestone crag beneath a steady drizzle. She had not married Robert out of any lightness of heart. During the first two years of Stephen’s absence, when letters had come fitfully and the rumour of battles had cast a shadow over the valley, she had waited, enduring the small humiliations of a woman whose promised man is absent and whose prospects are uncertain. But Robert had been there, a steadfast, homely presence, repairing her father’s byre, speaking plainly of stock and tenancy, until the boundary between neighbourly dependence and marital obligation had worn so thin that she had stepped across it almost without knowing. She had believed Stephen lost to her in the vast machinery of war, swallowed by that monstrous unknown which we call History, and she had built her life on the rational supposition of his death. Now that he was returned, the supposition was shattered, and she found herself living face to face with the living evidence of her own inconstancy.

The evenings in the farmhouse kitchen were a curious study in human restraint. The firelight played upon the rough-hewn beams and the stone flags, casting long, flickering shadows that seemed to mock the stillness of the three figures gathered there. Janet moved quietly between the table and the hearth, her hands busy with the earthenware crocks and the wooden spoons, her eyes carefully lowered, as if she feared that a direct glance might carry the weight of a spoken confession.

"Eat, Stephen," Robert would say, pushing the bread trencher towards his brother with a heavy hand. "Tha' looks fair worn oop wi' t' day. T' lads i' t' top field said tha' wert mending t' gap by t' clough."

"Aye," Stephen would answer, his voice curt, stripped of all unnecessary syllables, as if the economy of his speech might prevent the escape of some dangerous emotion. "Stones were loose. I've packed 'em tight enow."

"I'll need to look at it," Robert would continue, impervious to the tension, or perhaps merely choosing to ignore it with that sturdy capacity for non-comprehension which is often the saving grace of a practical man. "If t' hoggets get out, we'll have t' master fro' t' Hall on us like a shot."

Janet would pour the small beer, her fingers trembling almost imperceptibly, and Stephen, as he took the mug from her hand, would feel the ghost of her touch like a sudden, sharp vibration in his nerves, awakening a chorus of remembered sensations—the feel of her waist in the orchard at harvest-time, the scent of her hair, the sound of her laughter before the war had taught him that all earthly sounds were fragile.

There are moments when the very pressure of unspoken words creates a tension more perilous than any open quarrel. Stephen, labouring in the fields through the spring, felt the slow accumulation of a bitterness that was not simply jealousy, but a profound resentment against the whole order of things. He had been a sergeant; he had commanded men; he had known the sharp, decisive action of life and death. Now he was a hired hand, driving the plough across the furrows his brother owned, and sleeping in a room where the faint creak of the adjacent bedstead was a nightly torment.

One Sunday afternoon, when Robert had gone to the upper pasture to inspect a sick ewe, Stephen and Janet were left alone in the kitchen. The fire burned low, and the air was thick with the scent of dried lavender and baking bread. Janet was seated at the spinning-wheel, her foot pressing the treadle in a monotonous rhythm, her face pale and drawn. Stephen stood by the window, looking out at the grey slopes of the Edge, where the mist was creeping down like a slow, deliberate tide.

"Janet," he said, without turning round.

The treadle stopped. The silence in the kitchen was suddenly absolute, broken only by the faint ticking of the Dutch clock on the mantelpiece.

"Stephen," she answered, her voice barely above a whisper, "tha mustna."

"Mustna what?" he asked, turning to face her. "Mustna speak? Mustna look at thee? What is it I mustna do, Janet? For I am thinking there is little left for me to do but breathe, and even that seems a task too heavy some days."

She looked up at him then, and in her eyes he saw a misery that matched his own. It was not the look of a woman who had betrayed, but of one who was herself betrayed—betrayed by time, by circumstance, by that very complexity of human motive which makes our choices seem inevitable in retrospect and yet so arbitrary in the making.

"I never meant for this," she said, her voice trembling. "When I heard nought fro' thee—when they said t' regiment was cut up at Badajoz—I thought tha wert gone, Stephen. I thought tha wert dead, and I were left alone, and Robert—he was good to me. He were steady. What else could I do?"

She spoke with the simple, unadorned directness of her class and upbringing, yet beneath the homely dialect there lay a depth of feeling that no refinement of language could have heightened. Stephen looked at her, and the anger within him struggled against a wave of compassion that was, in its way, more painful than the anger itself. He saw that she was trapped as securely as he was, caught in the web of consequences that had been woven by the slow, blind fingers of fate.

He moved towards her, and she stood up from the spinning-wheel, her hands clasped tightly in front of her. For a long moment they stood thus, separated by a yard of stone floor, the firelight casting their shadows in a single, merged shape upon the wall. Stephen raised his hand, almost involuntarily, as if to touch her hair, and then let it fall again to his side.

"I'll not break it," he said, his voice thick and uneven. "I'll not break thy home, Janet. Robert is my brother, and he is a better man than I am, I reckon. He has made a life here, and I will not tear it apart."

He turned away from her and went to the door. The mist had thickened outside, and the yard was dark. He paused on the threshold, breathing in the cold, damp air of the Derbyshire evening, feeling the chill seep into his bones. Behind him, Janet made a small, choked sound, half sob, half exclamation.

"Stephen—"

But he had already stepped out into the mist, pulling the door shut behind him. He walked across the yard to the stable, where the horses shifted restlessly in their stalls, and leaned his forehead against the rough wood of the partition. The smell of hay and horse and damp earth surrounded him, the familiar, homely smells of the life he had been born to. And in that darkness, with the sounds of the farm around him, he felt the slow, heavy settling of resignation, not as a sudden surrender, but as a gradual, geological process, like the pressing down of strata beneath the weight of accumulating years. He knew that he would stay, that he would labour in the fields, and eat at the table, and sleep in the narrow room, and that the pain of it would not diminish but would become, in time, a part of the very substance of his being, woven into the fabric of his days as inextricably as the roots of the old hawthorn were woven into the limestone of the wall against which he leaned.
