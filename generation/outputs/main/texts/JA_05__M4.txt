The house in Upper Brook Street was reckoned, by the standards of the neighbourhood, a very creditable house; and Mrs. Ellinor Vane, who had taken it for the season, was at no small pains to have it thought so. She was a widow of independent fortune and of an age when a woman of spirit may yet hope to be admired without the trouble of deserving it, and she had brought her niece to town with the double design of improving the girl's manners and of showing the town what a handsome equipage and a well-dressed aunt could do.

Miss Harriet Ashby was the daughter of a clergyman in Somersetshire, and had come up from the country with a heart prepared to wonder at everything and a wardrobe prepared for nothing. Her aunt had promised her pleasures, and had kept her promise in the manner of a woman who finds it cheaper to promise than to perform; for Mrs. Vane's pleasures were chiefly of the kind that are paid for by other people, and her generosity, though loud, was of a nature to be felt rather in the hearing than in the pocket.

It was not long before Harriet was taken up by a set of fine young ladies whose acquaintance her aunt was proud to own. There was Miss Delacourt, who wore pearls by day and spoke of her cousin the baronet; there was Miss Wharton, whose laugh was always ready and never quite sincere; and there was Mrs. Rushworth, a widow like Mrs. Vane, but younger and more expensive, who had a way of pressing a girl's hand and calling her "my dear" that Harriet thought the very height of elegance. They drove out together, they drank tea together, they spoke of milliners and of men; and Harriet, who had never in her life spent so much money in a fortnight, began to think that London was a place where happiness was to be bought, and that the price, though high, was worth the paying.

The price, indeed, was the first thing that began to trouble her. A subscription to a concert, a pair of gloves that were not wanted, a raffle in which she won a work-bag and lost two guineas, a present of lace for a friend who would have been better pleased with her silence—these expenses came upon her one by one, and each was presented by Miss Delacourt as a matter of course, and by Miss Wharton as a matter of necessity, and by Mrs. Rushworth as a matter of kindness. Harriet's purse, which had seemed so ample in Somersetshire, grew thin in Upper Brook Street; and she began to perceive that the friendship of fine ladies is a commodity that must be paid for in ready money.

She said as much, in a moment of confidence, to her aunt; and Mrs. Vane laughed, and said that a girl must learn to spend before she could learn to live, and that her niece was very welcome to anything in her purse. But the purse was not opened, and the lecture was not repeated, and Harriet began to suspect that her aunt's generosity was of that kind which is most easily exercised in words.

It was about this time that she began to hear things. She heard them first from Miss Wharton, who had a way of saying a thing and then denying she had said it, and who observed one afternoon, when the tea was poured out and the talk had turned upon reputations, that Mrs. Vane was a very charming woman, and that it was a great pity she had not been so prudent as she was charming. "But then," said Miss Wharton, with a little laugh, "who is prudent in town? We are all of us a little imprudent, are we not? Only some of us are found out."

Harriet did not understand her; and said so. Miss Wharton looked at her with an air of pity that was worse than any explanation, and said that she had forgotten Miss Ashby was from the country, and that in the country, she believed, they still thought a woman's character was her own. "In town," she added, "we know better. A woman's character is her husband's, or her brother's, or her aunt's; and if her aunt has none to give her—why, my dear, she must do as she can."

The words sank deeper than the speaker intended. Harriet began to observe her aunt with new eyes, and to observe the world's treatment of her; and she saw that Mrs. Vane was received with a kindness that was not quite respect, and that the gentlemen who called in Upper Brook Street were of a sort that no woman of unblemished name would have chosen to receive alone. There was Mr. Ellwood, who was always going to be married and never was; there was Captain Ferrars, who had a wife in the country and a laugh in town; there was Mr. Sotherton, who spoke of Mrs. Vane as a woman of spirit, and who had once been heard to say that she was the sort of woman who might have been anything if she had not been so much of everything. Harriet did not understand all of this, but she understood enough; and what she understood made her ashamed.

It was on a Tuesday, when the rain had kept them within doors and the fine ladies had gone away in their carriage, that Mrs. Vane said something that brought the matter to a point. "You are very grave, my dear," said she. "I hope you are not going to be one of those girls who are always thinking. It is a very bad habit, and it spoils the complexion."

"I have been thinking, ma'am," said Harriet, "of what Miss Wharton said about you."

"Miss Wharton," said Mrs. Vane, with a smile that was not quite easy, "is a very good girl, and says a great many things. What did she say?"

"She said that a woman's character was her aunt's, and that if her aunt had none to give her, she must do as she could."

There was a silence. Mrs. Vane's colour rose, and then, what was worse, it fell. "Miss Wharton," she said at last, "is a fool, and a very impertinent fool; and I shall be glad if you will not repeat her words in my house. But since she has said them, I will tell you the truth, and you may make what you can of it. I have lived in town a great many years, and I have not always lived as I ought. I have been imprudent, and I have been talked of, and I have been forgiven; and I have brought you here to give you the pleasure of my acquaintance, and to show you a little of the world, and to give you a husband if I can. And if you are going to be very good and very proud, and to refuse to know me because the world has not always been kind to me, why, you may go back to Somersetshire, and marry your father's curate, and be very respectable and very dull, and I shall not prevent you."

Harriet listened to this with a beating heart. She was a girl of good sense, and of a temper that had been formed in a quiet parsonage, and she had been taught to think that truth was better than finery, and that a good name was better than a great acquaintance. She looked at her aunt, and saw, for the first time, not the fine lady of Upper Brook Street, but a woman who had spent her character and was spending her money, and who was trying to buy for her niece the very things she had lost for herself. And she was sorry for her, and ashamed of her, and angry with her, all at once; and she was more angry with herself for having been so long pleased with what was not worth the pleasing.

"I will go back to Somersetshire, ma'am," she said, "if you please. I do not think I am fit for London."

Mrs. Vane looked at her a moment, and then she laughed—a laugh that had more of pain in it than of mirth. "You are very like your mother," she said. "She was always for going back to Somersetshire. Well, go, my dear; and when you are married to your curate, you may tell your children that your aunt Vane was a very fine lady, and that she taught you what London was worth. It is a good lesson, and cheap at the price."

So Harriet went back to Somersetshire, with a lighter purse and a heavier heart; and if she had learned nothing else in Upper Brook Street, she had learned that the friendship of fine ladies is a very expensive thing, and that a woman who has no character of her own to give her niece may yet give her a very useful warning.
