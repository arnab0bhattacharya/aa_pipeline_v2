The lecture hall in Cartwright Street, with its high windows letting in the thin November light, was almost empty; dust motes swirled in the slanting rays, and the speaker, a pale man with a beard, was saying something about the future of the empire, something about duty and the continuity of institutions, which fell upon the rows of empty-seeming faces like rain on stone, and was absorbed, or reflected, or simply dissolved into the general hum of the ventilation pipes and the distant clatter of a horse and cart on the cobblestones outside. Giles Hambro sat near the back, his long legs stretched out, his hat on his knees, and thought not of the empire but of the word "continuity"—how it reminded him of a river, and how a river reminded him of his mother washing her hands in the basin at home, the water slipping through her fingers, and how she had died in the spring, and how the daffodils had come up anyway, indifferent, in the garden bed by the window, and how that was continuity too, but not the sort the lecturer meant.

It was at the moment when the lecturer paused and cleared his throat, and the audience shifted, and a woman two seats to the left of Giles turned her head and said, quite audibly, as if she were not in a lecture hall at all but in a kitchen arguing with someone over the washing up, "But that is perfectly absurd," that Giles first saw Phyllis Beckwith.

She had dark hair drawn back loosely, and a face that was not beautiful but alert, with the kind of alertness that is almost aggressive, like a bird that has found a worm and is daring another bird to take it. She was wearing a green coat, slightly worn at the cuffs, and she held a pamphlet in her lap which she had been folding and unfolding along the same crease, so that it was almost cut in two. She worked, he later learned, for the East London Committee for Distressed Families, which meant that she spent her days in rooms where the wallpaper hung in strips and the smell of boiled cabbage soaked into the very plaster, and she had come to the lecture not because she cared about the future of the empire but because her employer, Mr. Gribble, a man with a wen on his neck and a passion for self-improvement, had given her the ticket and told her to broaden her mind, which she had done, apparently, by becoming furious.

"Perfectly absurd," she had said, and then, feeling perhaps Giles's glance upon her, she turned and looked at him directly, without coyness, without any of that soft preliminary fluttering that women were supposed to perform, and which Giles, in his twenty-six years of life, had seen perhaps twice and had found each time unspeakably depressing. She looked at him as if he were a lamppost she had walked into and she was assessing the damage.

"Did you think so?" Giles said, in the interval, when the lecturer had retired behind a curtain and people were standing and putting on their coats, and the light outside had shifted from thin grey to thick grey, which meant it would probably rain.

"I think all talk of continuity is a way of avoiding the present," she said. "People talk about the future of the empire because they cannot bear to look at what the empire is doing now, this afternoon, in rooms in Limehouse where children have no boots."

"But surely—" Giles began, and then stopped, because she had already turned away, as if the conversation were finished, a thing completed, like a letter posted. But it was not finished. He followed her to the door and said, rather desperately, as if the words were being pulled out of him by a string attached to his sternum, "But poetry is also a way of looking at the present. It is not only evasion."

She paused in the doorway. The rain had begun, spitting lightly on the steps. She considered him. She considered the rain. She said, "What poetry?"

And that was how it began.

They met on Thursdays, first at the teashop in Marchmont Lane where the buns were stale but the urn hissed companionably, and then, when the teashop closed for refurbishment in January, at the bench under the plane tree in Cartwright Gardens, regardless of weather, which was a kind of madness, but then Giles had always felt that the chief characteristic of life was its madness, its determination to go on being itself, damp bench and all, when any rational person would have given up and lain down. He came from the office, where he spent his hours copying figures into ledgers—figures which represented, he supposed, something real, crates of tea, bales of cotton, the labour of men in distant places—but which seemed to him, as he wrote them, to have no more substance than the letters in a poem, and rather less beauty. He came with ink on his cuffs and his mind full of lines that would not settle, phrases that hovered like moths at the edge of his thought, and he would find Phyllis already there, reading, or eating an apple, or simply sitting with her hands in her coat pockets, staring at the bare branches of the plane tree as if they had personally offended her.

They argued about everything. They argued about whether one could write poetry about poverty, or whether poverty was precisely the thing that destroyed the capacity for poetry. They argued about the war, which was coming, which was already in the air like a low hum beneath the conversation. They argued about Keats, whom Giles loved and Phyllis found insufferably self-absorbed. "He died of his own sensibility," she said once, biting into an apple with such force that the juice ran down her chin, and Giles, watching her, thought: she is the most alive person I have ever seen, and the thought alarmed him so much that he said nothing for several minutes, which she took, characteristically, as concession.

But it was not concession. It was that something in him had turned, or opened, like a door in a wall he had always assumed to be solid, and through it came a flood of something—feeling, he supposed, though the word was inadequate—feeling for the way she held her head, slightly tilted, as if listening to a sound no one else could hear; for the way her gloves were mismatched, one black, one brown, because she had lost a glove and not replaced it, not from poverty exactly but from a kind of defiant indifference to appearances that struck Giles as heroic, almost, in a world where everyone was so careful; for the way she said "absurd" and "intolerable" and "ridiculous" with such conviction, as if these words were not opinions but facts, as solid as the bench they sat on, as the plane tree, as the rain that fell now in earnest and soaked them both so that they sat with water running down their faces like tears, though neither of them was crying.

"You are very stubborn," she said to him once, in February, when the wind was so cold that the words seemed to freeze in the air between them.

"You are very certain," he replied.

"Someone must be certain. Otherwise we should all sit about writing poetry while the world—" she gestured vaguely at the square, at the houses, at the distant spire of a church, at the whole visible and invisible structure of London, which seemed, in that gesture, to be trembling slightly, though whether from cold or from some deeper instability, he could not tell.

But he was not listening to the words. He was thinking that her hand, red with cold, held out against the grey sky, was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen, and that he would, if he could, have written a poem about it, but that the poem would have been, as she said, an evasion, a way of turning the thing into something else, something safe and shaped and distant, when what he wanted was the thing itself, the hand, the cold, the red, the sky, the woman who was, at this moment, looking at him with an expression that was not quite anger and not quite anything else either, but something new, something he had no word for, something that perhaps there was no word for, and that this—the absence of the word, the space where the word would be—was, after all, where poetry began, and also where it failed, and that this failing was, perhaps, what love was: the insistence on the particular, the stubborn, the untranslatable, this hand, this sky, this bench, this Thursday, this woman saying something he could not quite hear, now, over the wind, over the rattle of a cart, over the whole vast noise of London in February, saying something that was, he thought, though he could not be sure, the thing he most wanted to hear.
