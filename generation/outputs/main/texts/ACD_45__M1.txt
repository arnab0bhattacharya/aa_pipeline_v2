The room at 14, Pembroke Terrace, was furnished with that respectable austerity which marks the lodging-house of the better sort. The air, thick with the fog of a Southampton November, was sharp with the smell of wet wool and the faint, lingering scent of ship’s tar that clung to my person. My visitor, Mr. Percival Croft, stood with his back to the small coal fire, his hands—strong, capable hands, now strangely unsteady—clasped behind him. He was a second officer of the Blue Star Line, and his face, tanned to a leathery brown by southern suns, was pale beneath the tan.

“It is a simple matter, and yet it is not,” he began, his voice low and precise. “I have been robbed, Mr. Blackwood. My lodgings were turned over last night while I was at the docks, seeing to my ship’s stores. But it is the cause of the robbery that has brought me to you. It is a thing I do not understand.”

I gestured for him to be seated in the one armchair. “Pray, tell me the whole story from the beginning. The smallest detail may be of the greatest consequence.”

He nodded, appreciating the point. “We were homeward bound from Callao. Among the passengers was a gentleman, a Mr. Alistair Finch, who was dying of a fever. He was an Englishman, but had been many years in Peru. He kept to his cabin, and saw no one but the ship’s surgeon and myself. Three days from land, he sent for me. He was propped on his pillows, a mere wraith, but his eyes were as bright and sharp as a hawk’s. He had this.” Croft produced from his pocket a small, flat object wrapped in oilskin. It proved to be a letter, sealed with a wafer of black wax, upon which was impressed the image of a coiled serpent. The address was written in a fine, sloping hand: ‘For the immediate and private attention of Mr. Julian Thorne, Ashworth House, Richmond, Surrey.’

“He made me swear a solemn oath,” Croft continued, his gaze fixed on the fire, “to deliver this into Mr. Thorne’s own hand. ‘It is a matter of family, and of life and death,’ said he. ‘You are a man of your word, I see it in your face. Do not fail me.’ He gave me five sovereigns for my trouble. It was a great sum, and I felt the weight of the trust upon me. I promised him I would do as he asked.”

“And you did so?”

“Upon my word of honour. I took a train to Richmond yesterday afternoon. Ashworth House is a fine place, set back from the road behind a high wall. I was shown into a drawing-room where the family was assembled. There was Mr. Thorne, a tall, stern-looking man of perhaps fifty, with iron-grey hair and a face like a carved mask. His wife, a nervous, fluttering sort of woman, sat by the tea-table. And there was their daughter, Miss Clara Thorne, a handsome girl with her father’s dark eyes. I introduced myself and explained my errance, presenting the letter as Mr. Finch had directed.”

Croft paused, and a look of profound unease crossed his features. “Mr. Thorne took the letter. He looked at the seal, and I saw his jaw tighten. He did not break it at once. He asked me several questions about Mr. Finch—how he had died, what were his last words, and so on. I answered him truthfully. Then, without a word to his family, he walked over to the fireplace, where a bright fire was burning, and dropped the letter upon the coals. The black wax melted in an instant. The paper curled, blackened, and was gone in a flash of flame. Mrs. Thorne gave a little cry, but her husband silenced her with a look. He turned back to me, his face quite impassive, and said, ‘Thank you, sir. You have discharged your duty. You will find my coachman at the door.’ He pressed a sovereign into my hand—a different sort of payment altogether—and that was the end of it. I was shown out, feeling more like a messenger who had delivered a bomb than a simple letter.”

“And that night your rooms were ransacked?”

“To the very floorboards. My sea-chest was broken open, my papers scattered, the mattress of my bed slit with a knife. They were searching for this, of course.” He gestured to the oilskin packet. “But they did not find it. I had taken it with me to the docks in my pocket. It seemed a foolish precaution at the time, but now I am glad of it.”

I rose and took the unopened letter. The paper was of a fine, foreign quality, and the serpent seal was distinctive. “You say Mr. Finch was from Peru?”

“For many years. He spoke of it as his adopted home.”

“And the name Alistair Finch meant nothing to the Thorne family?”

“Nothing. Mr. Thorne’s face did not change when I mentioned it. But when he saw the seal… I cannot describe it. It was as if a shutter had come down behind his eyes. A profound and deliberate coldness.”

This was the heart of it. A family secret, carried across an ocean by a dying man, and a reception so violent that the message itself was destroyed before it could be read. The ransacking of Croft’s rooms confirmed that the matter was not closed. The sender was dead, but his business lived on.

“You have acted with great discretion, Mr. Croft,” I said. “You have brought me a puzzle of the first order. The letter is now in my keeping. I shall make a few enquiries. It would be best if you were to say nothing of this to anyone.”

He agreed readily and took his leave. For an hour I sat in the gathering gloom, the letter on the table before me. To open it would be a breach of the trust placed in me by Croft, and by the dead man through him. Yet to leave it sealed was to leave the mystery locked away. My first duty was to understand the players in this little drama. I wrote a short note to my solicitor, a man with connections in the City, asking for any information he could gather on Mr. Julian Thorne of Ashworth House, and sent my boy off with it.

It was nearly ten o’clock when a sharp rap came at the door. I opened it to find Inspector Davies of the local constabulary, a man I knew slightly, standing on the step with a constable behind him. His expression was grim.

“Good evening, Mr. Blackwood,” said he, stepping inside without waiting for an invitation. “I am sorry to disturb you at this hour, but I have some grave news. Do you know a Mr. Percival Croft, a ship’s officer?”

My heart gave a sudden lurch. “He was in this very room some hours ago.”

The Inspector nodded slowly. “I thought as much. He was found in an alleyway not a hundred yards from here, less than an hour ago. His throat had been cut from ear to ear. We are making enquiries in the neighbourhood, and the landlady mentioned he had visited you.”

The fog seemed to press in against the windowpanes. The room, which had felt like a sanctuary, now seemed a place of peril. “Good God,” I murmured. “This is terrible.”

“It is,” Davies agreed, his eyes scanning my face. “A professional job, by the look of it. We found nothing on him save for his purse and papers. Did he say anything to you about his business? Did he leave anything in your care?”

I looked at the Inspector, a decent man bound by his duty to the law. Then I looked at the letter on my table. It was no longer a mere puzzle; it was the cause of a man’s death. To hand it over now would be to surrender it to the very forces that had sought it. Croft’s trust, and Finch’s, now rested with me alone.

“He came to me about a personal matter, Inspector,” I said, my voice steady. “A question of an inheritance from a distant relative in South America. He left nothing in my keeping. I am as shocked as you are.”

Davies held my gaze for a long moment, then gave a curt nod. “Very well, sir. If you think of anything more, you will find me at the station.” He turned and was gone, leaving me alone with the silent, ticking clock and the weight of a dead man’s secret. I picked up the letter. Whatever lay within, it was a thing of poison, and I knew then that I must see the business through to its end, not merely for the sake of the dead, but for my own.
