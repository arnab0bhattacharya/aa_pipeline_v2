It was in the autumn of 1833, when the tempestuous winds swept violently up the Bristol Channel, howling through the cramped and soot-stained streets of the city, as if they were the audible breath of a discontented and mourning nature, that the heavy curtain of my destiny was again drawn aside. For twenty years I had dwelt in a sort of voluntary solitude within the walls of my counting-house in Queen Square—a solitude not of the body, for the machinery of commerce daily brought me into contact with my fellow-men, but of the soul, which, having once committed an act of profound and unnatural darkness, shrinks ever afterwards from the light of human sympathy. The rain beat against the casement of my private room with a dismal, unceasing tide, and the flickering lamp upon my desk cast long, tremulous shadows upon the walls, resembling the phantoms of a guilt-laden conscience. I was immersed in a ledger, tracing the cold arithmetic of gains and losses, when a timid, yet distinct, knock broke the solemnity of my retreat.

I bade the visitor enter, and there stepped into the apartment a figure at once so frail and so deeply marked by the stings of adversity, that my heart, callous as it had become, suffered a sudden and violent pang. She was pale, exceedingly pale; her dark eyes, luminous with a melancholy fire, seemed the sole repositories of a life which the chill hand of poverty had already half extinguished. Her attire was of the plainest description, yet bore the impress of a stern and unyielding respectability. She stood before me, trembling like a leaf in the autumn blast, and, with a voice whose low, musical cadence belied the depth of the anguish it concealed, she stated her errand. She sought employment as my secretary; she was skilled in the arts of writing and computation, she said, and the heavy burden of existence, the dread of starvation, had driven her to seek bread by the labour of her hands. 

I listened, and as I listened, the present faded from my view, and the dark gulf of the past yawned beneath my feet. I gazed upon her features with an intensity that seemed to freeze the current of my blood, for in the majestic outline of her brow, in the sweet, yet sorrowful curve of her lips, and in the very expression of her eyes, I beheld the living, breathing image of Edward Valence. Valence! The name alone, echoing in the secret chambers of my memory, was sufficient to conjure up all the fiends of remorse. He had been my partner, the sharer of my schemes, the confidant of my fortunes; and when, in an hour of desperate and unexampled temptation, I myself had forged the accursed documents that drained the resources of our house, I had, with a cowardice unequalled in the annals of guilt, cast the whole weight of the crime upon his head. My testimony—it was false as the foul fiend’s own promise—had sent him to a living tomb. He had perished in prison, broken in spirit and ruined in fame, and I, the true author of his woes, had ascended upon the stepping-stone of his dead honour to a seat of unchallenged opulence. 

And now, behold the retribution! His daughter, unknowing of the serpent that coiled in the bosom of her father’s former friend, stood before me, supplicating for the crust that should sustain her life. "My father," she murmured, as if to plead her cause, "was once a merchant in this city; but misfortune overtook him, and he died far from those who should have cherished him. I am left alone, a solitary wanderer, to work or to perish."

She knew me not. Time, which had whitened my hair and ploughed furrows in my cheek, had mercifully veiled me from her recognition; or perhaps she had been too young, when the thunderbolt fell, to retain any distinct recollection of the destroyer of her house. I sat motionless, a statue of stone, while the tempest without raged with redoubled fury, and the rain lashed the panes as if the elements themselves wept for the tragedy that was acting within. 

"Your name?" I asked, with a voice which I strove to render calm, but which trembled despite my efforts, like a severed string upon a viol.

"Elinor Valence," she replied.

Valence! It was the very seal of my damnation. I felt as if an ice-cold hand were grasping my heart-strings, and pulling them down into the abyss of despair. Why did I not confess? Why did I not fall upon my knees, and, crying out, "I am the wretch who murdered thy father!" deliver myself up to the justice which her righteous indignation would demand? Alas, the weakness of humanity is such, that even in the deepest depths of remorse, it clings to the shadow of respectability. I dared not speak. The fear of her gaze, the dread of seeing the light of youth and hope turn to the fire of abhorrence in her eyes, bound my tongue. I was a coward still. 

"You shall be my secretary," I said, and the words seemed to be uttered by another, so hollow and remote did they sound. "You shall find in me a protector, if you will accept the task I assign you."

She thanked me with a burst of eloquent gratitude, which cut me to the very soul. She spoke of me as a benefactor, a preserver, a being sent by Providence to rescue her from despair. Oh, the irony of fate! To be worshipped as an angel of light by the child of the man whose soul I had immolated upon the altar of my own selfishness! I gave her the pen and the desk, and she sat down, in the very chair where Valence himself had often sat in the days of our early, untainted friendship, to write the letters I dictated. 

As she wrote, I watched her. The graceful turn of her wrist, the droop of her eyelashes, the soft, regular breathing of her pure and innocent bosom—all were his. She was Valence risen from the grave, softened into womanhood, come to haunt me, not with spectres and chains, but with the irresistible eloquence of suffering and virtue. The wind without roared in the chimney, and the flames leaped up the flue, casting a ruddy glow upon her pale, thoughtful face. I thought of him, pining in the damp and noisome cell, far from the light of the sun and the voices of his kind; I thought of the slow fever that consumed him, and the last groan which had passed his lips, unheeded by the world, but registered in the book of Heaven. And I, who had wrought this ruin, was here, in a warm and luxurious room, dispensing alms to his orphan child! 

The torture was exquisite, yet I would not have exchanged it for oblivion. It was a punishment which kept my sin ever present to my mind, a hair-shirt that reminded me of my guilt. I had purchased ease at the price of my soul, and now the bill was presented to me. Elinor wrote on, unconscious of the agony she was inflicting, and I sat, a self-condemned prisoner, in the hell of my own thoughts. 

When the evening was far spent, and she had finished her task, she rose to depart. "I will return to-morrow," she said, with a smile of such touching sweetness that it seemed to pierce me like a sword. "May Heaven bless you, sir, for your goodness to a friendless girl."

"May Heaven forgive me!" I murmured, as the door closed behind her, and I was left alone with the night and my despair. The storm had subsided, but the darkness was profound. I threw myself upon my chair, and, burying my face in my hands, wept tears of such bitter and unavailing sorrow as I had not wept for twenty years. I had thought that time would blunt the edge of remorse, but I now knew that it only sharpens it, and that the ghost of a murdered reputation will rise at last, in the form of an innocent child, to demand a vengeance which no earthly power can avert.
