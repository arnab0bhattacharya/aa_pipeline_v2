Mr. Arthur Pendleton, of Whitfield Park in Hampshire, was a gentleman of such exact and methodical understanding, that he had never yet been surprised by an event he could not attribute to some failure of principle in the party responsible. He had arranged his dinner party with the precise calculation of a general arranging his ranks, and the evening was to proceed exactly as he had dictated. The company was select, the courses were elegant, and the conversation, he trusted, would be equally refined. His own consequence in the county was too well established to admit of any rival; and he looked forward to an evening of rational, if not deeply animated, intercourse. He had, however, a brother—a circumstance he could not alter, though he had often wished it otherwise.

This brother, Lieutenant Frederick Pendleton of the Royal Navy, was a young man of open countenance, easy manners, and a total disregard for those gradations of society which Mr. Pendleton held to be the very pillars of civilization. He had been absent for two years in the Mediterranean, and his family had received from him only such letters as were brief, cheerful, and uninformative. Mr. Pendleton, who valued regularity above affection, had ceased to expect his return, and had certainly not included him in the evening’s arrangements.

It was, therefore, with a sensation bordering upon dismay that Mr. Pendleton, while conducting one of his guests to the dining room, beheld a carriage—muddy, ill-appointed, and evidently hired from some inferior posting-house—draw up before the entrance. The door opened, and before any servant could intervene, a young man in a travelling coat, with a complexion tanned to a degree that Mr. Pendleton could only regard as indecorous, sprang out and crossed the threshold with the assurance of one who feels he has a right to be there.

“Arthur!” cried the lieutenant, with a warmth that his brother could not return. “I am come home at last, and not a day too soon, for I have had enough of salt water to last me a lifetime.”

Mr. Pendleton received this address with a bow so stiff that it might have served as a geometrical instrument. He was not a man to express surprise, but he could not wholly conceal it; and the slight elevation of his brows, as he regarded his brother’s disordered appearance, was the only token of that mortification which he already began to feel. He introduced the lieutenant to such guests as were within hearing, with the studied composure of a man who is determined that no domestic irregularity shall disturb the harmony of his table. The lieutenant bowed to each in turn, said something civil to every lady, and something hearty to every gentleman, and appeared wholly unconscious that his arrival was, in his brother’s view, a calamity.

At dinner, Mr. Pendleton had placed the lieutenant as far from the head of the table as propriety would allow; but this precaution availed him little, for Frederick’s voice, trained by years of commanding watches in gales, carried with an ease that rendered distance no obstacle. He addressed himself chiefly to his neighbour, Mrs. Bertram, a widow of mild disposition and considerable sensibility; but his remarks were delivered with such good humour that they soon attracted the attention of the whole table.

“I have been shipwrecked twice since I last saw you, Arthur,” said he, in a tone that might have befitted a man recounting a pleasant excursion. “The first time was off the coast of Portugal, in a storm so violent that we lost the mainmast and three of our best hands. We were two days in the boats, with nothing but biscuit and rainwater, before a Portuguese fishing vessel took us up. I confess I never was more sensible of the value of dry land than when I stood upon that slippery deck and saw our frigate go down.”

A silence followed this communication, of a kind that Mr. Pendleton found peculiarly distressing. It was not the silence of indifference, but of attention; and he perceived, with mingled indignation and alarm, that his guests were not merely tolerating the narrative but receiving it with visible interest. Mrs. Bertram pressed her handkerchief to her eyes; Miss Woodley, the eldest daughter of Sir Charles Woodley, leaned forward with parted lips; and even Mr. Mansfield, a gentleman of notoriously cold address, betrayed a degree of curiosity that was, in Mr. Pendleton’s judgement, quite beneath him.

“And the second time?” inquired Miss Woodley, with an earnestness that her father might have thought required some moderation.

“The second, Miss Woodley, was upon a reef in the Greek islands, in fair weather and broad daylight—a circumstance which I mention only to observe that a calm sea may be as dangerous as a rough one, when one is dealing with hidden rocks. We were fortunate in losing only the vessel and not the crew, though we lost most of our provisions and all of our patience. I assure you, I have never since regarded a chart without a degree of suspicion.”

This sally was received with a general laugh, in which Mr. Pendleton could not bring himself to join. He applied himself to his wine with an air of grave abstraction, and endeavoured, by the gravity of his countenance, to recall the company to that standard of decorum which he conceived to be appropriate to the occasion and the company. His efforts, however, were unavailing; for Frederick, having once begun, continued to entertain the table with such particulars of naval life as Mr. Pendleton considered wholly unfit for a dinner party. The lieutenant spoke of prize money with a frankness that shocked his brother’s sense of delicacy, and described a skirmish with a French privateer with a vivacity that was, in Mr. Pendleton’s opinion, dangerously close to enthusiasm.

Mr. Pendleton bore this with the outward composure of a man who has long practiced the art of concealing his emotions; but his inward suffering was considerable. He had designed the evening for the display of his own taste and the advancement of his own consequence, and he found himself supplanted by a younger brother whose claims to attention rested upon nothing more respectable than a series of adventures which Mr. Pendleton could not but regard as irregular and inconvenient.

The dessert had been placed upon the table, and Mr. Pendleton was beginning to entertain a hope that the worst was over, when the lieutenant, who had been engaged for some minutes in a private conversation with Miss Woodley, suddenly rose from his chair. The company fell into an expectant silence, and Mr. Pendleton, who had been in the act of dissecting a pear, laid down his knife and fork with a presentiment of evil.

“I believe,” said Frederick, with a colour heightened by feeling, but a voice steady and cheerful, “that I owe an apology to this company, and above all to Sir Charles Woodley, for taking such a liberty as I am about to take, at so unusual a time and place. But I am a sailor, and have never been famous for waiting upon ceremonies; and having loved Miss Woodley from the first hour I saw her, which was two years ago, before I sailed for the Mediterranean—and having reflected upon her, with unabated constancy, through shipwrecks, skirmishes, and other inconveniences—I cannot now refrain from asking her, before you all, whether she will do me the honour to be my wife.”

Miss Woodley, who had been pale a moment before, now became very red, and then very pale again; and after a silence which appeared to the company to be of considerable length, but which was, in reality, not more than a few seconds, she said, in a voice scarce audible, that she was very much obliged to him, and that—that she believed she might accept him.

Sir Charles Woodley, who had been wholly unprepared for this address, looked from the lieutenant to his daughter, and from his daughter to the lieutenant, with an expression of countenance that was neither wholly approving nor wholly the reverse. He was a gentleman of phlegmatic temperament, and not easily moved to either resentment or delight; and after a pause sufficient for the collection of his thoughts, he observed that the proposal was somewhat sudden, but that he had no objection to the lieutenant’s character, provided Miss Woodley was satisfied. Miss Woodley’s satisfaction being but too apparent in every feature of her face, the matter was considered, by the younger part of the company at least, as settled.

Mr. Pendleton, who had risen from his chair in the first moment of consternation, now sat down again. He felt that he had been defeated, though he could not precisely say by what means or in what engagement. His dinner party, so carefully planned, had been converted into a betrothal scene; his brother, whom he had wished to keep in the background, had become the hero of the evening; and the eldest daughter of Sir Charles Woodley, whom he had himself regarded with a degree of attention that he now perceived had been wholly insufficient, was lost to him for ever. He could not, however, relinquish the consolations of a correct mind; and as he surveyed the scene before him—the gratified lovers, the amused guests, the discomposed but not displeased father—he reflected that, whatever might be the immediate embarrassment of the occasion, it was at least better that his brother should marry Miss Woodley than that he should continue to be shipwrecked at intervals and arrive at dinner parties without warning.
