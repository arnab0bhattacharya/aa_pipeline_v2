In the gardens of the Palace of Silver Eglantine, where the peacocks trailed their trains of tarnished gold across lawns of emerald velvet, there lay a pool of water so still and so clear that it seemed a single flawless drop of jade, fallen from the sky and forgotten. It was bordered by marble, veined with the pale rosy flush of dawn, and set about with cypresses that stood like dark and slender mourners at a perpetual funeral of light. Here, on evenings when the great amber sun had sunk behind the world, the Lady Iseult of the Luminous Hands would come to walk, her gown a whisper of pale silk against the stone, and it was said that the very swans, envious of her whiteness, would hide their heads beneath their wings as she passed. But the true sovereign of that sequestered court was neither princess nor swan, but a single water lily, a thing of such exquisite and deliberate pallor that it appeared less a flower than a thought of whiteness, a dream of chastity conceived by the pool itself.

Its name was Nymphaea, and it was the most perfect of its kind. Its petals were like carved ivory, or the inner curve of a pearl, and in their heart there burned a small and secret flame of gold. By day it floated upon the surface, a queen upon a glassy throne, receiving the homage of the dragonflies and the hushed admiration of the garden. But Nymphaea cared nothing for the sun, that vulgar monarch of the day, who flaunted his power in the brazen sky and demanded the adoration of all things. Her soul, if a flower may be said to possess a soul, was a thing of twilight and of silence, and she reserved the whole of her passion for the moon.

Each evening, as the shadows lengthened and the world turned from gold to grey, a tremor of anticipation would pass through her slender stem. She would watch, with the patience of the truly devoted, for the first gleam of that silver wanderer to crest the dark rim of the cypresses. And when at last Selene, pale and remote and magnificently indifferent, would sail into the vast, violet sea of the heavens, Nymphaea would open her petals in a slow, voluptuous sigh of surrender. It was a rapture that bordered upon pain. She would turn her face upwards, baring her golden heart to the moonlight, and for those few enchanted hours she would imagine that the cold, chaste beam was a lover’s touch, a silver benediction that set her apart from every common bloom.

“She is a perfect fool,” croaked a voice from the bank, thick and complacent. It was Gorboduc, the eldest and most warty of the frogs, a creature of immense self-regard who considered the entire pool his personal republic. “A perfect and utter fool. Look at her, trembling for a face in the sky that she can never reach. It is indecent.”

“It is worse than indecent,” agreed his companion, a sleek and cynical frog named Caliban, who affected the manners of a philosopher. “It is unhygienic. All that sighing and swooning cannot be good for the constitution. A sensible flower would close her petals at a respectable hour and think of nothing more taxing than the next fly.”

From the deeper, cooler waters, a goldfish named Chrysostom, who was adorned in scales of opulent orange and who fancied himself a wit, swam to the surface. He blew a small, contemptuous bubble. “My dear Gorboduc, my dear Caliban,” he said, his voice a silvery gurgle, “you are too charitable. The creature is not merely a fool; she is a snob. She disdains the sun, which is the source of all life, all warmth, all colour—the very patron of our kind—for the sake of a cold, dead rock that has no light of its own but merely borrows it. It is the worst sort of affectation. She is a decadent. She worships a reflection and calls it a god.”

And so the days passed in this manner, the frogs with their pompous moralising, the goldfish with his brittle epigrams, and the lily with her silent, unshakeable devotion. She heard their mockery, but it seemed to her a noise from a world she had long since transcended. What were their petty concerns of flies and warmth compared to the sublime melancholy of her love? To be misunderstood, she had come to believe, was the first proof of a superior soul.

One night, however, the sky was not a clear violet sea but a troubled ocean of cloud, and the moon was hidden. Nymphaea waited, her petals half-open, a question poised on her lips. A profound and terrible anxiety seized her. Was Selene angry? Had she tired of her earthly worshipper? The other flowers, the roses and the lilies of the valley, slumbered in smug contentment, their heads bowed in the simple, honest sleep of those who love the sun. But Nymphaea kept her vigil, and the darkness felt like a personal affront.

When at last the moon did appear, it was not the perfect, silver shield of other nights, but a thin, wasted crescent, a ghost of its former self, and it seemed to Nymphaea that it wore an expression of profound weariness. A cold dread, colder than any night wind, crept into the lily’s heart. She saw, for the first time, not a lover, but a celestial body following an immutable and indifferent law. Its light was not a caress; it was a physical fact. Its distance was not a mystery to be bridged by devotion; it was an absolute and eternal void. The frogs were right. The goldfish was right. She was a fool.

A great shudder passed through her. Her beauty, which had seemed a sacred offering, now felt like a grotesque and elaborate jest. Her devotion, which had been the very core of her being, was revealed as a sublime and ridiculous self-deception. In that moment of cruel clarity, she understood the terrible loneliness of the beautiful, who mistake the admiration of the world for a meeting of souls.

The next evening, the Lady Iseult came to the pool’s edge. She was in a pensive mood, and her gaze fell upon the water lily, which was now closed tight, a pale, clenched fist of a flower. Its petals were no longer the colour of ivory, but of a sickly, faded parchment, and it had lost the will to hold itself above the water. It was a picture of exquisite and heartbroken decay.

“How strange,” murmured the Lady Iseult to her attendant. “The lily is dying. And yet, look how beautiful it is in its sorrow. It has a pathos, a distinction, that it never possessed in its prime. Its vanity has been turned to tragedy. It is a far more interesting flower now than it ever was before. One might almost say it has become an artist’s model for melancholy itself.”

And so the lily Nymphaea faded, and the frogs, who are the natural conservatives of any pond, declared it a great victory for common sense. But the Lady Iseult, who was a poet in her own right, had the dead flower gathered and pressed between the pages of a volume of verses by a minor and forgotten romantic, and she would sometimes look at it and smile, for she knew that to have loved the moon, even foolishly, and to have been broken by its beauty, was a far more splendid fate than to have been merely a successful flower.
