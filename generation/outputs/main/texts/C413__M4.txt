The rain had not deigned to stop for his arrival, nor for the hansom cab that rattled him through the familiar grey streets, nor for the moment he stood on the doorstep of the house in Bloomsbury, water dripping from the brim of his hat. Three years. Three years of sun and dust and the strange, flat light of the Antipodes, and London received him back with the same damp indifference it had shown him when he left. He had imagined this moment a thousand times—the door swinging open, her cry of joy, the scent of her hair. He had not imagined the footman, a new one, who regarded him with the blank suspicion reserved for damp strangers.

Mr. Alistair Finch, he said. To see Miss Cora Bellamy.

The footman’s expression did not change, but something flickered in his eyes. He was bid to wait in the hall. The house felt different. The wallpaper in the hall was new, a bold, swirling pattern of peacock blue that Cora would have called vulgar. He stood, hat in hand, watching the rain trace paths down the windowpane, and a coldness that had nothing to do with the weather began to settle in his bones.

It was not Cora who came to him, but a man. He emerged from the drawing room, tall and fair, his waistcoat straining slightly at the buttons. It took Alistair a full five seconds to recognise him, and when he did, the floor seemed to tilt.

“Julian?” he said. The name felt foreign on his tongue.

Julian Ashworth, his oldest friend, the man who had stood beside him at his father’s graveside, stopped short. His face, which had been arranged in a polite, enquiring smile, went through a rapid, ugly series of contortions—shock, disbelief, and then a sickly, guilty pallor.

“Alistair,” Julian breathed. “Good God. Alistair.”

The drawing room door opened again, and there was Cora. She was wearing a gown of deep burgundy, a colour she had once told him she detested. Her hair was piled high, and there were pearls at her throat. She was beautiful, and she was looking at him as if he were a ghost.

“Alistair,” she whispered, and the name was a small, broken thing. Her hand flew to her chest, not in joy, but in alarm.

The silence that followed was a thick, suffocating thing. It was broken by a small, fretful cry from the hallway behind her. A nursemaid appeared, a plump, aproned woman, dandling a red-faced infant on her hip.

“He’s teething, ma’am,” the nursemaid said, oblivious to the tableau in the hall. “Shall I take him up?”

Cora’s eyes, wide and fixed on Alistair, did not move. “Yes,” she managed, her voice thin. “Yes, take him.”

The pieces clicked into place with a dreadful, silent finality. The new wallpaper. The pearls. Julian’s waistcoat. The child.

“I see,” Alistair said, and his own voice sounded strange to him, flat and calm, as if he were discussing the weather. He looked from Cora to Julian, who had the grace to look utterly miserable. “I seem to have arrived at an inopportune moment.”

“Alistair, we thought you were dead,” Cora burst out, the words tumbling over one another. She took a step forward, her hands outstretched. “The ship. The *Aurora*. It went down off the Cape. We had a telegram. We had a service. We mourned you.”

He remembered the *Aurora*. A rusting tub, unfit for the coastal trade, let alone a voyage to the colonies. He had laughed at the agent who tried to book him on it, had waited a fortnight for a berth on a proper steamer. He had written to her, of course. Dozens of letters. He had told her of the delay, of the new ship, of his love.

“My letters,” he said. “I wrote to you every month.”

“We received none,” Julian said, finding his voice at last. It was firm, defensive. “Not a single one. The wreck was reported in all the papers. It seemed… it seemed conclusive.”

Alistair looked at Cora. Her eyes were filling with tears, but they were not the tears of a grief-stricken widow miraculously reunited with her love. They were the tears of a woman caught in an awkward situation, a social inconvenience of the highest order. He saw it in the way she clutched Julian’s arm, a gesture not of shock, but of possession. He saw it in the way she avoided his gaze.

He could believe them. He could believe the lost letters, the tragic error, the cruel hand of fate. It was a story worthy of a penny dreadful. It was a story that required him to be noble, to bow out, to wish them well.

Or, he could believe the other story. The one that was forming in his mind, clear and cold as ice. Three years is a long time. A woman of Cora’s beauty and social ambition would not remain a widow for long. The grief would fade. The inconvenience of a lost fiancé would be replaced by the comfort of a living husband. Julian, his best friend, always there to offer a shoulder to cry on, a strong arm to lean upon. How convenient. How very, very convenient.

He looked at them, standing together, a picture of domesticity. Julian, solid and prosperous, heir to his father’s shipping fortune. Cora, the beautiful wife, the mother of his child. They were a perfect fit.

“I see,” he said again. The rain hammered against the window. He felt a profound, bone-deep weariness. The long voyage, the weeks of anticipation, the hope that had buoyed him up—it all drained away, leaving a hollow shell.

He looked at Cora. “You believed me dead,” he said, and his tone was not accusatory, but thoughtful. He turned his gaze to Julian. “And you, Julian? You believed it too?”

Julian’s jaw tightened. “Of course. We both did. It was… it was a terrible time.”

Alistair nodded slowly. He saw the flicker of relief in Cora’s eyes, the hope that he would simply accept their story and go. But he saw something else, too. A certain tightness around her mouth, a flicker of something that looked horribly like boredom. He remembered her restless energy, her constant need for amusement, her fear of being left behind while life went on elsewhere.

He could see it so clearly. The first months of grief, genuine enough. Then the slow, creeping tedium. The quiet dinners with Julian, who was always there. The realisation that the world had not stopped, and that she was young, and that a living man was infinitely more satisfactory than a dead hero.

He considered both possibilities. The tragic accident, the cruel twist of fate. Or the simple, pragmatic decision of a woman who had grown tired of waiting. He weighed them in his mind, and found, to his surprise, that he could not tell the difference. They were two sides of the same coin, and the coin was worthless.

“You know,” he said, and a strange, wry smile touched his lips. It did not reach his eyes. “I find I cannot decide which explanation I prefer.”

Cora blinked. “Alistair?”

“The one where you believed me dead,” he continued, as if she had not spoken. “That is flattering, in its way. It suggests a love so profound that my supposed death was the only thing that could sever it. A grand, tragic love.”

He paused, his gaze shifting to Julian. “And the other explanation. The one where you simply grew bored.” He let the word hang in the air, watching them both flinch. “That is also flattering. It suggests I was not worth waiting for. That my memory was a trifle, easily set aside for a more convenient arrangement. It removes any burden of guilt from your shoulders, you see. It makes you merely human.”

He picked up his hat from the hall table. The gesture was final.

“I believe I shall not trouble you for an answer,” he said. “The truth, I suspect, is a mixture of the two, and neither version is particularly becoming. You have your life. I shall find my own.”

He did not look back as he opened the door. The rain had stopped, and the air was sharp and clean. He stepped out onto the pavement, a free man, leaving the two of them standing in the hall of their peacock-blue house, with their pearls and their child and their convenient, comfortable, and utterly flattering lies.
