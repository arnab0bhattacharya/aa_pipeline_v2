The command of Queen Elara arrived not with the clatter of horse-hooves or the rustle of silk, but with the silence of a snowflake landing on a blackthorn bush. It was carried by a falcon, a bird of such negligible size that one might have mistaken it for a thought too large for the mind. The message was brief, and carried with it the heavy, perfumed scent of the court: *Weave for me a robe that shall make me the most beloved queen in the world. You have one year. The penalty for failure is not death, which is too crude for such an offense, but the loss of your sight, which is merely a loss of perspective.*

Elara’s envoy, a man whose smile was as sharp as a paring knife, looked at the cottage at the edge of the Whispering Woods with an expression of mild, aesthetic disgust. It was a place of mud and thatch, of soot-stained hands and eyes that had never seen the polished marble of the palace. He did not bother to look at the girl, Mirin, who sat by the loom, her fingers moving with the rhythmic precision of a metronome. To him, she was merely a tool, a bit of machinery set in motion by the caprice of a monarch.

‘The queen is impatient,’ he said, adjusting his cuffs. ‘Impatience is the only virtue that remains to the powerful. The rest have been worn away by the friction of ruling.’

Mirin did not look up. ‘And what is the color, Your Lordship? Gold, to match her crown? Or crimson, to match her heart?’

The envoy blinked. He had expected gratitude, or perhaps terror. He had not expected a question about color. ‘The color is unimportant,’ he sniffed. ‘It is the effect that matters. She wishes to be loved. Surely that is a simple enough request for a weaver. Love is a fabric, is it not? And you are paid to work with it.’

He left, taking with him the silence of the forest, which seemed to grow louder in his absence. The falcon, having delivered its burden, flew off to the south, where the air was warmer and the politics were less brittle.

For the first month, Mirin wove in white. She wove the linen of the dawn, the flax of the river, the cotton of the cloud. It was a beautiful robe, light as a sigh and strong as a promise. But as she worked, she saw in the white threads the blinding glare of the palace, the way the light there struck the faces of the courtiers and turned their eyes into blank mirrors. White was the color of indifference, she realized. It reflected everything and absorbed nothing. It was the color of the mask the queen wore when she spoke of charity, and the color of the wall she built between herself and the people.

So she tore it down.

For the second month, she wove in blue. She wove the indigo of the night, the azure of the sky, the deep, melancholy blue of the sea. It was a robe of depth and mystery, of secrets kept and mysteries solved. But as she wove, she heard the echo of the queen’s laughter, a sound that was too light, too detached, like the tapping of a fingernail on a crystal glass. Blue was the color of distance. It was the color of the queen’s gaze, which always looked through people rather than at them. It was the color of the chasm that separated the throne from the floor.

She tore that down, too.

For the third month, she wove in gold. She wove the saffron of the sunset, the amber of the honey, the bright, dazzling yellow of the sun. It was a robe of opulence, of wealth, of the kind of beauty that burns the eye. But as the threads interlaced, she saw the queen’s hands, stained with the dirt of power, and the way the gold hung on her, not as a garment, but as a cage. Gold was the color of greed. It was the color of the queen’s desire, which was insatiable, which wanted to be loved not for who she was, but for what she was. It was the color of the price tag attached to every smile.

She tore that down, as well.

The year wore on. The seasons changed. The leaves turned from green to red to brown, and the forest shed its skin, revealing the stark, beautiful bones of the trees. Mirin wove and unwove, wove and unwove. She had learned that love was not a thing that could be commanded, any more than the wind could be caught in a net. Love was not a color. It was not a texture. It was not a substance that could be spun from flax or silk.

And so, for the last month, she wove in grey.

It was not the grey of ash, nor the grey of dust. It was the grey of the mist that rises from the river at dawn. It was the grey of the stone that holds the bridge together. It was the grey of the sky before the storm, and the grey of the sky after the rain. It was the color of the world as it is, stripped of its varnish, its paint, its performance. It was the color of truth, which is always plain, and often unlovely.

On the day of the delivery, the envoy returned. He was thinner now, and his smile had lost its edge. He looked at the cottage, and for the first time, he saw it. He saw the mud, and the thatch, and the soot, and the girl. He saw that she was not a tool, but a person. He saw that she was alone, and that her aloneness was a kind of strength.

‘It is done,’ Mirin said. She held up the robe. It was a simple thing, of a single, unbroken thread of grey. It had no pattern, no embroidery, no jewels. It was just a robe.

The envoy took it. He held it up to the light. It was soft, and it was strong. It was the color of the earth.

‘This is it?’ he asked. ‘This is the robe that will make her the most beloved queen in the world?’

‘Yes,’ said Mirin. ‘It is the only color that does not lie.’

The envoy looked at her, and for a moment, he forgot to be an envoy. He forgot the palace, and the queen, and the falcon. He looked at her, and he saw her. He saw the weariness in her eyes, and the peace in her hands. He saw the truth of her, which was plain, and often unlovely, but real.

‘I do not know what to do with this,’ he said. ‘It is not what the queen wanted. It is not what she expected. It is not what she deserves.’

‘No,’ said Mirin. ‘It is what she needs.’

The envoy left the robe on the table. He did not take it. He walked out into the forest, and he did not look back. He walked until he was far from the cottage, far from the woods, far from the weight of the crown. He walked until he reached the river, and he stood on the bank, and he watched the mist rise.

The queen received the robe the next day. She held it up to the light. She saw the grey. She saw the plainness. She saw the truth.

She did not weep. She did not rage. She did not order the weaver to be blinded. She simply hung the robe on a hook in her chamber. And for the first time in her life, she looked in the mirror, and she saw herself.

She saw a woman who was not loved, but who was alone. She saw a woman who was not powerful, but who was free. She saw a woman who was not a queen, but who was a person.

And she smiled. It was a small smile, and a sad smile, and a true smile. It was the only smile that mattered.

The forest breathed. The mist rose. The grey robe hung in the silence, and it was the most beloved thing in the world, because it was the only thing that was real.
