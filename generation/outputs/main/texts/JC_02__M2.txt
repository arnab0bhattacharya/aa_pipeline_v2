The heat in the valley did not merely exist; it was a physical substance, a thick, amber viscosity that clung to the skin and seeped into the bones. Arthur Penrose sat in the shadow of the makeshift surveyor’s tent, watching the dust motes dance in the single shaft of light that pierced the canvas. It was a light of terrible clarity, illuminating nothing but the immediate, dusty foreground, while the rest of the mountain range remained a silhouette of absolute, impenetrable darkness against the dying sky. He held his theodolite not in his hands, but in his mind, a cold, hard object that had lost all its precision in this humidity. The line he had drawn on the map three days ago, a thin black stroke cutting through the white emptiness of the Andes, now seemed less like a path and more like a scar, a violent incision into the flesh of the earth.

He remembered the words of Minister Valdes, spoken in the suffocating gloom of the government office in the capital, where the air was stale with cigar smoke and the weight of officialdom. Valdes had spoken of "progress," of "the iron spine of the nation," and of "civilisation" as if it were a gift to be bestowed upon the indigenous population, who, in his view, were merely obstacles to be smoothed away by the steam of the locomotive. Penrose had nodded, feeling the hollowness of his own assent. He was a man of measurements and angles, not of politics, yet here he was, a tool in the hands of a government that held no more power over the mountains than a whisper holds over a storm.

The rebel general, a man named Soriano, was a different creature. He had appeared two days ago, not with an army, but with a handful of gaunt, sunburned men who moved through the scrub with the silent grace of predators. Soriano himself was a lean figure, his face a map of deep lines carved by wind and hunger. He did not speak of civilisation. He spoke of "sovereignty," of "the blood of the ancestors," and of the railway as a chain, a golden noose that would tighten around the neck of the people until they strangled. Yet, when he looked at the surveyor’s equipment, there was a strange, almost reverent hunger in his eyes. He wanted the line. He wanted to control it. He wanted to be the gatekeeper, the one who decided who could pass and who would be left behind in the dust.

Penrose’s mind drifted back to the night before, when he had tried to sleep. The silence of the camp was not empty; it was filled with a low, thrumming vibration, the sound of the earth itself shifting, or perhaps the sound of his own blood rushing in his ears. He had felt a presence at the edge of the tent, a cold weight that pressed against the canvas. He had not dared to look out. In the darkness, the distinction between the enemy and the friend, between the state and the rebel, had dissolved. They were both hungry. They both wanted the line. And he, the engineer, was the only one who knew how to draw it.

A sound broke the stillness. It was the clatter of boots on the gravel, heavy and deliberate. Penrose did not look up immediately. He knew the gait. It was Valdes’s aide, a young man named Ruiz, who had the habit of walking as if he were treading on eggs, yet with the force of a man determined to crush them.

"Señor Penrose," Ruiz said, his voice thin and reedy in the twilight. "The Minister sends his regards. He asks if the route is settled."

Penrose finally raised his eyes. The light had faded further, and the valley was becoming a pool of indigo shadow. "The route is difficult, Ruiz," Penrose said, his voice rough from disuse. "The terrain is unforgiving. There are no shortcuts."

"The Minister does not care for shortcuts," Ruiz replied, standing rigid in the last of the light. "He cares for the deadline. The train must run by the end of the month. It is a matter of national pride."

Penrose looked at the young man and saw not a soldier, but a victim. He was caught in the same trap, bound by duty and fear, just as Penrose was bound by his contract and his pride. "And General Soriano?" Penrose asked, the question slipping out before he could stop it.

Ruiz flinched, a subtle twitch of the jaw. "General Soriano is... a concern. He has spoken to some of the workers. He says the railway will bring death. He says we are digging graves."

"Everyone says that," Penrose murmured. "The government says it brings life. The rebels say it brings death. Perhaps it is both. Perhaps it is simply a line, and what happens on it is up to those who walk it."

Ruiz stared at him, his eyes wide with a mixture of confusion and fear. "You are strange, Señor Penrose. You speak like a philosopher, but you work like a machine. Do not forget your duty. The Minister expects the final survey by dawn."

When Ruiz was gone, Penrose was left alone with the darkening valley. The heat had broken, replaced by a chill that crept up from the river below. He looked at his theodolite, lying on the table, its glass lenses dark and lifeless. He thought of the small republic, this fragile patch of land on the map, struggling to assert its existence against the vast, indifferent forces of geography and history. The railway was not just a line of iron and steel; it was a declaration, a scream into the void, a demand to be heard, to be seen, to be part of the modern world.

But who would be heard? The Minister, with his promises of prosperity, or the General, with his warnings of destruction? Or was it Penrose, the invisible man, the one who drew the line but would never ride the train? He felt a profound sense of isolation, a moral vertigo that had nothing to do with the height of the mountains. He was suspended between two powers, both claiming to represent the future, both willing to crush him to get their way.

He picked up his pencil and looked at the map again. The line was there, a thin, confident stroke. He could erase it. He could burn the map. He could walk away into the mountains and let the republic remain a collection of isolated villages, cut off from the world. But he knew he would not. He was an engineer, and he had a job to do. The line would be drawn, the tunnels would be bored, and the rails would be laid. The train would come, bringing with it the noise and the smoke and the inevitable changes that could not be stopped. Whether it would bring salvation or ruin, he did not know. He only knew that the line had to be straight, and that he had to be the one to ensure it.

The darkness closed in around the tent, complete and total. Penrose sat in the blackness, listening to the silence, feeling the weight of the decision pressing down on him, heavier than the heat, colder than the night. He was a man of angles, and the only angle he had left was the one that pointed forward, into the unknown, where the light had not yet reached.
