I must write down these events, even though the act of remembrance is a wound that bleeds anew with every syllable. It is a duty I owe to the living and the dead, and to my own soul, which groans beneath the weight of a sorrow too vast to be borne in silence. We had come to Italy, my father, my dear sister Agnes, and myself, seeking the warmth of a southern sun to banish the miasma of our English grief, that melancholy which had clung to our house since my mother’s passing. But in that land of ancient ruins and passionate hearts, we found a sorrow of a different, more terrible kind.

Milan, in the year of our Lord eighteen hundred and twenty, was a city of beautiful contradictions. The sun blazed upon its marble palaces and gilded domes, yet a shadow lay upon the spirit of its people. It was a shadow cast by the Austrian eagle, whose wings seemed to darken every piazza and whose presence was a constant, silent affront to the proud Italian soul. My father, a man of sober English principle and unshakeable order, saw only the outward splendour. He admired the operas and the paintings, but he was blind to the fire that smouldered in the eyes of the young men who gathered in the cafés, their voices hushed and urgent. To him, such politics were the feverish fancies of a volatile race, best left undisturbed.

It was in the hallowed quiet of the Ambrosian Library that I first saw him. I was examining a manuscript, my fingers tracing the delicate script of a forgotten age, when a voice, rich and low, spoke beside me. “It is a beauty that has survived the cruelty of centuries, is it not, signorina?” I looked up into a face that seemed a living embodiment of the Italian spirit: dark, expressive eyes that held both an infinite tenderness and a fierce, unquenchable fire, and a mouth whose smile was both gentle and proud. He was Signor Lorenzo Vittori, a scholar, he said, with a passion for the histories of his country. From that hour, a new world opened before me. Under the pretence of scholarly discourse, he led me through the city’s hidden heart. He spoke not of popes and emperors, but of the soul of Italy—of Dante’s exile, of the struggles of the Lombard communes, of a dream of a united, free nation, a dream for which, he confessed with a burning intensity that thrilled and terrified me, he and many others were prepared to sacrifice everything. His love for his country was a sacred flame, and in its light, I felt my own quiet, sheltered existence to be a pale and shadowy thing.

Our clandestine meetings could not long escape my father’s notice. He saw the change in me—the heightened colour in my cheeks, the new light in my eyes, the distracted air with which I performed my accustomed duties. One evening, as a storm broke over the city, its thunder echoing the tumult in my heart, he summoned me to his study. His face was grim. “Eleanor,” he began, his voice cold with a disapproval that struck me to the core, “I have observed your conduct with this Italian. It ends now.” I tried to speak, to explain that Lorenzo was a man of noble mind and virtuous character, but my words were as chaff before the wind of his wrath. “Virtue!” he exclaimed, striking the table with his fist. “He is a plotter, a conspirator! He speaks of liberty, but he means treason. He would draw you into his web of intrigue and ruin. I forbid you, in the most absolute terms, from seeing or communicating with him again. You are an English girl, and you will not forget the decency and security of your station.” He locked me in my chamber, and for days, I saw no one but Agnes, whose gentle tears were a poor balm for the agony that consumed me. I was a prisoner, and my heart was a cage of despair.

The news of Lorenzo’s arrest came not from a friend, but from the coarse laughter of soldiers in the street below our window. They had taken him in the night, along with a dozen others of his circle, accused of sedition against the Austrian Crown. The world, which had seemed so full of light and promise, collapsed into a single point of blackness. He was gone. He was in the hands of our oppressors, and I was powerless, a useless, caged bird. The days that followed were a waking nightmare. I moved through the rooms of our apartment like a ghost, the silence broken only by the distant roll of a drum or the clank of a soldier’s sabre, each sound a fresh torment. My father maintained a stony silence, believing the affair to be a harsh but necessary lesson.

Then, a note was slipped under my door, its paper coarse, its script hurried. It was from the wife of one of the arrested men. She begged, she implored, for me to use my influence. She wrote of a petition, of a plea for clemency that might be heard if it came from a foreigner, from an Englishwoman of respectable family. It was a fool’s hope, a desperate grasp at a straw in a hurricane. But it was a hope. To plead for him would be the ultimate act of defiance against my father. It would confirm all his fears, stain my reputation forever, and expose me to the very dangers he had so desperately sought to avoid. Yet, could I do otherwise? Could I sit idle, wrapped in the cowardice of safety, while the man I loved faced an unknown, and doubtless dreadful, fate? To remain silent was to betray him, to betray the very love that had awakened my soul from its long slumber. The choice was between my duty to my father and my duty to my heart; between the respectable world of my birth and the passionate, perilous world Lorenzo had shown me.

I have made my decision. This very night, while my father sleeps and the city is cloaked in an unnatural quiet, I will go. I will find the wife of the prisoner and add my voice, my name, my tears, to her plea. I know it is likely futile. I know the Austrian masters are not swayed by the lamentations of a girl. But it is an act I must perform. It is the only prayer left to me. Whatever may be the consequences—my father’s estrangement, the world’s censure, my own ruin—I must be able to face myself and the memory of Lorenzo. I must be able to say that I did not abandon him. I go now into the Milanese night, a night as dark and full of terror as my own soul, to perform this one, last, desperate act of love and faith. God grant me courage.
