It was said of Adrian Melmoth that he had set the drawing-rooms of London to music, and that London had forgiven him. His drawing-room ballads were indeed things of a fragile and deliberate beauty, constructed of silver sentiments and mother-of-pearl rhymes. They were sung by duchesses who possessed neither ear nor heart, but who possessed, to an extraordinary degree, the desire to be thought sensitive. To produce such ballads was considered by the fashionable a proof of a charming disposition; to be unable to sing them was deemed a social tragedy.

It was, therefore, with a shock of no mean proportions that Society received the announcement that Adrian Melmoth intended to write only serious music henceforth. He had grown tired of the sweet, the shallow, and the easily applauded. He desired, he said, to converse with the profound, to clothe the agonies of the soul in harmonies that the mere amateur would find unendurable. He had discovered, in a moment of terrible clarity, that popularity is the graveyard of art, and he wished to rescue his genius from the tomb.

His publisher, Mr. Sterling, a man who measured the merit of a composition by the weight of the cheques it inspired, called upon the composer in his rooms in Chelsea, a district then much frequented by those who had either too much art to afford Mayfair, or too little Mayfair to afford art.

Mr. Sterling sat in a chair that was beautiful but uncomfortable, and gazed at his host with the tragic eyes of a man who sees his dividends evaporating.

‘You cannot be serious, Adrian,’ he began, playing nervously with his watch-chain.

‘I am intensely serious,’ replied Melmoth, adjusting a singularly morbid orchid in a Venetian vase. ‘I have never been more serious in my life. Indeed, I have been serious for a whole week. It is a profoundly exhausting condition, but one that yields a certain bleak satisfaction.’

‘But the public!’ cried Mr. Sterling. ‘The public adores your "Sighing Zephyrs." The public demands another "Sighing Zephyrs."’

‘The public demands what it is given, because it knows no better,’ returned the composer, with a faint, weary smile. ‘To supply the public with what it demands is the trade of the grocer. To supply it with what it does not know it ought to desire is the privilege of the artist. I am tired of feeding the multitude on sugar. I intend to offer them bread, and if they find it too hard, they may break their teeth upon it. I have no objection to the breaking of teeth, provided the jaw is left intact to pronounce my name.’

‘You will ruin me,’ groaned the publisher.

‘I shall elevate you,’ corrected Melmoth. ‘To be ruined is merely a financial mishap; to be elevated is a moral necessity. Besides, you have plenty of other composers who manufacture musical sweets. I hear young Arbuthnot has just produced a ballad about a dying robin. It is, I am told, immensely affecting. The robin dies in the key of E flat, which is always a melancholy key, and the public weeps without knowing why. You shall be rich, even if I am obscure.’

Mr. Sterling departed, murmuring of contracts and clauses, and Melmoth was left to the silence of his room and the sombre majesty of his new resolutions.

The desertion of his admirers was swift and complete. It was felt in the drawing-rooms that Melmoth had committed an offence against good breeding. To write serious music was to imply that the drawing-rooms were not serious, which was an imputation no lady of fashion could tolerate. The duchesses who had warbled his melodies ceased to bow to him in the Park. The young men who had hummed his tunes while tying their cravats cut him dead at the clubs. Society is exceedingly kind to those who amuse it, but it is entirely merciless to those who instruct it. Melmoth found himself as isolated as a prophet, and prophets, as he often remarked, are generally stoned, not because they are wicked, but because their stones are more decorative than their doctrines.

It was in this hour of his social and commercial desolation that he received a visitor whom he had least expected: Mr. Evelyn Vane.

Vane was the one critic in London who had always despised Melmoth’s ballads. He had written of them in terms of elaborate contempt, dismissing them as the musical equivalent of a sentimental novel, things composed of false notes and falser feelings. He was a man of immense intellectual arrogance, with a style of prose that was as cold and brilliant as a diamond, and quite as devoid of warmth.

He arrived at the Chelsea rooms on a Tuesday afternoon, when the fog hung over the river like a grey pall, and the gas-lamps in the street were beginning to show their sickly yellow eyes.

‘I am told,’ said Vane, without any preliminary greeting, ‘that you have decided to cease being a fool.’

Melmoth, who was sitting before a piano, examining a manuscript that was heavily scored with black notes, looked up with a smile.

‘I have decided,’ he replied, ‘to cease being a tradesman. Whether that makes me a fool or a philosopher, I leave to you to determine. You are, I believe, an authority on both subjects.’

‘I am an authority on everything,’ said Vane, sitting down. ‘It is the privilege of the critic to be omniscient. I have come to ask if the rumour is true. Are you actually attempting to write a symphony?’

‘I am attempting to write the truth,’ said Melmoth. ‘A symphony is merely the most sonorous method of telling it.’

Vane raised his eyebrows. ‘The truth is rarely sonorous,’ he observed. ‘It is usually discordant. That is why Society avoids it, preferring the harmonious falsehood. You gave Society the harmonious falsehood, and Society rewarded you with a considerable income. You now propose to give it the discordant truth, and you will be rewarded with oblivion. Are you prepared for that?’

‘I am prepared for anything,’ returned Melmoth, ‘except being bored. The ballads bored me. They were too easily understood. A thing that is easily understood is never worth understanding. It is the obscure, the difficult, the inaccessible, that possess the true charm. I have spent my life making things easy for the intellect; I intend to spend the remainder of it making things hard.’

Vane gazed at him with a look of genuine, if somewhat cynical, interest. ‘You are a strange man, Melmoth,’ he said. ‘You have the vanity of a poet and the obstinacy of a mule. You will, of course, fail. The public will not listen to your symphony. They will say it is noise, and they will be right, for to the uneducated ear all truth is noise. But I shall come to hear it, and I shall write about it. I shall probably say that it is the work of a madman. But I shall say it with respect.’

‘That is the greatest compliment you have ever paid me,’ said Melmoth. ‘To be called a madman by you is to be acquitted of mediocrity. I would rather be a madman than a sentimentalist. A madman may utter a truth; a sentimentalist only utters a sigh.’

‘And yet,’ said Vane, with a faint, mocking smile, ‘your sighs were very profitable.’

‘Profit is the consolation of the uncreative,’ returned Melmoth. ‘It is what a man accepts when he cannot achieve fame. I have had profit. I desire, now, only the dangerous luxury of being misunderstood by the right people.’

‘The right people,’ repeated Vane, ‘are always the wrong people. However, I wish you well in your folly. It is a finer folly than your wisdom was.’

He rose to leave, and paused by the door. ‘By the way,’ he added, ‘the Duchess of Harley has taken young Arbuthnot’s dying robin into her repertoire. She sings it in the key of F, which is even more melancholy than E flat. The robin, I understand, is now practically extinct from over-singing.’

Melmoth laughed softly. ‘Let it die,’ he said. ‘I have no further use for robins. I am writing now of the sea, and the sea does not die. It merely drowns those who try to sing of it.’

When Vane had gone, Melmoth returned to his piano. The fog pressed against the window-panes, and the room was filled with the grey twilight of a London afternoon. He struck a chord that was strange and full of pain, a chord that had no precedent in the drawing-room. It was the beginning of his serious music, and it was the end of his popularity. But he was content, for he had discovered that the only thing worth having is the thing one cannot easily give away; and art, like a rare jewel, is valuable only because the multitude cannot wear it.
