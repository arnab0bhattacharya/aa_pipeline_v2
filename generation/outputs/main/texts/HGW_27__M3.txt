It is a curious thing to observe how thoroughly a population can divest itself of an ancestral terror, only to have that same terror return with compounded force. In the case of our great metropolis, the terror was the dark. I am, by profession, a Sub-Assistant Luminance Adjuster in the Municipal Lighting Office. My name is Elara Vance. My duties are largely tedious, consisting of the regulation of the great artificial suns that banish night from our city. We have not known a true night for over three centuries. The cost, in coal and chemical fuel, is staggering, but the civic fathers have always maintained that perpetual daylight is the cornerstone of our advanced civilisation. Crime is non-existent, productivity is ceaseless, and the old superstitions of ghosts and goblins have faded into mere nursery tales. 

It was on a Tuesday—I remember the date precisely, the fourteenth of May—that I first noticed the discrepancy. I was seated at my desk in the Lighting Office, a vast, brass-fitted chamber overlooking the square. My task was to tabulate the daily efficiency reports of Solar Reflector Number Four. The great mirrors, mounted on the highest iron towers, catch the chemical light generated in the subterranean vaults and cast it back across the rooftops. I noticed a minute but undeniable drop in the luminous output. It was a fraction of a candle-power, really, but it was there.

I took the reading to Mr. Pondereave, the Chief Illuminator. He was a stout, practical man with a deep aversion to anything that might upset the municipal budget.

“It is nothing, Miss Vance,” he said, adjusting his spectacles and peering at the figures. “A slight fluctuation in the chemical vats. We have had such things before. The men down below will correct it.”

“But the consistency of the drop,” I argued. “It is not a sudden fluctuation. It is a steady, mechanical decline. Like a clock winding down.”

“You see too much in a column of figures,” he replied Pondereave, with that bland, dismissive patience he reserved for subordinates. “The system has stood for two hundred years. It is not about to fail now. Run along and file your report.”

I filed my report, but I did not run along. Instead, I went down to the lower archives. The Lighting Office keeps meticulous records of every adjustment and every ounce of fuel consumed since the Great Illumination. It took me the better part of the afternoon to unearth the earliest logs. What I found there was alarming. The system was not merely fluctuating; it was dying. The chemical compounds that powered our suns were depleting at an exponential rate. The original engineers had calculated a lifespan of four centuries, but they had not accounted for the corrosive nature of the city’s atmosphere. The vats were dissolving from within.

I took this new data to the Civic Council. They were sitting in session, a room full of earnest, well-fed men who looked at me with a mixture of polite boredom and irritation. I laid out the charts. I explained the mathematics. I implored them to begin stockpiling combustibles, to institute a period of controlled darkness, to teach the people how to live without light.

“Miss Vance,” said the Mayor, a man named Haversham, “you are young, and you are excessively nervous. The city has never been brighter. Look out the window! The streets are as clear as noonday. To suggest we plunge the populace into darkness would be to invite panic. It is against reason.”

“But the figures—” I began.

“Figures can be made to show anything,” interrupted a Councilman named Filby. “You can show black is white by argument, but you will never convince me that our magnificent system is failing. The engineers assure us the vats are sound.”

“They are looking at the exterior shells,” I cried. “It is the interior lining that is corroding!”

“A likely story,” said the Mayor. “We shall appoint a committee to investigate your claims. In the meantime, Miss Vance, I suggest you confine your attentions to your desk.”

The committee, of course, took six weeks to form. In that time, the light dimmed further. It was no longer a fraction of a candle-power. It was a noticeable greying of the air, a faint muddying of the shadows in the alleys. People began to complain, but the Council merely ordered the vats to be stoked harder, which only accelerated the decay.

It was on a Tuesday evening—I recall it vividly, the twenty-fifth of June—when the end came. I was walking home from the office, my eyes heavy with the strain of watching the dials. The sky, which should have been a brilliant, artificial blue, was turning a sickly violet. Then, without warning, there was a deep, shuddering groan from beneath the streets. It was not an explosion, but a profound, structural sigh, as if the very earth had given up a burden. And then, the light went out.

It did not flicker. It simply ceased. The great suns went dark. The streetlamps, which drew their power from the same source, went dark. The glowing signs on the buildings went dark. In an instant, the city was plunged into a blackness so absolute, so complete, that it seemed to have substance.

I stood still, my hand gripping the iron railing of a bridge. And then, the screaming began.

It was not the screaming of physical pain. It was the screaming of minds unmoored. Three centuries of perpetual daylight had bred out of us the ancient tolerance for the dark. We were no longer savages who knew how to huddle around a fire and watch the shadows. We were a civilised people, accustomed to seeing everything, and the sudden absence of sight was a catastrophe beyond our comprehension.

I heard glass breaking. I heard people running, crashing into one another, into walls, into lampposts. A man ran past me, sobbing, his hands outstretched. I could hear the sound of feet scrambling on the cobblestones, the frantic whinnying of a horse. And above it all, a rising tide of panic, a wordless, formless terror.

I myself was not immune. I felt a coldness in my stomach, a primal urge to run, to find a light, any light. But I knew running was useless. I knew the geography of the city, but in that absolute blackness, every street was a chasm. I forced myself to be calm. I remembered the old tales my grandmother had told me, of a time before the Great Illumination, when people carried small, portable fires. I had a box of matches in my pocket, a relic of a hobby of mine.

I struck one. The tiny flame sprang up, and in its feeble glow, I saw the face of the terror. People were not merely stumbling; they were clawing at the air, at their own faces. A woman nearby had fallen and was lying still, her eyes wide open, staring at nothing. A man was beating his fists against a brick wall, crying out for the light.

The match burned my fingers. I struck another, and another. I gathered some loose paper from the gutter and lit it. The small fire drew people like moths. They clustered around me, their faces pale and streaked with tears.

“Be still!” I shouted. “Be still! The dark will not hurt you!”

But they did not believe me. They had forgotten how to believe in anything but the light.
