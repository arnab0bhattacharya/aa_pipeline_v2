I had determined, from the very moment my brother introduced us, that Mrs. Clara Blackwood was a fortune hunter. She stood in the drawing-room of our family estate, Thorneleigh Hall, with a complexion too clear and a manner too serene for a woman who had spent her formative years in the shadow of a declining London trade house. Her gown was simple, yes, and her jewels modest, but I saw the calculation behind her soft brown eyes. She had managed to secure Julian, and with him, Thorneleigh. I decided it was my duty to expose her.

I began my surveillance the morning after the wedding breakfast. The sun crept through the heavy damask curtains of the breakfast parlor, illuminating the silver tea service. I sat in the corner, pretending to mend a handkerchief. Clara entered, her morning dress a pale, unadorned muslin. She went straight to the sideboard. I watched her hands, expecting them to hover over the costly imported chocolates or the sweet meats. Instead, she took a piece of dry toast and a cup of plain tea. She did not even take butter. I frowned. A woman secure in her new position would indulge. Her restraint was a performance, designed to make us believe she was unaccustomed to wealth. I saw through it instantly.

Julian entered, his face bright with that foolish, lovesick expression he had worn all month. "Clara, my dear, you must let Mrs. Hodges make you a proper breakfast. There are kidneys, eggs—"

"I am perfectly satisfied, Julian," Clara said, her voice carrying that maddening, unrippled calm. "Mrs. Hodges is a treasure, and I would not distress her with extra work for my sake."

She was currying favor with the servants. I noted it. A fortune hunter knows that a loyal staff is the best defense against a suspicious family. By praising the housekeeper, she was building a fortress of allies.

Over the next week, I attached myself to her side like a shadow. I accompanied her on her walks through the gardens, watched her during her visits to the tenants, and sat beside her in the drawing-room during the evenings. I was looking for a slip, a moment of greed or vanity that would prove my case.

On Tuesday, we walked to the village. The road was dusty, and I had worn my sturdy boots. Clara wore a pair of plain leather shoes that were already acquiring a fine coat of dirt. As we passed the milliner’s shop, I deliberately slowed my pace. "Oh, look at that bonnet," I said, pointing to an elaborate confection of silk and ostrich feather in the window. "It is quite the finest in the county. I know you must miss the fashions of Town, Mrs. Blackwood. Julian would be only too happy to purchase it for you."

It was a test. A true fortune hunter would leap at the chance to spend her husband’s money on such a frivolity.

Clara looked at the bonnet, her head tilted slightly. "It is very beautiful," she said. "But I think the blue ribbon would clash with my spencer. Besides, I have a perfectly good bonnet at home. It would be wasteful to buy another."

She had deflected with practicality. I felt a spike of frustration. She was too clever. She knew I was watching, and she was carefully masking her desires. Her refusal was not born of genuine modesty but of a strategic mind. She was playing the long game, securing Julian’s trust so she could bleed him slowly over the years.

When we returned to the Hall, I found her in the stillroom, sorting through dried herbs. The air smelled of lavender and rosemary. She was making sachets for the linen presses. I leaned against the doorframe, observing her quick, efficient fingers.

"You take to the duties of a country wife with remarkable ease," I said, letting a hint of sarcasm color my tone.

She looked up, a small smile on her lips. "I find purpose in keeping busy, Miss Blackwood. A idle mind is a burden, is it not?"

She had turned my inspection into a philosophical reflection. I tightened my grip on my sewing. She was impenetrable.

The following evening, we were gathered in the drawing-room. Julian was reading aloud from a periodical, and my mother was dozing by the fire. I sat opposite Clara, watching her over the rim of my teacup. She was working on a piece of embroidery, a simple border of ivy leaves. Her stitches were even and small. I noticed the thread she was using was not silk but plain cotton.

"You use cotton thread," I remarked. "Silk would be more becoming for a woman of your new station."

Clara did not look up from her work. "Cotton wears better," she said. "And the ivy will be on a cushion for the hall chair. It will see much use. Silk would be ruined in a month."

Again, the practicality. I felt a tightness in my chest. She was always correct, always modest, always thinking of the household. It was unnatural. No woman of her background, raised in the cramped quarters above a merchant’s warehouse, could be so indifferent to the luxuries now within her reach. She was hiding her true nature, burying it under layers of domestic virtue. I was certain of it.

I decided to set a more elaborate trap. I knew the bailiff, Mr. Spears, was coming to discuss the quarterly accounts with Julian. I arranged to be in the library when the meeting concluded, hoping Clara might appear and show an unseemly interest in the estate’s finances.

She did not come. But later that afternoon, I found her in the kitchen garden, picking beans for the evening meal. She had her sleeves rolled up, and her hands were stained with earth.

"I thought you might be with Julian," I said. "Reviewing the accounts."

Clara dropped a bean into her basket. "Julian manages the estate. I manage the house. That is the proper division of labor, is it not?"

She was evading my trap with the skill of a seasoned diplomat. I began to feel a grudging respect, which only deepened my resentment. Her perfection was a weapon, and she wielded it with devastating effect.

A few days later, I was walking through the portrait gallery when I overheard Clara speaking with Mrs. Hodges. I pressed myself against the wall, just out of sight.

"The blue damask in the guest chamber is becoming quite faded," Mrs. Hodges was saying. "Shall we order new hangings from London? Mr. Blackwood would not object, I am sure."

"I am sure he would not," Clara replied. "But the fabric is still sound. A good cleaning and a new lining will see it through another few years. We must not be extravagant, Mrs. Hodges. The estate has its responsibilities, and we must do our part."

I stepped away, my mind racing. She was refusing to spend money on the house itself. She was refusing to spend it on clothes, on bonnets, on food. She was a fortress of economy.

And that was when I understood. Her modesty was not a mask. It was a strategy of a different kind. She was not simply hiding her greed. She was cultivating an image of such flawless virtue that Julian would never believe a word against her. By denying herself the small indulgences, she was securing her claim to the larger fortune. She was making herself indispensable, not through extravagance, but through frugality. She was positioning herself as the savior of the estate, the woman who would protect the Blackwood wealth from ruin.

It was, I realized, the most sophisticated form of fortune hunting I had ever encountered. She did not want the money to spend. She wanted the money to own. By wrapping herself in the mantle of household economy, she was making herself the guardian of the family’s fortune. And Julian, blinded by his affection, would hand her the keys to the entire kingdom.

I sat down on a bench in the gallery, the portraits of my ancestors gazing down at me with their painted eyes. I had watched her for a fortnight, and I had found no proof of avarice. But I had found something far more alarming. I had found a woman who understood that true power lay not in the spending of wealth, but in the control of it.

Clara appeared at the end of the gallery, carrying a small vase of cut flowers. She saw me and smiled, that same gentle, unruffled smile.

"You look troubled, Miss Blackwood," she said. "May I sit with you?"

I looked at her, this woman with her plain dress and her earth-stained hands, and I felt a cold certainty settle in my stomach. "You are very kind," I said, the words tasting bitter on my tongue.

She sat beside me, arranging the flowers on the bench between us. She did not press me to speak. She simply sat, a picture of quiet companionship.

And as I looked at her, I knew I had lost. She had outmaneuvered me at every turn. Her propriety was her armor, and I had no weapon that could pierce it. I could not accuse a woman of greed who ate dry toast and mended her own gowns. I could not expose a fortune hunter who refused to spend a fortune.

She was a fortune hunter of the most dangerous kind. She did not want the money. She wanted the power that came with keeping it. And she had already begun to take it, stitch by careful stitch, ivy leaf by ivy leaf, bean by bean. Thorneleigh was no longer my family’s home. It was hers.
