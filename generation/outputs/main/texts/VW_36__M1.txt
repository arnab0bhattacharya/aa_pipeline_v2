The train had left the valley far below, a smudge of grey smoke and the thin, remembered sound of the whistle, and now the air was a different thing altogether; it was a substance, a cold, clean element that you did not so much breathe as drink. It filled the lungs with a sharpness that was almost a taste, like water from a deep, dark well. For Julian, propped against the plush of the carriage seat, this new air was the very reason for his being here, prescribed as surely as the little white tablets he swallowed morning and night. His illness, a lingering fever that had clung to him all through the London spring, had left him thin and transparent, so that the light seemed to pass through him rather than fall upon him. Now, he was told, the mountains would make him solid again.

He watched the world tilt and rise outside the window. The dark pines, which had been a forest, became a texture, a dark green cloth laid over the steep slopes. Then even that texture faded, and there were only the grey bones of the world, the rock, and above them, a white so absolute it seemed to swallow the sky. The Grand Hôtel des Cimes materialised not as a building but as a sudden, impossible patch of order upon that chaos of stone and snow. It was all white walls and green shutters and a great glass conservatory that caught the sun and threw it back in a blinding, liquid glare.

Inside, the world was hushed and warm. A great fire of logs crackled in the hall, its heat a soft pressure on the cheek. There was the low murmur of voices, the rustle of a newspaper, the distant, melancholic chime of a piano. This was a world of the unwell and those who tended them. He saw it in the careful, measured steps of a woman in black, in the pale, waiting face of a man by the window, in the quiet desperation that lay just beneath the surface of the polite greetings. They were all here to be made solid again, or to watch over those who were. He felt a kinship with them, a shared fragility that was a kind of comfort.

It was on the terrace, where the air was still and the sun fell in a warm, golden sheet, that he first saw her. She was not like the others. She was not reading, nor was she staring out at the magnificent, indifferent peaks with the vacant gaze of convalescence. She was doing nothing at all, simply sitting in a wicker chair, her hands folded in her lap, her face turned up to the light. There was a stillness about her that was not the stillness of weakness but of a profound and self-contained peace. She was entirely alone, yet she seemed more complete than any of the groups around her. Her presence disturbed the quiet order of the place. She was a note of vibrant, unexplained colour in a room of muted greys and whites.

He saw her again at dinner, at a small table near the window. A formidable older woman, her aunt he supposed, sat opposite her, speaking in a low, insistent voice. The young woman—her name, he learned from a whispered conversation between two nurses, was Elara—did not reply. She simply listened, her gaze fixed on a point somewhere beyond her aunt’s shoulder, on the last, rose-gold stain of light upon the snow. She was not ill; that was clear. Her cheeks had a bloom to them, her movements were fluid and certain. She was here for some other reason, an escape perhaps, from a life that was too full, too loud, too demanding. The thought came to him with the force of a revelation. She had come to the mountains to be empty, while he had come to be filled.

Days passed in a gentle, monotonous rhythm. The sun would flood the white corridors in the morning, and the air would grow thin and cold by evening. Julian would walk slowly along the gravel paths, his breath pluming in the chill, and he would watch for her. Sometimes he saw her with a book, but she rarely turned the pages. Her eyes were on the horizon, on the slow, silent procession of clouds that gathered and dissolved around the highest peaks. They never spoke. Their acquaintance was a thing of glances, of a shared silence in the conservatory as the afternoon light slanted through the glass, illuminating the dust motes in a golden, swirling dance. In those moments, he felt he understood her completely. He understood her need for this vast, silent emptiness, this freedom from the intricate web of human expectation that had so ensnared her.

One afternoon, a sudden squall blew up from the west. The sky, which had been a brilliant, aching blue, turned a bruised and violent purple. Snow began to fall, not gently, but in a blinding, horizontal rush that erased the mountains, the hotel, the world. Julian was in the library, a small, dark room that smelled of leather and old paper. He stood by the window, watching the storm erase everything. The door opened and closed softly behind him. He did not need to turn to know who it was. He could feel her presence in the room, a calmness in the heart of the tempest.

They stood in silence for a long time, watching the whiteness consume the view.

“It is a mercy,” she said at last, her voice quiet, yet it filled the room. “It hides everything.”

He turned. Her face was pale in the gloom, her eyes dark and serious. “I was thinking it was a pity,” he said. “To lose the view.”

“Oh, no,” she replied, and a faint smile touched her lips. “The view is always there. It is the one thing you can be sure of. It is a relief to have it taken away for a little while. To see nothing.”

And he understood then that she was not talking about the mountains. She was talking of the relentless clarity of her own life, the endless, demanding view of family and duty and social obligation from which she had fled. Here, in this storm, she was finally hidden. He felt a sudden, sharp pang of something that was not pity, but a profound and aching recognition. He, too, was hidden here. His illness had been his blizzard, his excuse to retreat from the world.

He wanted to tell her this, to bridge the small space between them with words, but what was there to say? Their understanding was too delicate for speech. To speak of it would be to make it solid, to give it a shape it did not possess. So he simply nodded.

The fire in the library had burned low. The only light was the strange, luminous glow of the snow against the windowpane. They stood there, two figures in the quiet, sheltered dark, while the storm raged on, a great, white, silent mercy. The snow fell and fell, covering the paths, the terraces, the hard, grey rocks, until there was nothing left but a soft, undifferentiated world, a world without edges or demands, a world in which two people could stand, for a moment, perfectly still.
