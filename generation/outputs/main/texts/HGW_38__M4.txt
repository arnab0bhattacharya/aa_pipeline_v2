The trouble began, as such troubles so often do, not with a bang nor even with a very perceptible whimper, but with a certain unaccountable tidiness in Mr. Henry Ponderby’s north forty. Ponderby was a man of the old school, who believed in the rotation of crops as he believed in the Ten Commandments, and the appearance of a dense, even, and strangely vigorous stand of wheat in a field that had been sown with a modest and, he had thought, quite adequate dressing of clover and rye-grass, was a source of profound moral and agricultural offence to him. He spoke of it at the market ordinary in Wymondham, and the landlord, a lean, sceptical man named Croot, said it was the weather, and that a man who expected the land to do the same thing twice running was a fool.

But the wheat did not confine itself to the north forty. It appeared, with the same disconcerting neatness, in the barley of Mr. Jessop, a Nonconformist of rigid views and a heavy hand with his sons; it threaded itself through the roots of the turnips on the glebe farm of the Reverend Mr. Pashley, who wrote a letter about it to the Bishop and received no reply; and it came up, with a sort of quiet, botanical insolence, in the very midst of Mrs. Hart’s prize-winning cabbage patch, where it stood taller and greener than anything she had ever coaxed from the soil in thirty years of widowhood and manure. By the middle of June it was apparent to the meanest intelligence that the whole district was in the grip of a single, monstrously fertile variety of wheat, a wheat that had no respect for boundaries, for hedges, for the carefully considered plans of man, and that was, moreover, choking the life out of every other green thing that sought to share the sun with it.

The author of this botanical pestilence was a small, wiry, abstracted man of fifty-five, with a bald forehead, a straggling reddish beard, and the kind of mild, speculative blue eye that seems to be looking at something a great deal more interesting than the object immediately before it. His name was Septimus Gannet, and he was the director of the East Anglian Experimental Station for the Improvement of Forage and Cereal Crops, a modest establishment of red brick and corrugated iron situated on a piece of poor, sandy, and, as the neighbouring farmers had long ago agreed, quite hopeless land some three miles from Attleborough. To this station, for the past seven years, Gannet had devoted himself with an enthusiasm that bordered upon the monomaniacal. His particular ambition was to produce a wheat that would flourish upon the light, hungry soils of the district, soils that had hitherto been fit only for rye and the hardier sorts of sheep. He had crossed and re-crossed, selected and re-selected, until at last, in the spring of the previous year, he had obtained a strain that seemed to him to possess every virtue. It was hardy, it was early, it was prolific, and it had a root system of such aggressive efficiency that it seemed to draw sustenance from the very air.

The first intimation he had that his creation had escaped the bounds of the experimental plots was a call from a large, red-faced, and extremely angry farmer named Wherry, who drove up to the station in a dog-cart one afternoon in early July and demanded to see the man who had ruined him. Gannet, who was in the act of examining a slide under a microscope, received him with the gentle courtesy of a man who has been interrupted in a matter of far greater importance.

“Mr. Gannet,” said Wherry, planting his boots firmly on the laboratory floor and looking about him with an eye that plainly regarded the whole establishment as a kind of licensed lunatic asylum, “I want to know what you mean by it.”

“By what, Mr. Wherry?” said Gannet, mildly.

“By this damned wheat of yours,” said Wherry, with a sweep of his arm that threatened the microscope. “It’s all over my land. It’s in my barley, it’s in my roots, it’s coming up in the very road. It’s worse than charlock. It’s worse than twitch. It’s a plague, that’s what it is, and I hold you responsible.”

Gannet’s face expressed a polite and scientific interest. “Dear me,” he said. “You don’t say so. It has spread, then?”

“Spread?” cried Wherry. “It’s a flood. It’s a judgment. I’ve got a field of it that’s so thick you could not get a rabbit through it, and it’s smothering my mangolds. And I’m not the only one. There’s Jessop, and Pashley, and old Mother Hart, and half a dozen more, all a-coming to-morrow to a meeting at the King’s Head to see what’s to be done about it. And I tell you straight, Mr. Gannet, they mean to have the law of you. They’re talking of an injunction, or a prosecution, or something. They say you’ve let loose a noxious weed.”

Gannet stroked his beard thoughtfully. “It is not a noxious weed, Mr. Wherry,” he said. “It is a very valuable variety of Triticum vulgare. I call it Gannet’s Hardy Bearded. It is, I believe, the most promising wheat for light soils that has ever been produced.”

“I don’t care what you call it,” said Wherry, whose face had grown a deeper and more alarming shade of crimson. “It’s a nuisance, and it’s got to be stopped. It’s spreading like a fire. It’s got into my hedges, and it’s coming up in my wife’s flower-beds. What do you propose to do about it?”

“Do?” said Gannet. “Why, nothing. It is a wheat. It is a good wheat. If it grows, it will produce a crop. Surely that is what you farmers want?”

“I want my own crop,” said Wherry, “and not a wilderness of your infernal experiments. I want my barley, and my roots, and my clover, and I want ’em without a lot of damned scientific wheat a-growing up in the middle of ’em. And what’s more, I’ve got a good mind to take a stick to you.”

He did not, however, carry out this threat. He contented himself with a few more remarks of a pungent and personal character, and drove away in a cloud of dust, leaving Gannet to his microscope and his reflections. The reflections were not pleasant. He had, he was obliged to admit to himself, been guilty of a certain carelessness. The pollen of his new strain, borne upon the wind, had fertilised the ordinary sorts of the district, and the resulting hybrids, possessing the aggressive vigour of the new creation, had spread with a rapidity that he had not foreseen. The thing was a botanical triumph, but it was also, from the farmers’ point of view, a catastrophe.

The meeting at the King’s Head was a stormy one. It was held in the large upper room, under the low, smoke-blackened ceiling, and it was attended by every farmer of any consequence in the district, together with a number of smaller men who came chiefly for the beer and the excitement. Wherry presided, and his opening speech was a model of that kind of blunt, ungrammatical eloquence which is so effective in a country district. He described the wheat as a “blight,” a “pestilence,” and a “visitation,” and he called upon Mr. Gannet to come forward and explain himself.

Gannet rose, a small, slightly stooping figure in a shabby tweed suit, and looked at the sea of red and angry faces before him. He was not a man of the world, but he was a man of science, and he had a profound belief in the power of fact.

“Gentlemen,” he said, “I have listened with great attention to what has been said, and I am bound to admit that there is a great deal of truth in it. The wheat has spread. It has spread beyond my expectations. It has, in fact, spread beyond my control. I regret that. But I would ask you to consider for a moment what this wheat is. It is not a weed. It is not a disease. It is a new and valuable variety of wheat, especially adapted to the poor soils of this district. It has been produced by careful selection and cross-breeding, and it is, I believe, destined to be of great benefit to agriculture.”

A murmur of dissent ran round the room. “Benefit?” cried a voice from the back. “It’s ruining us.”

“It is ruining you,” said Gannet, calmly, “because you are trying to grow other crops in the same field. But suppose you were to grow this wheat and nothing else? It would give you a better return than any crop you have ever grown on this land. I have the figures here.”

He produced a notebook and began to read out a series of statistics relating to the yield of his new strain on the experimental plots. The farmers listened in stony silence. They were not interested in statistics. They were interested in their own fields, their own crops, and their own immediate prospects. When he had finished, there was a moment of silence, and then a small, sharp-featured man named Tusser, who farmed a few acres on the edge of the heath, rose to his feet.

“That’s all very well, Mr. Gannet,” he said, “but what are we to do? We can’t sell a crop that nobody wants. There’s no market for your new wheat. The millers won’t look at it. It’s a foreigner, a upstart. We’ve got our own sorts, and our own ways, and we don’t want to be messed about by a lot of scientific gentry. What we want is to get rid of the stuff, and that’s what we mean to do.”

“But how?” asked Gannet.

“By burning it,” said Tusser, “and by pulling it up, and by putting salt on it, and by doing anything else we can think of. And we want you to pay for it.”

The meeting ended in a resolution, proposed by Wherry and seconded by Tusser, to the effect that Mr. Gannet be requested to take immediate steps to eradicate the wheat from the lands of the district, and that a committee be formed to assess the damages. Gannet walked home in the dusk, a solitary, troubled figure on the white Norfolk road, with the scent of his own triumphant wheat in his nostrils and the sound of the farmers’ anger still in his ears. He looked at the darkening fields, where the new wheat stood tall and strong among the ruins of the old crops, and he felt a sudden, strange pang of doubt. Was it possible, he wondered, that he had been too clever? That in his eagerness to improve upon nature he had produced something that was, in its very perfection, a monster? The stars came out, one by one, over the flat, mysterious landscape, and he walked on, a small, perplexed, and deeply thoughtful man, into the night.
