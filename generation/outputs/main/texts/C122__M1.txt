The cottage of the woodcutter, Yoric, was the last human dwelling before the great forest of Eldwood began its long, dark reign over the northern kingdom of Veridia. In this cottage lived Yoric and his son, Finn. Finn was twelve years old, with eyes the colour of a winter sky and a left leg that had been twisted since birth. He could not run with the other boys in the village of Oakhaven; he could only watch from the cottage window as they chased each other down the muddy lane, their laughter like birdsong he could hear but never quite touch. His world was the small patch of garden, the smell of his father’s pipe, and the endless, patient view of the trees.

The stag began its visits on a moonless night. It was not a creature of the forest, not truly. It was made of moonlight and silence, its coat the colour of fresh snow, its antlers like a crown of bleached ivory. It did not graze on the wild clover in the yard or drink from the rain barrel. It simply stood at the edge of the clearing, its dark, liquid eyes fixed upon the cottage, upon the small, dark window where Finn lay awake. It was a beacon in the oppressive dark.

For three nights, Finn watched it from his bed, his heart a frantic drum against his ribs. On the fourth night, driven by a longing that was sharper than any hunger, he slid from under his woollen blankets. He strapped on his wooden brace, the familiar leather and buckles a comfort and a curse. He moved slowly and quietly, his lame leg dragging a soft rhythm on the floorboards. He unlatched the cottage door, the groan of the iron hinges swallowed by the vast quiet of the night.

The stag did not flee. It turned its elegant head and began to walk, not towards the village, but into the maw of Eldwood. Finn followed. The brace was clumsy on the forest floor, catching on roots and sinking into soft moss. Thorns snagged his trousers. The air grew colder, smelling of damp earth and decay. But the white stag moved with an impossible grace, its pale form a guiding star through the suffocating gloom. It never looked back, yet it never outpaced him, its steady, silent pace perfectly matched to his own halting gait.

They walked for hours, or perhaps only for minutes. Time had become a strange and fluid thing. The trees grew impossibly tall, their branches weaving a canopy that blotted out the sky. The only light came from the stag itself, a soft, internal luminescence that cast long, dancing shadows. Then, through the trees, came another light: a cold, blue-white glow. The stag stepped aside, melting back into the shadows as if it had been a dream. Finn limped forward and stood on a precipice, looking down.

Below him lay a city. It was carved from the very rock of the mountain, its spires and domes and elegant bridges all hewn from a single, seamless sheet of grey stone. There was no wood, no thatch, no clay. The city was lit by glowing crystals set into the walls, casting a sterile, shadowless light over everything. It was beautiful in the way a perfectly cut gemstone is beautiful: cold, hard, and utterly without warmth. He could hear no sound from it—no laughter, no crying, no clamour of trade. Only a low, resonant hum, like a great, sleeping machine.

He found a set of stone steps carved into the cliff face and began his slow descent. At the bottom, a gate stood open. The city, which he would later learn was called Aethelgard, was populated, but its people were a mystery. There were no children, their high voices absent from the air. There were no old men or women, their faces smooth and unlined, their hair without a streak of silver. And no one was lame. Every person Finn saw moved with a fluid, effortless grace that made him feel like a broken thing. Their movements were perfect, synchronized, as if they were dancers in a play he did not understand.

But the most unsettling thing was their faces. They were handsome, placid, and utterly devoid of emotion. There was no joy in their eyes, no sorrow, no anger, no curiosity. They passed each other on the polished streets without a word or a glance, their expressions as blank and empty as the stone around them. Finn, with his twisted leg and his face full of wonder and fear, was an anomaly, a splash of muddy colour in a world of grey.

He saw a woman shaping a crystal on a wheel, her hands moving with flawless precision. Her face was a mask of placid concentration. He saw two men standing by a silent fountain, their gazes fixed on nothing. They did not speak. They simply… were. The city hummed on around them, a perfect, empty machine. No one was happy, Finn realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the cold stone. Happiness required hope, or memory, or desire. Here, there was only existence.

He was drawn to a central plaza where a single, massive crystal pulsed with the same cold light as the city itself. An old woman—or what he first took for an old woman—sat on a stone bench before it. Her face was unlined, like all the others, but her eyes held a weariness that seemed ancient. She was the only one who looked at him.

“You are not of Aethelgard,” she said. Her voice was not a question. It was a flat statement, like the tolling of a distant bell.

“No,” Finn stammered, his hand tightening on his brace. “I followed a stag.”

A flicker of something, perhaps memory, crossed her placid features. “The Guide. It brings the unfinished here sometimes. Those with cracks in them.”

“What is this place?” Finn asked, gesturing to the perfect, silent people.

“This is the city of Perfection,” she said, her gaze returning to the pulsing crystal. “We are all whole here. We are all strong. We do not age. We do not sicken. We do not suffer the frailties of the flesh.”

“But you’re not happy,” Finn whispered.

The woman looked back at him, and for the first time, he saw a glimmer of profound, bottomless sorrow in her eyes. “Happiness is a flaw, child. It is an imperfection, a wild and unpredictable thing. It is born of want, and we want for nothing. It is born of love, and love is a madness that leads to loss. It is born of pain overcome, and we feel no pain. We traded it all away. Our sorrows, our tears, our aches, our ages… and with them, our joy. We are perfect, and we are empty.”

She looked down at his leg, at the worn leather of the brace. “You are full of cracks,” she said, and her voice held a note of something that was not pity, but a deep, aching envy. “That is why you can see the stag. It cannot be seen by the flawless. It is a creature of imperfection, of wildness, of the world we left behind.”

A sound echoed through the plaza—the faint, distant cry of a bird. It was a sharp, broken, beautiful sound. The people of Aethelgard did not react, but the woman on the bench closed her eyes, a single, perfect tear tracing a path down her smooth cheek. It was the first sign of true emotion he had seen, and it was a tear of pure, undiluted grief.

“You must go back,” she urged, her eyes opening wide with a desperate urgency. “Do not let them see you. The whole would try to fix you. They would smooth your cracks and polish your flaws until you were as perfect and as empty as we are. Run back to the forest. Back to your world of beautiful, broken things.”

Finn did not need to be told twice. He turned and fled, his lame leg a clumsy anchor, his heart pounding with a terror and a pity so fierce it felt like a fire in his chest. He scrambled up the stone steps, his breath coming in ragged sobs. The city of Aethelgard, in its perfect, silent beauty, was the loneliest place in the world.

When he reached the top of the cliff, the white stag was waiting for him at the edge of the trees. It dipped its great, ivory head as if in acknowledgement, then turned and began the journey back. This time, the walk was faster, the way clearer. The forest seemed to welcome him, the familiar scents of pine and damp earth a balm to his soul. He emerged into the clearing just as the first hint of dawn was turning the eastern sky a soft grey.

The stag paused at the edge of the trees one last time. It looked at Finn, its dark eyes seeming to hold the memory of the city and the sorrow of its people. Then, it dissolved, not into the forest, but into the morning mist, its form breaking apart like smoke in the wind.

Finn limped back to the cottage. His father was just stirring, the smell of porridge beginning to fill the small room. The world was the same as it had been the night before. The muddy lane, the silent forest, the small, warm cottage. But Finn was not the same. He looked down at his brace, at the twisted leg it held, and for the first time in his life, he did not feel its weight as a burden. He felt it as a key. It was the crack through which the wild, imperfect light of the world could enter. He was broken, and because he was broken, he could be happy. He was whole.
