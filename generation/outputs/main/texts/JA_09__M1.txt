Mr. Ferris’s arrival at the rectory of St. Cuthbert’s was an event of the first consequence, for the parish of Ashby-Welland had been agog with the intelligence for a fortnight. Mr. Ferris, a cousin of Mrs. Pomeroy’s, was the heir to a considerable fortune in the West Indies, and, being now in his thirtieth year, had declared his intention of taking a wife from among his own countrywomen. The Pomeroys, with three marriageable daughters and a living that, while respectable, could not be called lucrative, were naturally to be considered the foremost candidates in the county.

The visit was conducted with a solemnity that bordered on the ceremonial. Mrs. Pomeroy had aired the best linens and impressed upon her daughters the necessity of appearing at their most pleasing. Arabella, the eldest, a young woman of twenty-two whose beauty was of a serene and classical order, was to be the principal object of his attention. Clara, at nineteen, whose attractions were of a livelier, more conversational sort, was to be her charming support. And then there was Sophia.

Sophia, at fifteen, was the bane of her mother’s existence and the despair of her sisters. She possessed a quickness of wit that she employed with an almost reckless lack of discretion, and a habit of speaking her mind that was wholly untempered by the conventional arts of pleasing. It was universally agreed at St. Cuthbert’s that she was too clever for her own good.

Mr. Ferris proved to be a man of handsome person and an address that was, perhaps, a little too conscious of its own superiority. He spoke of his sugar plantations with an air of proprietorship that seemed to extend to the very air of the room, and his opinions on society were delivered with the confidence of one who had purchased the right to them. He listened to Mr. Pomeroy on the state of the parish with a polite, but not quite concealed, impatience, and turned the conversation to London at the earliest opportunity.

Arabella received his compliments with a graceful blush, and Clara answered his questions about the local assemblies with a fluency that was meant to be enchanting. Sophia, who had been observing the proceedings from her place by the window with an expression of intense, critical amusement, chose this moment to enter the conversation.

“You must find the country a great trial, Mr. Ferris,” she said, her voice clear and carrying. “After the diversions of town, I wonder you can bear the quiet. Do you find it very dull to be obliged to converse with people who have no new bonnets to discuss?”

A dreadful silence fell upon the party. Mrs. Pomeroy looked as though she might faint, and Arabella shot her sister a look of such profound horror that it might have withered a less resilient spirit. Clara’s fan fluttered to her lap with a sound of finality.

Mr. Ferris, however, did not appear offended. He turned to the youngest Miss Pomeroy, and a slow smile, genuine and rather surprised, spread across his features.

“You are direct, Miss Sophia,” he observed.

“I find it saves a great deal of time,” she replied, meeting his gaze without a trace of confusion. “And it prevents one from being misunderstood. But I see I have caused my mother some distress. She believes conversation should be like a well-behaved dog, and never jump up.”

Mrs. Pomeroy found her voice. “Sophia, my love, you must not tease our guest. Pray forgive her, Mr. Ferris; she is a mere child.”

“On the contrary,” said Mr. Ferris, still looking at Sophia. “I find her observation most acute. I confess, the absence of new bonnets is a severe deprivation.”

He then began to question her, not with the indulgent condescension one might show a child, but with a genuine curiosity. He asked her opinion of the novels from the circulating library, of the character of the local squires, and of the merits of the sermon she had endured that morning. Sophia, far from being abashed, answered him with a candour that was at once witty and startlingly perceptive. She mocked the hero of a popular romance as a man who would be insufferable in a drawing-room, and declared their principal neighbour, Sir Reginald Thorne, to be a man whose library was more impressive than his conversation.

Throughout this exchange, Mr. Ferris appeared more and more diverted. He had come to Ashby-Welland in search of a wife, and had pictured a young woman of beauty and accomplishment, who would adorn his table and be grateful for his protection. He had not anticipated a fifteen-year-old philosopher in a white muslin gown, who treated his worldly experience as a subject for gentle ridicule.

Arabella and Clara sat in a state of frozen mortification, certain that the match was ruined beyond all hope of repair. They could not comprehend it. They had offered him beauty, deference, and agreeable nonsense, and he had ignored it all in favour of Sophia’s sharp tongue.

When at last Mr. Ferris took his leave of the rectory the following day, it was not with an offer for Arabella, nor even for Clara. He took Mr. Pomeroy aside and spoke to him for some time in the study, before departing for London with a promise to return.

It was only after his carriage had disappeared down the lane that the truth was revealed. Mr. Pomeroy, looking more bewildered than triumphant, informed his family that Mr. Ferris had been captivated, utterly and completely, by the youngest Miss Pomeroy. He had confessed that he had come prepared to admire, but had not expected to be so thoroughly entertained, and that he found in Sophia’s mind a challenge and a delight that no other young woman had ever offered him. He intended to return in a month’s time to pay his addresses to her, with her father’s permission.

A silence of a different kind now filled the room. Mrs. Pomeroy looked from her husband to her youngest daughter, her mind a whirl of confusion. Arabella, after a moment, managed a smile that was both gracious and relieved, for the burden of expectation had been lifted from her shoulders. Clara, however, could not so easily forgive.

“Well, Sophia,” she said, her voice tight with a resentment she could not conceal. “You have certainly succeeded in making yourself remarkable.”

Sophia, who had been watching the last of the dust from the carriage settle on the hedgerow, turned to her sister. A thoughtful, and perhaps not entirely unhappy, expression was on her face.

“Indeed,” she said. “It appears I have. I had not thought it so easy.”
