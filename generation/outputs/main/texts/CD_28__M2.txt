The shop of Barnaby and Barnaby stood on the high street of Chatham like a stoic old soldier, grey-faced and dignified, with a brass plate above the door that had long since lost its shine to the soot of the Navy and the smoke of the docks. It was a place of respectable mediocrity, smelling perpetually of dried lavender, stale tobacco, and the faint, sweet rot of over-ripe pears. Inside, the air was thick with the dust of a century and the silence of a ledger. Here presided Silas Barnaby, the elder brother, a man whose head was as round and bald as a billiard ball, and whose eyes, set deep in their sockets, possessed the penetrating quality of a hawk that had eaten too many mice. Silas was a creature of habit, of precision, and of a profound, unshakeable belief that a penny saved was a penny earned, and a penny spent was a soul lost. He wore a waistcoat that had been mended so many times it had become a patchwork quilt of its former self, and he moved through the aisles of tins and jars with the solemnity of a priest performing a high mass.

His brother, Felix, was the very antithesis of this order. Where Silas was stone, Felix was water; where Silas was the ledger, Felix was the dream. Felix Barnaby had a face that was always on the verge of a smile, though the smile itself rarely arrived, leaving him with an expression of perpetual, pleasant bewilderment. He was the sort of man who could look at a sack of flour and see not a commodity, but a landscape of white dunes stretching into an endless, flour-dusted horizon. He did not run the shop; he drifted through it, borrowing shillings from the till to buy ribbons for his hat, or engaging in long, philosophical debates with the fishmonger next door about the nature of the tide. He was charming, he was frivolous, and he was, in the estimation of his brother, a calamity wrapped in a velvet coat.

It was on a Tuesday, when the light in the shop was a dull, yellowish grey, that Felix made his announcement. He stood by the counter, holding a letter with the delicacy of a surgeon holding a scalpel, while Silas, behind the till, weighed a parcel of tea with a frown that could have curdled milk.

"Silas," said Felix, his voice taking on a tone of theatrical gravity that was entirely at odds with the mundane surroundings. "I have made a discovery."

"About the price of currants?" Silas grunted, not looking up from his scales. "I tell you, Felix, the currants are up a penny. I shall not have them brought in at that rate. I shall send them back to the grower and tell him to grow them cheaper, or grow them elsewhere."

"It is not of currants I speak," Felix replied, tapping the letter against the counter. "It is of love. It is of Miss Cordelia Vane."

Silas stopped. The scales trembled. He looked up, his eyes narrowing to slits. "The Vane girl? The one who lives in the house with the three chimneys and the dog that barks at the moon? She is a good girl, I suppose. But she is above our station. She wears gloves that cost more than my weekly wages. She smells of lavender water and old money. We smell of sugar and dust. There is no meeting place for these scents, Felix."

"Oh, Silas!" Felix cried, throwing his arms wide, nearly knocking over a pyramid of tin boxes. "You are a man of the earth, a man of the soil, but you lack the vision of the spirit! Cordelia is to be my wife. We are to be wed in the spring. And for this, we require... funds."

Silas picked up a tin of sardines and began to polish it with his sleeve. "Funds. Yes. I have heard of funds. I have seen them. I have counted them. I have locked them in the safe under the floorboards where the rats cannot reach them, and where you, unfortunately, cannot reach them either. What do you require, exactly?"

"A dowry," Felix said, with the air of a man requesting a cup of tea. "A modest one. To settle the accounts. To buy the rings. To hire the carriage. To pay for the wedding feast, which must be of a certain standard, Silas, or the Vane family will look at us as if we had invited them to a pigsty. I ask you to sell the shop."

The silence that followed was not the silence of the shop, but the silence of the grave. Silas placed the sardine tin down with a soft *click*. He looked at his brother, really looked at him, for the first time in years. He saw not the dreamer, not the charming fool, but a man who had spent their inheritance on nothing, who had treated their life’s work as a mere stepping stone to a richer man’s daughter.

"Sell the shop?" Silas repeated. His voice was very quiet. It was the quiet of a storm before it breaks, the quiet of a bank vault before it is broken into. "You wish me to sell the shop? The shop that I have kept open since we were boys? The shop that provided the roof over our heads when our father died? The shop that is the only thing in this world that is truly, honestly, undeniably ours?"

"It is merely a shop, Silas," Felix said, though he looked slightly less certain. "It is a means to an end. And the end is happiness. Is not happiness the highest good?"

"Happiness," Silas said, tasting the word as if it were a spoiled apple. "A fine word. A very fine word. It is a word that tastes sweet on the tongue but leaves a bitter aftertaste in the stomach. You speak of happiness as if it were a coin to be spent, Felix. But happiness is not a coin. It is a fire. And you wish to burn down our house to keep warm, while I stand in the street and freeze."

"But the Vane girl—"

"The Vane girl can wait," Silas said, his voice rising now, gaining the heat of indignation. "The Vane girl can wait until you have learned what it is to earn a penny. Until you have stood behind this counter, in this dust, and felt the weight of every shilling. Until you have watched the sun rise over the river and known that the money in the till is not yours to give away, but is the fruit of your labor, drop by drop, day by day. I will not sell the shop. I will not sell the past. I will not sell the future for a wedding ring that will tarnish in a month."

Felix slumped into a chair, his theatrical gravity deflating like a punctured balloon. He looked at his hands, then at the floor. "Then I am undone," he whispered. "I have no money. I have no credit. I am nothing."

"You are not nothing," Silas said, and his voice softened, just for a moment, like the wind easing off a cold night. "You are my brother. And I love you. But love does not mean I must enable your folly. You will work here. You will stand behind this counter. You will learn the price of a pound of butter and the weight of a sack of rice. And when you have learned that, and when you have saved enough to buy a ring with your own sweat, then you may go and court Miss Cordelia. But not before. Not one day before."

Felix looked up, his eyes wide, filled with a mixture of shame and a strange, new clarity. He looked at the dusty shelves, at the jars of preserves, at the brass scales. For the first time, he did not see a landscape of dreams. He saw a life. A hard, dusty, respectable life.

"Very well, Silas," Felix said, straightening his waistcoat. "I will stand behind the counter."

Silas nodded. He picked up his polishing cloth and returned to his sardines. The dust motes danced in the grey light, settling on the tins, on the ledgers, on the faces of the two brothers. The shop stood on, grey-faced and dignified, waiting for the next customer, and for the slow, hard work of change.
