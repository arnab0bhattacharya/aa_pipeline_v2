Lord Eustace Mandeville’s house in Berkeley Square was, as usual, a perfect triumph of bad taste. The air, thick with the scent of hothouse gardenias and the shimmer of a thousand candles, was a palpable thing, a warm, cloying blanket woven of gossip and ambition. Upon this sea of silk and velvet, the Honourable Percival Finch bobbed like a piece of unfortunate flotsam. He was a curate, a man whose spiritual vocation was matched only by his profound social awkwardness, and he had been invited to this reception by a host who, in a moment of singular confusion, had mistaken his modest parish in the East End for the celebrated Explorers’ Club.

“It is an honour, an absolute honour, to have you here, Lord Ashworth,” Lord Eustace had boomed at the door, seizing Percival’s damp hand. “Your account of the Serpent River was the talk of the Athenaeum. The talk!”

Percival, too flustered to correct the egregious error of both his title and his travels, had been swept into the glittering throng, where he now stood miserably beside a potted palm, contemplating the moral perils of a canapé. He was a man of simple sermons and simple pleasures; the complexities of a Mayfair drawing-room were as foreign to him as the jungles he was supposedly so familiar with. It was then that Lady Seraphina Vane, a woman whose beauty was as celebrated as her conversation was lethal, detached herself from a cluster of admirers and drifted towards him. Her gown was the colour of a dying rose, and her eyes held the cool, appraising glitter of amethysts.

“Lord Ashworth,” she breathed, her voice a silken murmur. “I was told I should find the most interesting man in London hiding behind the foliage. They did not exaggerate. Tell me, is it true that the moon in Africa is a different colour entirely?”

Percival’s heart performed a frantic jig against his ribs. To confess the mistake now would be a social annihilation of the most profound order. He would be a laughing-stock, a bumbling curate who had wandered into the wrong party. A strange calm, born of sheer desperation, descended upon him. He looked into Lady Seraphina’s expectant eyes, and a fiction, as wild and improbable as a jungle orchid, began to bloom in his mind.

“It is a different colour, Lady Seraphina,” he heard himself say, his voice a low, resonant baritone he did not recognise as his own. “It is the colour of a bruised plum, and it casts a shadow so deep that a man might lose his own soul in it, should he be careless.”

A small crowd began to gather, drawn by the promise of a good story. Lady Seraphina’s smile widened. “How thrilling! And the stars? Are they as brilliant as our own, or duller, like old silver?”

“Far more brilliant,” Percival continued, the lie gaining a momentum of its own. “They are not mere points of light, but great, silent jewels. I have seen constellations that our astronomers have never named. One, which the natives call the Celestial Serpent, is made of emeralds so pure they seem to drip green fire into the black velvet of the night.”

“Have you truly seen such things, my lord?” asked a portly gentleman with a magnificent moustache.

“I have seen more,” Percival said, warming to his theme. “I have journeyed to the City of Whispers, a place carved from a single block of obsidian in the heart of the continent. There, the people do not speak aloud. Their language is one of gesture and silent thought, and the very stones of their city seem to hum with the echoes of forgotten prayers. To speak there is a profanity.”

He described the great beasts of the jungle, not as mere animals, but as mythological terrors. He spoke of the Maned Lion, whose roar could curdle milk at a distance of ten miles, and the River-Horse, a creature of living bronze that slept beneath the waterfalls of the Serpent River. He painted pictures with words of silks woven from spider-silk and dyed with the juice of rare, nocturnal flowers; of fruits that tasted of honey and sorrow; of a mountain that wept a constant, shimmering waterfall of liquid silver.

The crowd grew. The initial murmurs of curiosity gave way to a rapt, breathless silence. Percival Finch, the shy curate, had vanished. In his place stood Lord Ashworth, the great explorer, a man whose eyes had seen wonders and whose soul was a repository of impossible truths. He spoke with the quiet authority of a man who has wrestled with nature and philosophy in lands beyond the reach of maps. He was, for one glorious, terrifying evening, the most interesting man in London.

As the final words of his grand invention fell into the hushed room, a new figure stepped forward. He was an older man, with a face like a craggy map of some unforgiving terrain and eyes that held the clear, hard light of the Arctic. This was Sir Julian Croft, the chairman of the Royal Geographical Society and the very man whose exploits Percival had been mistaken for.

“A remarkable account, Lord Ashworth,” Sir Julian said, his voice crisp and devoid of flattery. “Truly remarkable. The City of Whispers is a fascinating concept. We have long suspected a highland civilisation of some sort, but to have it confirmed… and the mineral wealth you describe! The silver mountain alone would fund a dozen expeditions.”

Percival felt a cold dread seep into his veins. The fiction was becoming terrifyingly real.

“It is precisely for that reason,” Sir Julian continued, turning to the assembled guests, “that I feel we must act. Lord Ashworth’s knowledge is unparalleled. His courage is legendary. We have the funds, thanks to the munificence of our host, Lord Eustace, who has just pledged a considerable sum to the cause. What we lack is a leader. A man of vision.”

He turned back to Percival, his gaze unwavering. “Lord Ashworth, will you do us the honour of leading a new expedition to the heart of Africa? To find this City of Whispers and chart the Serpent River to its source? The Society, and indeed the Empire, would be eternally in your debt.”

A roar of approval went through the room. Lord Eustace beamed, already seeing his name in the history books as a patron of great discovery. Lady Seraphina looked at Percival with an expression of undisguised admiration, and a promise he was not sure he could interpret.

Percival Finch stood frozen. The applause washed over him like a death sentence. He looked at Sir Julian’s earnest, weathered face, at the hopeful eyes of the crowd, at the magnificent, impossible lie he had spun from thin air. He was invited to lead an expedition to a place that did not exist, to find a city he had invented, and to chart a river he had named. He thought of his small, quiet rectory, of his well-worn Bible and the honest simplicity of his Sunday sermons. It seemed a world away, a lost paradise.

He drew a deep breath, the scent of gardenias and impending doom filling his lungs. He gave a slow, thoughtful nod, the very picture of a great man accepting a great burden.

“Sir Julian,” he said, his voice steady and grave. “To lead men into the unknown is a sacred duty. I accept.”
