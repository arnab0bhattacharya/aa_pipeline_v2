The intelligence of Mr. Fenwick’s return to Ashcombe reached Miss Letitia Wareing not through the medium of a letter, nor from the lips of the gentleman himself, but from the altogether less welcome source of her sister-in-law. Mrs. Wareing, who had never in her life conveyed a piece of information without first weighing it for its capacity to discompose, announced it at the breakfast table with an air of studied indifference.

“I collect your brother is to have his friend at the Grange again,” said she, buttering her toast with a deliberation that Letitia found particularly provoking. “Mr. Fenwick is come down from town at last. I dare say we shall be obliged to have him to dinner.”

Letitia’s cup paused midway to her lips. “Mr. Fenwick? Indeed. I had thought him fixed in London for the whole of the season.”

“So had we all. But it seems he has taken the house at the end of the lane—the one with the mulberry tree—and means to settle among us. Your brother says he is to be made a magistrate.”

“A magistrate!” Letitia set down her cup with a click. “Mr. Fenwick a magistrate! Surely the county can boast a better candidate for the bench than a man who, when last he dined here, mistook the salt cellar for the sugar basin and was three times at a loss for the name of the village he was born in.”

Mrs. Wareing permitted herself a thin smile. “He has been much in London. People improve, you know, by being much in London.”

“They may improve their tailor’s opinion of them,” returned Letitia. “I am not so certain of their understanding.”

It was not, perhaps, the most prudent observation to make before her brother, who entered at that moment with his usual air of good-humoured abstraction, and who had, from his first acquaintance with Mr. Fenwick at Oxford, regarded that gentleman with an esteem that no evidence of folly on either side had ever been able to diminish. Edward Wareing was a man who loved his friends as he loved his old books—not for their elegance, but for the associations of long possession—and he had never quite forgiven his sister for the readiness with which she had, on more than one occasion, supplied the deficiencies of Mr. Fenwick’s conversation with the deficiencies of his character.

“You have heard the news, I suppose,” said he, taking his seat. “Fenwick is come. He is to dine with us on Thursday. I have asked the Harringtons and Miss Ashby to meet him.”

Letitia felt a small, distinct pang. Miss Ashby—Harriet Ashby—was her particular friend; a woman of sense and delicacy, whose good opinion Letitia valued above almost any other, and who had been, for the past twelvemonth, the confidante of every opinion, every jest, every unguarded sally that Letitia’s lively spirits had produced at Mr. Fenwick’s expense. To have Harriet Ashby in the same room as Mr. Fenwick, and to have Mr. Fenwick on his best behaviour, was to have her own past levity brought before her in the most disagreeable light.

“I hope Miss Ashby will be able to come,” said Letitia, with an attempt at carelessness. “She has been much engaged of late.”

“She has promised,” said Edward. “And I am glad of it. Fenwick has been long desirous of making her acquaintance. He has heard me speak of her, you know.”

Letitia said nothing. She had a very clear notion of what Edward had said of Harriet, and a still clearer notion of what she herself had said of Mr. Fenwick; and the two notions did not sit comfortably together.

---

Thursday came, as Thursdays will, and with it Mr. Fenwick, who was not at all the man Letitia remembered. He had grown broader in the shoulders and quieter in his manner; his coat was well-cut, his bow correct, and when he spoke, it was with a deliberation that suggested thought rather than the want of it. He was, in short, a man of thirty-five who had learned something of the world, and who had learned it, Letitia suspected, at some cost.

“Miss Wareing,” said he, as he took his seat beside her at dinner, “I am glad to find you at Ashcombe. I had feared you might be in Bath.”

“I am seldom in Bath,” said Letitia. “I find the waters disagree with my constitution, and the company with my temper.”

“Then I am the more obliged to the waters,” said he, with a smile that was almost grave, “for keeping you here.”

Letitia looked at him sharply. It was not the sort of speech she had been prepared for; it was not, indeed, the sort of speech she had ever heard from him before. She was conscious of a small, uncomfortable sensation, which she could not immediately identify, and which she was obliged to attribute to the unexpectedness of the thing.

Across the table, Harriet Ashby was listening to Edward Wareing with her usual air of gentle attention. She was a woman of twenty-eight, with a countenance more expressive of goodness than of beauty, and a manner that put everyone at their ease except those who had something to conceal. Letitia, watching her, felt the weight of her own concealment press upon her.

“I hear you are to be a magistrate, Mr. Fenwick,” said Mrs. Wareing, with the air of one who has read the peerage and found it wanting. “You will find Ashcombe very dull, I fear, after London.”

“I have had enough of London,” said Mr. Fenwick. “It is a place where a man may be very busy and yet do very little. I hope to do a little more here, and be less busy.”

“A sentiment worthy of a country gentleman,” said Edward, with satisfaction.

“A sentiment worthy of any man who has wasted a few years,” returned Mr. Fenwick. “I have wasted mine, I think, in the pursuit of an importance that does not exist. I am come home to be useful.”

Letitia felt the colour rise to her cheeks. She had said, not three months since, in this very room, and to Harriet Ashby, that Mr. Fenwick was a man who would never be useful to anyone but his tailor. She had said it lightly, as she said everything; she had not thought of it since. Now it seemed to her a cruelty, and a stupid one.

After dinner, when the gentlemen had joined the ladies, and the card tables were set out, Letitia found herself standing by the window with Harriet Ashby, watching the last of the light fade over the mulberry tree at the end of the lane.

“You are very silent to-night,” said Harriet.

“I am thinking,” said Letitia.

“That is a dangerous employment for you. You are generally thinking of something you ought not.”

Letitia smiled, but it was a rueful smile. “I am thinking, Harriet, that I have been very foolish.”

“That is not news.”

“No. But it is news to me.” She paused. “I have said things of Mr. Fenwick—to you—that I ought not to have said. I have laughed at him, and made you laugh at him, and I have done it because it was easy, and because I thought him a man of no consequence. I find I was mistaken in him, and I am ashamed of myself.”

Harriet looked at her for a long moment. “I know what you have said of him,” said she. “I have heard it all. And I have thought, sometimes, that you said it because you were afraid of him.”

“Afraid of him!”

“Afraid of finding him better than you wished him to be. It is a common thing, Letitia. We laugh at what we do not wish to admire.”

Letitia was silent. The justice of the remark was not to be escaped, and she was not a woman who could not bear to be told the truth, though she preferred to tell it herself.

“I must speak to him,” said she at last. “I cannot have him think me a friend when I have been so little of one.”

“He does not think you a friend,” said Harriet. “He thinks you a woman of spirit, which you are, and a woman of sense, which you are also, when you choose to be. He is not a fool, Letitia. He has known you for five years, and he has heard you laugh at him, and he has come back. I leave you to draw the conclusion.”

Letitia drew it, and found it more astonishing than any she had drawn before. She looked across the room at Mr. Fenwick, who was standing by the fire, listening to Edward Wareing with an attention that was neither servile nor indifferent, but simply kind; and she felt, with a suddenness that took her breath, that she had been laughing at a man who had been worth the trouble of knowing, and that the fault was hers, and that it was not, perhaps, too late to mend it.

She went to him before he left, and said, in a low voice, and with a colour that she could not command, “Mr. Fenwick, I have been very unjust to you, and I am sorry for it. I hope you will allow me to begin again.”

Mr. Fenwick looked at her with an expression that was not surprise, but something more like relief. “Miss Wareing,” said he, “I have been waiting five years to hear you say something of the kind. I hope you will not think me impertinent if I say that I am very glad to hear it.”

“I think you impertinent,” said Letitia, recovering herself, “if you say nothing more.”

“Then I shall say more,” said he, “when I have the honour of waiting upon you to-morrow.”

And Letitia, who had never in her life been at a loss for a reply, found that she had nothing to say at all.
