The wind from Blackstone Edge seldom ceased its assault upon the parsonage walls. It came howling across the heather, bearing the cold baptism of the high Pennine peaks, and rattled the leaded casements with a fury that seemed almost personal. To me, shut up in the narrow back-parlour with my slate and my grammar, grading the tedious exercises of the village children, that wind was the very voice of my imprisonment. I was twenty-eight; I was plain; I was, by the unyielding decree of circumstance, the sole stay and prop of a frail household. My sister Clara, two years my junior, lay on the sofa by the fire, wrapped in her grey shawl, her thin hands folded over a scrap of paper. She was an invalid, and a secret poet. I had given up my own half-formed scribblings long ago, burying them in the bottom of my work-box, when our father’s failing sight and the stark demands of the parish school demanded a sacrifice. I had made it. I thought I had made it cheerfully.

Mr. Lockwood, the critic from London, had arrived that afternoon, driven through the miry lanes by a reluctant carrier, to inspect our father’s antiquarian library. He was a sharp-featured, restless man, with eyes that pierced through the gloom of our low-ceilinged rooms. Father had been called to the bedside of a dying parishioner, and I had been left to serve the visitor his tea. Clara, roused by the unusual presence of a stranger, had sat up, and in the shifting of cushions, a few loose leaves of her manuscript had fallen, fluttering to the rug. I had stooped instantly to gather them, my face burning, for I knew what they were. I meant to thrust them into my pocket, to hide her foolishness from the probing gaze of a man of letters. But Mr. Lockwood was quicker.

"Pray, do not conceal them," he said, his tone abruptly altering from polite indifference to a sudden, keen alertness. "What is this verse? Let me see."

I stood paralyzed, my hand lingering on the latch of the door. "They are nothing, sir. Mere idle scratching. She is not well; it distracts her."

But he had already taken the crumpled pages. He read them standing by the dying fire, his profile sharp against the crimson glow of the coals. The silence in the room grew oppressive. I watched him, my heart beating with a strange, suffocating violence. Clara had sunk back against her pillows, her cheeks flushed with the feverish hue that always heralded her worst spasms, her great dark eyes fixed upon him with a look of terrified hope. He read for what seemed an hour, though it was barely five minutes. When he raised his head, his gaze went straight past me, straight to the quiet, shrinking girl on the sofa.

"This is astonishing," he said, and his voice was no longer languid; it vibrated with a fierce, genuine emotion. "This is the true voice of the moors. There is a fire here, a grasp of the sublime and the terrible, that I have not encountered in any living writer. You have written these, Miss Clara?"

She murmured a faint, frightened affirmative.

"You must publish them," he continued, stepping closer to her, entirely oblivious to my presence in the shadow of the doorway. "You have a gift that is not yours to hide. It is a duty. This stanza—'The raven plucks the thorn / And bleeds upon the snow / So doth the spirit mourn / When the worldly winds blow'—it has the ring of prophecy. It is Scripture and nature wedded in a single breath."

I saw Clara’s eyes fill with tears. I saw a light, a radiant, unearthly light, break over her pale features, transforming her from a suffering dependent into a being touched by divine fire. And at that precise moment, a pang, sharp, black, and utterly venomous, shot through my own breast.

It was jealousy. I knew it the instant I felt it; I named it with a ruthless, inward condemnation, yet I could not quell it. Why her? Why not me? I had scribbbled the same dark verses in my youth; I had felt the same fierce promptings of the soul, the same desire to carve my name into the hard rock of the world. But I had been strong; I had been the one to toil, to teach, to cook, to nurse. I had ground my spirit into the dust of duty, and now the laurel was to be placed on the brow of the weak, the sheltered, the helpless. The injustice of Providence stung me like a physical lash. I was ashamed. I felt the heat of my own unworthiness rise to my very brow. I was a wretch, a Pharisee, envying the cripple her crutch.

"I am glad," I said, and my voice sounded strange and hard in my own ears, emerging from a throat constricted by suppressed passion. "I am glad, Clara, that your amusement has found favor."

Mr. Lockwood turned, as if suddenly remembering my existence. "Amusement, Miss Ellerton? It is no mere amusement. It is genius. And you, do you not write?"

"I write bills for the baker," I said, with a sudden, bitter flash. "And sums for the village idiots. I have no time for genius."

He looked at me a moment, a slight contraction of his brow betraying his surprise at my vehemence. I did not care. I was possessed by a demon of unrest. I turned and left the room, shutting the door behind me with a force that bordered on violence.

I fled to the kitchen, the cold, stone-floored room that smelled of damp and boiled mutton. I stood by the window, pressing my forehead against the freezing glass. Outside, the rain had returned, lashing the dark yard, beating down the late autumn grass. The sky was a vault of iron, unbroken by a single star. I breathed heavily, battling the tempest within. What was I? A drudge, a cipher. My youth was passing, consumed by the unending round of petty tasks. I had thought I was resigned. I had thought I had made my peace with the altar of sacrifice. But the peace had been a lie, a hollow crust over a volcano of ambition and thwarted desire.

I thought of the text Father had read at morning prayers: "The last shall be first, and the first last." I had always taken it as a promise of heavenly recompense. Now it felt like a mockery. I was the first in labor, the last in reward. Clara would be praised; Clara would be remembered; Clara would live in the minds of men, while I would moulder in the churchyard beside the parsonage, unwept, unsung, a mere name on a crumbling stone.

The door creaked behind me. I did not turn. I expected the heavy tread of our old servant, come to scold me for leaving the visitor. But the step that crossed the flags was light and hesitant. I felt a thin hand slip into mine. It was Clara.

"Esther," she whispered, her voice trembling. "Esther, do not be angry."

"I am not angry," I said, but my tones were harsh, and I did not look at her.

"You are," she insisted, with a gentle, penetrating sorrow that cut me to the quick. "I saw your face. I know what you felt. Oh, Esther, I know what you have given up! I know what it costs you to see me praised for a thing that came to me in the quiet of my illness, while you toil from dawn to dusk. But I never meant to take anything from you. These verses—they are yours as much as mine. They are born of your endurance, of your strength. I could not have written them if you had not kept me alive."

I turned at last, my eyes blinding with sudden, hot tears. She stood there, a frail, pale phantom in the gloom, looking up at me with a love so deep, so unselfish, that it shamed the venomous serpent of my jealousy back into its den. I had been wrong. I had been wickedly, basely wrong. She was not my rival; she was my other self, the voice of the imprisoned soul I had buried. Through her weakness, my own strength had found expression.

"Clara," I said, and my voice broke. I gathered her thin form into my arms, holding her close, feeling the erratic beating of her heart against my own. "Forgive me. I am proud of you. I am proud. It is only—only—"

"I know," she murmured. "I know."

The wind shrieked outside, rattling the casement, but in the dark kitchen, a sudden warmth seemed to spring up between us, a glow of reconciliation that was deeper than praise, stronger than fame. I had lost my dream, but in her, it lived. And perhaps, in the eyes of Heaven, that was the same thing.
