The noise of it all was the first thing—the great, indiscriminate roar of wheels on stone that seemed not to stop at the walls of the hotel but to pass through them, carrying on its crest the shrieks of motor horns and the clatter of hooves, so that Clara, waking in the hot Paris morning, felt she was suspended not in a room but in the very throat of the city. She lay still, watching the pale green silk of the lampshade catch a slant of sun, and thought how strange it was that Stephen, in the next room, was already up and brushing his hair, parting it with that meticulous, medical severity which meant he was preparing to listen, to take notes, to be the Doctor. She must get up; she must put on the grey foulard that laced at the throat, because at nine they were to attend the opening address, and Stephen could not be expected to find his own collar-studs in a foreign city, though he had found them unaided for fifteen years in the house at Aysgarth.

She rose, and crossed to the window, pressing her face against the glass. The street below was a torrent of light and movement—carts piled with gladioli, their petals shaking; women in dark shawls carrying baskets of bread; and over all, a haze of dust that turned the sun to a dull gold, blurring the edges of things. It was so different from the moors, where the light was hard and clean and fell in sharp planes across the heather. Here everything bled into everything else, colours running like wet paint, and she felt a sudden, unreasonable excitement, as if the city had offered her something—she could not say what—a promise, a possibility, which she must not look at directly for fear it vanished.

They went down to breakfast in the salon rouge, where the mirrors reflected mirrors, and the air smelled of coffee and hot rolls and something else, something heavy and floral, which Clara thought might be the scent of the thick red curtains that hung in folds at the windows. Stephen sat down opposite her and unfolded his napkin with the air of a man performing a ritual. He was nervous, she could see; his eyes moved restlessly over the room, noting the other delegates, the French doctors with their quick gestures and dark beards, the Germans with their spectacles and heavy boots, and she felt a rush of tenderness for him, because she knew that in Aysgarth he was a king, but here he was only one of hundreds, a country doctor with a country doctor's hesitancies, and it cost him something to sit there and pretend he was not afraid.

"You must eat, Stephen," she said, and her voice surprised her, for it had a quality she did not recognise—a firmness, a brightness, as if the noise and the mirrors had given it back to her altered.

He looked up, and for a moment seemed about to speak, then turned to the waiter and asked, in his careful, textbook French, for more butter. The waiter bowed, and Clara saw that he took Stephen for what he was—an Englishman, a provincial, a man who pronounced his *r's* in his throat as if they were fishbones he was trying to swallow. She felt a sudden shame, and then a fierce pride, and then nothing but a desire to protect him from the waiter's faint, professional smile.

At the congress, in the great amphitheatre of the Sorbonne, the heat was suffocating. The speakers rose and fell like waves, their voices merging into a continuous murmur through which occasional words emerged—*glycogène*, *tissu*, *bacille*—floating up to the high ceiling where they dissolved into the general hum. Clara sat beside Stephen in the second row, her hands folded in her lap, and listened, or half-listened, letting the French wash over her in rhythms that were almost music, so that she found herself attending not to the meaning but to the cadence, the rise and fall, the way certain phrases repeated like motifs in a sonata. Stephen wrote in his notebook with a pencil that squeaked, and she saw that his hand was not quite steady.

Afterwards, at the reception in the Salle des Professeurs, it began. A tall Frenchman with a white waistcoat and a decoration in his lapel approached them, holding out his hand to Stephen, and then, turning to Clara, bowed slightly and said something she did not entirely catch, but she heard the word *madame*, and understood that he had taken her for Stephen's wife. She opened her mouth to correct him, but Stephen was already shaking the man's hand, and saying *ma sœur* in a voice that was drowned by the general noise, and the Frenchman, smiling, nodding, evidently not hearing, turned to Clara again and asked her, in that rapid, continuous French which she had to piece together word by word, whether she found Paris agreeable, whether the climate was not too humid for an English constitution, whether she had visited the Jardin des Plantes.

And she answered. She answered with a fluency that astonished her, as if the city had unlocked something in her that had been sealed for years—since their parents died, since she had come back to Aysgarth to keep house for Stephen and had slowly, imperceptibly, stopped saying things that were her own, and started saying things that were his, or the house's, or the district's, things about dinners and boilers and the price of milk and whether Mrs. Fawcett's leg was healing. Now, in this hot room, with the noise of voices and the clink of glasses, she found herself speaking of the light in Paris, how it differed from the light in Yorkshire, how it seemed to her to make everything more fluid, more provisional, and the Frenchman listened, truly listened, with his head on one side, and said *précisément, madame*, and she felt something open in her chest, a window long shut, through which air rushed in.

She looked at Stephen, but he was talking to a German doctor about the treatment of rheumatic fever, and did not notice. She thought: he does not see. He has never seen. Not because he is cruel, or unkind, but because in Aysgarth I am the one who remembers the milk, and here I am someone else, someone who might have been— She stopped the thought, for it frightened her, this sense of a self that had been living underground, pushing up now through the Parisian pavement, through the very stones.

At dinner that evening, in a restaurant on the Boulevard Saint-Germain where the candles were reflected in dark wood and the wine was the colour of light through stained glass, it happened again. The professor from Lyon, who sat on her right, asked her opinion on the education of women, and she spoke, and he listened, and Stephen, across the table, looked up once from his plate with an expression she could not read—surprise, perhaps, or something faintly uneasy, as if he had discovered a stranger in the place where his sister should have been. She met his eyes, and for a moment they sat in silence, and the noise of the restaurant roared around them like water, and she thought: I am thirty-two years old, and I have kept house for fifteen years, and no one at home has ever asked me what I think.

Later, in the hotel room, she stood at the window again and looked out at the city. The street lamps were lit, and their reflections ran in long streaks across the wet pavement, for it had rained, and the sky was the colour of a bruise, purple and yellow, with clouds moving swiftly across it. Somewhere a clock struck eleven, and the sound was muffled, swallowed by the city's breath. She unbuttoned the grey foulard and hung it on the back of the chair, and saw in the mirror that her face was flushed, her eyes bright, and she looked, she thought, like someone she had not yet become.

In the next room Stephen coughed, and she heard him turn the pages of his notebook, reviewing the day's lectures, and she felt the old pull of duty, the old gravity of Aysgarth drawing her back. But it was weaker now, thinner, like a thread stretched to breaking, and she held the moment to her—the window, the lamps, the wet streets, the memory of the Frenchman's bow, the professor's attention—as one holds a match in a draught, cupping it with both hands, breathing softly, unwilling to let it go.
