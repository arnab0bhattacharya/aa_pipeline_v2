If any one of the benevolent gentlemen who sat upon the board of management for the Clinkerbottle Night-School in the smoky town of Muddleton had been told that learning was a dangerous explosive, they would have nodded their heads, adjusted their stiff white cravats, and instantly ordered it to be put out. The Clinkerbottle Mill, which ground out calico and coughs in equal measure, had graciously provided a damp, soot-stained cellar beneath the counting-house for the moral improvement of its hands. And what a cellar it was! The walls wept a perpetual tepid moisture, as if the very bricks were perspiring from the labour of holding up the ponderous mass of machinery overhead. The flickering gas-jets cast shadows that danced a sort of mechanical jig upon the low, vaulted ceiling, making the place look for all the world like a gloomy grotto populated by the ghosts of half-starved arithmetic.

It was into this cheerful retreat that Mr. Amos Tallow, the night-school master, descended on a particularly damp Tuesday evening in November, carrying under his arm a leather satchel that had seen better days, and bearing in his heart a load of oppressive intelligence. Mr. Tallow was a thin, pale man, with a remarkably high forehead and a habit of clearing his throat when anxious, as if he were trying to dislodge an invisible obstruction from his windpipe. He wore spectacles that were perpetually sliding down his nose, giving him the appearance of a benevolent grasshopper peering over a pair of tiny fences.

Now, the oppressive intelligence Mr. Tallow carried was this: he had that very afternoon been summoned before Mr. Grindstone and Mr. Bluster, the twin titans of the Clinkerbottle Mill. Mr. Grindstone was a gentleman with a face like a red-hot poker, who seemed to speak entirely in the imperative mood and viewed all human sentiment as a dangerous form of insubordination. Mr. Mr. Bluster, on the other hand, was a large, puffing man, who breathed heavily through his nose and believed that the sum of human happiness was contained within a shilling piece. They had informed Mr. Tallow, with much thumping of the counting-house table, that the night-school was consuming too much coal-gas, and that henceforth, the curriculum must be strictly confined to Arithmetic and the Catechism.

"Arithmetic, Mr. Tallow!" Mr. Grindstone had roared, striking his ledger with a fist resembling a leg of mutton. "Pounds, shillings, and pence! The multiplication-table! Let them know their place, and let them know their sums! As for the Catechism, let them learn their duty to their betters, and keep the Sabbath holy, and no nonsense!"

"Quite so, quite so," Mr. Bluster had wheezed, mopping his purple brow with a yellow silk handkerchief. "No history, no geography, and above all, Mr. Tallow, no poetry! Poetry makes them discontented with their porridge. Arithmetic makes them calculating. The Catechism makes them submissive. Stick to the figures and the formula, sir, or we shut up the cellar!"

Mr. Tallow, feeling very much like a small boat in a tempest, had bowed and scraped and promised obedience. He had taken his leave, coughing nervously, and had walked the entire length of Muddleton High Street in a state of profoundest misery. For Mr. Tallow loved poetry. He loved the rolling cadences of Mr. Shakespeare, the pastoral sweetness of Mr. Wordsworth, and the martial tramp of Lord Macaulay’s Lays. And he knew, with a sinking certainty, that his scholars loved it too.

He reached his desk, a rickety structure that groaned at every touch as if complaining of the ague, and struck the little hand-bell. The scholars, who had been wrestling with slates and noisy in the manner of a cage of starlings, settled into their seats. They were a motley crew: pale-faced children of twelve, who should have been in bed; gaunt women with yarn in their hair; and grown men, whose hands were so blackened with mill-dust that the chalk seemed to fly from their fingers like snowflakes from a shovel.

Among these grown men was one Ezekiel Grub. He was a boiler-tender by trade, a man of prodigious strength and formidable breadth, with a beard like a thicket of iron wire and eyebrows that met in the middle like two black caterpillars embracing. His hands were a landscape of burns and calluses, and he smelt powerfully of coal-smoke and tallow, which was perhaps why he and Mr. Tallow had always felt a sort of sympathetic kinship. Ezekiel was not a man of many words; his usual conversation consisted of grunts, nods, and the occasional "Aye," delivered with the gravity of a judge passing sentence. But he was the star scholar of the night-school. He could add a column of figures with terrifying rapidity, and he knew the Catechism so thoroughly that he could recite it backwards in his sleep—a feat which, while perhaps not practically useful, was certainly a testament to his memory.

This evening, however, Ezekiel wore an expression of unusual expectancy. His small, sharp eyes, which twinkled in his soot-blackened face like two bits of mica in a lump of coal, were fixed upon Mr. Tallow with an intensity that was almost alarming. When the rest of the class had bent their heads to their slates, Ezekiel lingered by the master's desk.

"Mr. Tallow," he said, in a voice that sounded as though it were being dragged up from the bottom of a coal-pit. "I’ve a favour to ask, like."

Mr. Tallow, who was nervously sorting through a pile of dog-eared arithmetic primers, looked up with a start. "A favour, Ezekiel? What manner of favour?"

Ezekiel shifted his weight from one colossal foot to the other, producing a sound like the grinding of millstones. "It’s about the reading, sir. The reading-book. We’ve done the sums, and I’ve got the Catechism by heart, and I’m thankful for it. But I’ve heard tell, sir, that there’s books of a different sort. Books that don’t deal in sums nor sins. Books that—" he paused, and his great black face took on a curious, almost tender expression— "books that sing, sir."

Mr. Tallow’s heart gave a violent thump and retreated into his boots. "Sing, Ezekiel?"

"Aye, sir. Poetry, they call it. I heard the young master reading it aloud from the parlor window t'other Sunday. It sounded like the wind in the mill-chimneys, only sweeter. And I thought to myself, 'Ezekiel,' I thought, 'if I could read that, I’d be the happiest boiler-tender in all Muddleton.' So I was wondering, sir, if you might teach me. Just a bit. Just a line or two. On the sly, like."

Mr. Tallow looked at Ezekiel. He looked at the soot-stained ceiling. He looked at the gas-jet, which hissed and spluttered like an angry gander. He thought of Mr. Grindstone and Mr. Bluster, and he thought of the leg-of-mutton fist. And then he thought of Ezekiel, a man who spent his days shovelling coal into the fiery maw of a boiler, asking to be taught to read poetry.

A rebellion began to stir in Mr. Tallow’s bosom. It was a small rebellion, to be sure, and a very timid one, but it was there. He cleared his throat, adjusted his spectacles, and spoke in a whisper.

"Ezekiel," he said, "it is against the express orders of the board. If Mr. Grindstone knew, I should be dismissed, and you would be sent back to the boilers without so much as a by-your-leave."

Ezekiel nodded gloomily. "I know it, sir."

"But," said Mr. Tallow, and his voice trembled slightly, "I will do it."

Ezekiel’s face broke into a smile so wide and so unexpected that it seemed to split his countenance in twain, like the breaking of a black pudding. "You will, sir?"

"I will," said Mr. Tallow, with a desperate valour he did not know he possessed. "But we must be cautious. We must be secret. We must be as sly as a family of mice in a cat’s cradle. Come to me after the others have gone, and I will lend you a book. It is a book of poems by a Mr. William Wordsworth, who loved the poor and the simple, and who wrote of daffodils and clouds and the quiet beauty of the world."

Ezekiel looked somewhat bewildered. "Daffodils, sir?"

"Yes, daffodils. Yellow flowers, Ezekiel, that dance in the breeze. You may not have seen them in Muddleton, but they exist, I assure you, and they are very beautiful."

Ezekiel considered this for a moment, his brow furrowed in thought. "I think I saw a daffodil once," he said, slowly. "In a jug on the publican’s bar at the Pig and Whistle. I didn’t know it could dance."

"It dances in the poem, at any rate," said Mr. Tallow, "and that is what matters. Now, back to your slate, before Mr. Grindstone’s ghost appears and flogs us both."

Ezekiel retreated to his seat with a tread that made the cellar echo. And Mr. Tallow, turning back to his arithmetic primers, felt a warmth in his chest that no amount of coal-gas or boiler-fires could produce. He was a conspirator, a rebel, a subversive element in the body politic of Muddleton. He was teaching a boiler-tender to read poetry. And he felt, for the first time in his meek and uneventful life, that he was doing something genuinely, magnificently dangerous.

The clock on the mantelpiece ticked on, indifferent to the passions of men, and the gas-jet hissed its eternal refrain. And in the corner, Ezekiel Grub, boiler-tender and star scholar, bent over his slate with a new light in his eyes, scrawling not sums, but a secret, triumphant, and entirely unauthorized word: Daffodil.
