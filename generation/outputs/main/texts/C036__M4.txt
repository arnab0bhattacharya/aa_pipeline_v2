The summons had come at breakfast, slipped under my door on a folded square of paper that smelled faintly of carbolic soap. *Mademoiselle Ashworth. The study. Nine o'clock.* I had eaten nothing. The coffee in the refectory had tasted of rust, and the bread had sat in my throat like a stone.

The study was on the second floor, a room I had entered only twice before, both times to be congratulated on the comportment of my girls at the winter recital. Now the door stood open, and within I saw the headmistress, Madame Vervloet, seated behind her desk like a magistrate, her grey hair parted with a severity that seemed to condemn me before I spoke. Beside her, in the chair reserved for visitors, sat Sister Agnes, the school's directress of studies, her hands folded over her breviary. And standing by the window, her back to me, was the girl.

"Come in, mademoiselle," said Madame Vervloet. Her English was careful, almost ceremonial. "Close the door."

I did so. The room smelled of ink and old paper and the beeswax the servants used on the parquet. The girl turned. Elodie van der Meersch. Seventeen years old, with the pale, composed face of a girl who has never in her life been disbelieved. She was one of my best pupils in composition, though her translations had always struck me as too smooth, as though she had copied them from somewhere.

"Mademoiselle Ashworth," said Madame Vervloet, "Elodie has made a serious accusation against you. I wish you to hear it in her own words, and then I wish you to answer."

"I did not—" I began, but the headmistress raised one finger.

"Let her speak."

Elodie's voice was clear and unhurried, as though she were reciting a lesson she had learned well. "On Tuesday last, I left my writing case in the classroom after the afternoon lesson. It contained letters from my mother. When I returned for it, Mademoiselle was alone in the room, and my case was open on her desk. The letters were in her hand." She paused, and her gaze met mine without a flicker. "She had been reading them."

"That is not true," I said. My voice came out higher than I intended. "I was at my desk correcting exercises. The case was brought to me by another pupil—Marie-Claire—who found it in the corridor. I did not open it. I did not read anything."

"Marie-Claire has been questioned," said Sister Agnes, without looking up from her breviary. "She recalls only that she handed the case to you. She did not see whether it was open or closed."

"Because it was closed," I said. "I set it aside. I did not touch the latch."

Madame Vervloet regarded me with a patience that was worse than anger. "Mademoiselle Ashworth, you have been with us two years. Your references were excellent. But you must understand that the privacy of a pupil's correspondence is sacred. The parents of this school entrust us with their daughters. If a teacher were to read a girl's letters—letters from her mother, no less—it would be a grave breach. A dismissal matter."

"I understand that," I said. "I am telling you I did not do it."

"Elodie is a truthful girl," said the headmistress. "She has never been known to lie. Why would she invent such a thing?"

I looked at Elodie. She was watching me with an expression I could not read—not triumph, not malice, but something quieter, a kind of patient certainty, as though she were waiting for a clock to strike. And I understood, in that moment, that she had chosen me precisely because I was the easiest to choose. I was the English teacher, the foreigner, the one whose French was slow and whose Flemish was nonexistent, the one who took her meals alone and had no friend in the city to speak for her. I had no allies among the staff. I had no one to say, *She is not that kind of woman.*

"I cannot prove I did not read them," I said. "But I can tell you what I was doing. I was correcting the fourth form's essays on *Le Cid*. I had marked eleven of them. The twelfth was Elodie's own. I had written a comment in the margin about her use of the subjunctive. If you look at the papers on my desk, you will find them exactly so."

"The papers may be arranged to suit you," said Sister Agnes.

"Then ask the girls," I said. "Ask the fourth form whether I returned their essays on Tuesday. Ask whether I spoke to them about the subjunctive. I did. I remember because Elodie's essay was the best, and I told her so."

Elodie's composure wavered, just slightly. "Mademoiselle often praises my work," she said. "That proves nothing."

"No," I agreed. "It proves nothing. But it is true."

There was a silence. Outside, in the courtyard, I could hear the younger girls at their morning recreation, their voices rising and falling like birds. I thought of my room in the east wing, the narrow bed, the window that looked out onto a wall. I thought of the letter I had received from my mother the week before, in which she had asked whether I was happy, and I had written back that I was. I wondered what she would say if I came home in disgrace. I wondered whether I would be able to explain it in a language she understood.

"Mademoiselle," said Madame Vervloet, and her voice had softened, which frightened me more than her severity. "I do not wish to dismiss you. You are a good teacher. But I cannot have a teacher in my school whom a pupil accuses of such a thing and who cannot answer the charge. Do you understand? It is not a question of what I believe. It is a question of what I can defend."

"Then let me defend myself," I said. "Give me a week. Let me speak to the girls. Let me find out who else handled the case. Let me—"

"You may have until Friday," said the headmistress. "If by Friday you have not produced a satisfactory explanation, I shall have no choice but to ask for your resignation."

I nodded. My hands were trembling, so I held them behind my back.

As I turned to go, Elodie spoke again. "Mademoiselle," she said, and her voice was almost kind, "I am sorry. I truly am. But I know what I saw."

I looked at her. She was seventeen, and she was frightened, I realized—frightened of something I could not see, frightened perhaps of her own mother's letters, of what they might contain, of the fact that she had left them where anyone might find them. It was easier to accuse me than to admit she had been careless. It was easier to make me the villain than to face whatever it was she had been trying to hide.

"I know what you saw," I said. "You saw a foreign woman alone in a classroom with your writing case. And you made a story to fit it. I understand. But I did not read your letters, Elodie. I would not."

She said nothing. Her eyes dropped to the floor.

I left the study and walked down the corridor, past the portraits of old headmistresses and the glass cases of dried flowers, and out into the courtyard where the girls were playing. The sun was thin and white, the kind of sun that gives no warmth. I stood there for a moment, watching them, and then I went to my classroom to gather the fourth form's essays, because I had until Friday, and I intended to use every hour of it.
