The invitation had been a small, stiff card of cream-coloured stock, and its very brevity was the source of its power. It summoned one to dine at the house of Lady Cordelia Ashworth in Grosvenor Square on the twenty-first of June, at eight o’clock precisely. No mention of a motive, no hint of the other guests. To receive one was to be marked as someone of consequence; to speak of having received one was to be banished from the table. Lady Ashworth, a widow of formidable intellect and even more formidable ambition, had conceived a social experiment of exquisite cruelty: she would gather a collection of brilliant strangers and watch the sparks fly. The guest list was her masterpiece. A bishop known for his scholarly remove from the grime of the world, an actress celebrated for her tragic roles and tempestuous off-stage life, an explorer who had returned from the Congo with a limp and a haunted look, and a young poet whose first volume, ‘The Decadent’s Confession,’ had been banned from every respectable lending library in London. They were, to a soul, strangers to one another.

The Bishop of Thatcham, a man named Alistair Finch, arrived first, his gait slow and deliberate, as if he were measuring the Aubusson carpet for a new nave. He was followed shortly by Miss Isabella Vance, the actress, who swept in on a cloud of gardenia perfume, her eyes scanning the room not for acquaintances but for an audience. The explorer, Mr. Silas Croft, came next, his sun-scorched face a stark contrast to the soft candlelight, his hand gripping a silver-topped cane. Last was the poet, Julian Blackwood, who looked barely old enough to shave, his face pale and his eyes burning with a nervous, defiant energy.

Lady Ashworth, a vision in dove-grey silk, orchestrated the introductions with the smooth precision of a conductor. “Bishop Finch, may I present Miss Vance? Miss Vance, Mr. Croft, whose travels in Africa have quite captivated London. And Mr. Blackwood, our poet.” The bows and curtsies were shallow, the assessments immediate and unspoken. The Bishop saw a painted woman of the stage; the actress saw a dusty man of the cloth; the explorer saw a callow boy playing at world-weariness; the poet saw three relics of a dying age. Lady Ashworth observed it all with a faint, satisfied smile.

The first course, a delicate consommé, was served in the dining room, a grand space of dark panelling and ancestral portraits. For a time, the conversation was a cautious minuet. The Bishop spoke of the weather, the actress of the new Shaw play, the explorer of the difficulty of obtaining a decent cup of tea in Mombasa. Julian Blackwood said little, merely toying with his spoon and watching the others with a gaze that was both unnerving and perceptive. It was Lady Ashworth who, with the air of a woman tossing a pebble into a still pond, introduced the first stone. “Mr. Croft,” she said, her voice cutting through the polite murmur, “I read in The Times that you are to be honoured for your services to geography. Such a singular life. Were you never afraid, so far from civilisation?”

Croft’s jaw tightened. He dabbed his lips with his napkin. “Fear, Lady Ashworth, is a luxury for those who have the time to indulge it. In the jungle, one has only instinct. And regret.” He glanced involuntarily at his leg, then at the actress. “One learns the true cost of things.”

The Bishop, sensing a turn towards the macabre, sought to steer the conversation. “Indeed. The cost of a soul, for instance. A far more perilous journey than any across a continent, I should think.” He smiled benignly at Julian. “Mr. Blackwood, your book has caused quite a stir. I confess I have not read it—my duties leave little time for modern verse—but I am told it is… passionate.”

Julian’s eyes flashed. “Passion, Bishop, is merely truth that has not yet been tidied up by the Church. My book is a confession, and all confessions are, by their nature, passionate.”

Miss Vance, who had been studying the poet with a predator’s focus, leaned forward. “A confession implies a sin, Mr. Blackwood. What, pray, is yours? The critics were most unkind, but they were also uncommonly vague.”

“The sin, Miss Vance,” Julian replied, his voice dropping, “is to have seen the world as it is, and not as we pretend it to be. It is to have written of the decay beneath the gilt.”

The second course, a consommé of turtle, was cleared away, replaced by poached salmon. The air was thick with unspoken challenges. It was then that the first secret, fragile and explosive, detonated. The Bishop, in an attempt to regain his authority, turned to Miss Vance. “I understand you are to play Lady Macbeth next season. A demanding role. I saw the great Mrs. Siddons in the part as a boy. She was… terrifying.”

Isabella Vance’s smile was a thin, sharp line. “I am sure she was. But I do not model my art on the past. I model it on life. On the raw, ugly truth of it.” She paused, allowing the silence to stretch. “For instance, Bishop, I am currently researching the role of a woman who is wrongly accused. It is astonishing what one learns about the men who sit in judgment.” Her gaze was fixed on him, cold and knowing. “I recently had cause to visit the diocesan archive at Thatcham. A fascinating place. The records of the ecclesiastical courts from forty years ago make for chilling reading. So many ruined lives. So many secrets, hidden behind a collar.”

The Bishop’s benign smile froze. His hand, reaching for his wine glass, trembled slightly. He saw it then, in her eyes: she knew. She knew about the young curate, the hasty transfer to a parish in the Outer Hebrides, the sealed file. He had thought the matter buried deeper than memory. He cleared his throat, the sound a dry rasp. “The past… is a foreign country. They do things differently there.”

“Oh, I disagree,” Miss Vance purred. “The past is never dead. It is not even past. Isn’t that right, Mr. Croft?”

The explorer started, as if stung. He had been staring at the actress, his face a mask of horror. He knew her now. Not from the stage, but from a fever-dream in a mud hut in the Congo. She was the spitting image of the wife of the Belgian trader, a woman he had seen only once, through the smoke of a burning village, her face a portrait of terror as she was dragged away. He had done nothing. He had hidden. The shame, the cowardice, had been the true source of his limp, a wound to his soul that no amount of medals could heal. “You are from… you have people in Antwerp,” he stammered, the words a choked accusation.

Isabella’s eyes widened for a fraction of a second, then narrowed. She had him. The explorer’s secret was not one of geography, but of morality. She had heard whispers of the only survivor of a particularly brutal expedition, a man who had returned alone. She had not expected to find him here, so decorated and respectable. “My mother was Belgian,” she said softly, a statement that was also a weapon. “She told me stories.”

The table was a minefield. Lady Ashworth watched, her eyes gleaming. The Bishop was pale, the explorer sweating. But it was Julian Blackwood who, in his quiet, precise voice, lit the final fuse. He had been observing them all, piecing together the fragments of their terror. He looked at the Bishop, then at Miss Vance. “It is curious,” he said, as if to himself, “how a single act of cowardice can echo for decades. The curate who was sent away… he was a poet, was he not? He wrote a letter, a confession of his own, before he took his own life in that cold, grey place. I found it in a collection of ephemera I purchased for my research. A sad, beautiful, damning letter. He named the man who had ruined him. A man now a bishop.”

All eyes turned to Alistair Finch. He was no longer the Prince of the Church, but a man exposed, his mitre knocked askew. He opened his mouth, but no sound came out.

Julian then turned his gaze to Silas Croft. “And you, sir. You wear your medal for valour, but your soul wears a different one. A medal of silence. I have read your published journal. The gaps are as telling as the words. You were at the village of Nkole. The only European to leave it alive. The only one who did not try to save the woman they took. My book is called a scandal, but it is merely a mirror. You are all characters in a story far more decadent than any I could invent.”

The silence that followed was absolute, broken only by the crackle of a log in the fireplace. The actress stared at the bishop, her triumph curdled into a strange pity. The bishop stared at his plate, his world collapsed. The explorer stared at his own hands, seeing only blood he had not spilled. They had come as strangers, a collection of celebrated individuals. They were leaving as something else entirely: a group of broken people, bound together by the terrible, liberating truth. Lady Ashworth, the architect of this ruin, took a delicate sip of her wine. The season’s most exclusive dinner was, by any measure, a triumph.
