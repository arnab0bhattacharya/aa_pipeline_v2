There are epochs in the life of the soul, wherein the boundaries of our earthly suffering seem to contract, closing round us like the walls of a living sepulchre, until the breath fails, and the spirit sinks beneath the accumulated weight of its own agonising consciousness. Such, alas! was the fatal epoch which saw me, a solitary and devoted wanderer, confined within the echoing, metallic wards of the St. George’s Infirmary, in that late, disastrous autumn of our twenty-first century. The pestilence, whose name no science could yet define, had swept over London with a desolation unparalleled, leaving our shattered society a mere wreck of its former pride. I, Clara, a nurse by vocation, bound by the sacred vows of my melancholy office to administer such solace as mortal skill might afford, found myself immured in a vast charnel-house, where the groans of the dying formed a ceaseless, terrible anthem to the triumph of Death. The atmosphere, heavy with the fumes of antiseptic mists and the acuter, suffocating damp of mortality, seemed to weigh upon my very soul; and the glare of the synthetic lights, unsoftened by the pallid, sunless sky without, cast a ghastly, unnatural hue upon the pallid faces of the sufferers, whose eyes, glazed with the fever of approaching dissolution, turned towards me in speechless, unavailing agony. 

It was amidst this scene of unutterable desolation, in a remote corner of the ward where the shadows lay thickest, that I first beheld him—Edgar Valancy. He had been conveyed to us from the ruined outskirts of the southern banks, borne upon a litter, his noble form already wasted by the relentless malady which was fast consuming his vital energies. Yet, even in the grasp of death, a sublime and touching beauty rested on his countenance. His dark, eloquent eyes, though sunk in their orbits, beamed with a spiritual intelligence that seemed to belong to a higher sphere; and his voice, though faint and broken by the convulsions of his suffering, possessed a melody, a pathetic earnestness, which penetrated at once to the deepest recesses of my heart. I was appointed to his care; and from the first hour that I stood beside his bed, bathing his burning brow, or sustaining his feeble frame against the terrors of the night, I felt that a mysterious, irresistible sympathy had entwined my destiny with his. 

“Do not deceive me, gentle Clara,” he murmured one evening, as the tempest howled without, dashing the rain against the trembling glass; “I know full well that the destroyer has marked me for his own. The springs of life are exhausted; and though your kindness would fain revive the expiring ember, it is but the flickering of a lamp that has no oil. Tell me, then, that I must die.”

“They are but the gloomy imaginings of a fevered brain,” I replied, though my own heart sank within me as I spoke, and the treacherous tears started to my eyes, belying the forced courage of my speech. “The physicians have not wholly despaired; and my own unwearied care shall not be wanting to win you back to the light of day.”

“Alas!” he answered, raising his large, lustrous eyes to mine with an expression of mournful gratitude, “your voice is sweeter than the music of the spheres, but it cannot recall the departed spirit. I am grateful—deeply, unutterably grateful for your celestial ministry; yet I would not have you build upon a foundation of sand. If I must perish, let me at least feel that I am not utterly solitary; let me believe that one gentle, noble heart laments my fate, and remembers me when the cold turf is laid above my head.”

O, what words were those! They sank into my soul like water upon a parched and thirsty ground. I knew—I felt it with a certainty far deeper than any bodily sense—that the malady was indeed incurable; that the fatal taint had already too deeply pervaded his exquisite frame. I knew that the science of our age, with all its boasted mechanisms and its subtle, unceasing analyses, was here utterly powerless; that the grave was already yawning for its victim. And yet, from that very hour, a fatal, overwhelming passion took possession of my being. I loved him—I, who had hitherto lived but for the stern discharge of my duties, who had looked upon suffering and death with a calm, though melancholy, eye—loved him with a fervour, a wild, impassioned devotion, that swallowed up all other considerations, all prudence, all hope. It was madness, it was despair; but I could not, I would not, struggle against it. I gave myself up to the delicious, agonising illusion, as the drowning mariner clings to the frail, tempest-shattered plank, knowing that ruin yawns beneath. 

Day after day, and night after night, I watched beside him. The disease advanced with stealthy, but certain, steps. The hue of health faded entirely from his cheek, giving place to a transparent, marble paleness, which seemed rather to belong to a disembodied spirit than to a perishable mortal. Yet, as his bodily strength declined, the energies of his mind appeared to expand with a corresponding, almost supernatural, clearness. He spoke of the future, of the vast, unknown regions to which he was hastening, with a wild, prophetic eloquence that thrilled me with awe and anguish. He discoursed of our unhappy country, of the ruined state of humanity, of the sublime mysteries of the universe, till I forgot, in the entrancing melody of his voice, the awful reality of his situation. And when, at length, the paroxysms of his pain returned with redoubled fury, and he clung to my hand with a convulsive grasp, as if to shield himself from the invisible foe, my very soul seemed rent with unutterable agonies. 

“Clara,” he exclaimed, in a tone of thrilling, heart-rending pathos, “must we then part so soon? I have but just awakened to the knowledge of how much life contained of good, and the cup is dashed from my lips! O, why is this? Why am I thus torn from the world, from you—from the sweet, beloved light of day—to be plunged into the dreary, unfathomable abyss? Is there no pity in Heaven? No stay for this relentless stroke?”

“I am here, Edgar,” I cried, throwing myself on my knees beside him, and pressing his burning hand to my lips, while the tears streamed down my face in torrents; “I am here, and I will not leave you. If I cannot save you, I will at least share your sufferings; I will pour out my soul with yours in its passage through the dark valley. Do not despair, my beloved; think that you are not alone, that a heart which beats solely for you will follow you to the very gates of eternity.”

He looked at me with a gaze of unutterable tenderness. “Angelic girl,” he whispered, “your love throws a halo over the gloom of death. I could almost bless the pangs that have drawn me to you. But it is a selfish joy. You—you will be left behind, to mourn, to suffer, to endure the desolation of a solitary existence. Would that I could bear you with me, or that I could stay to be your shield and protector on earth!”

The struggle was brief, but terrible. The vital principle, long undermined, gave way at once; and in a few hours after this fatal conversation, he breathed his last in my arms, with a sweet, placid smile resting upon his pallid features, as if the spirit, in its flight, had caught a glimpse of the celestial tranquillity it was about to enjoy. I closed his eyes; I folded his hands upon his breast; and then, feeling that the last tie which bound me to the world of hope was severed, I gave way to a frenzy of grief, which, while it shook my frame to its inmost centre, yet brought a strange, wild consolation to my heart. I had loved him—I had watched beside him—I had been to him in his dying hours all that fond, devoted affection could be; and though I knew from the first that my love was hopeless, that I was nursing a dying man, yet I would not, for all the world contains, have foregone those hours of mingled anguish and rapture. They had elevated me above the common, sordid cares of life; they had revealed to me the depths of my own nature, the boundless capacity of the human soul for suffering and for love. And now, as I sat alone beside his lifeless corse, in the stillness of that desolate chamber, the storms of my own heart subsiding into a calm, unutterable melancholy, I felt that I was left a solitary wanderer upon the earth, severed from all that made life dear, and bound by an indissoluble chain to the memories of the dead.
