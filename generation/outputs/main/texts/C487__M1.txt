The salt-laced wind that swept across the deck of the *María de Gipuzkoa* was a familiar hand on Captain Etxeberria’s cheek. For thirty years, it had been the only constant in his life, a companion more faithful than any man. He stood at the helm, his knuckles white on the varnished wood, his gaze fixed on the grey, churning water ahead. The republic was a restless beast, and its coastline was a jagged, unforgiving jaw. He had sailed its teeth for three decades, carrying mail sacks that smelled of ink and news, barrels of coffee from the highlands, bolts of cloth for the port-city *señoras*, and, on occasion, the silent, grim-faced men who were always trouble. He never asked. The contents of a crate were a matter between God and the man who paid the freight. His only gospel was the tide chart and the almanac.

But this time, the gospel was written in lead and steel. Below deck, in the cargo hold, nestled amongst sacks of flour and crates of unremarkable tools, lay fifty wooden cases. He knew their weight, the specific way the ship’s timbers groaned under their burden. He knew the sharp, metallic scent that seemed to leak from the seams, a smell that cut through the familiar aromas of brine, coal smoke, and old wood. They were rifles, Martini-Henrys from the British surplus, their oiled barrels cold and waiting. He knew, because the man who had loaded them had not been a merchant. He had been a soldier with no uniform.

The commander, a lean, hawk-faced man who called himself only ‘El Lobo’, had found Etxeberria in a smoky cantina in the port of San Miguel. The Wolf’s eyes had been the colour of the winter sea, and he had spoken with the quiet authority of a man accustomed to being obeyed. He had laid a heavy purse of gold coins on the sticky table. “To Puerto Ceniza, Captain,” he had said, his voice a low rasp. “To the fishermen’s cove, not the main quay. After midnight.”

Etxeberria had stared at the gold. It was a handsome sum, enough to repair the *María*’s boilers properly and perhaps see him through another lean season. But the destination was a problem. Puerto Ceniza was a forgotten scratch on the coast, a place of nets and stinking fish, not a port of call. And the cargo… he had seen the cases being brought aboard under the cover of darkness, their weight and shape leaving no room for doubt. He had carried gunpowder for mining, and he had carried soldiers, but he had never carried the instruments of insurrection itself. To know was a burden he had spent a lifetime avoiding.

“Why?” he had asked, the single word feeling foreign and dangerous in his mouth.

El Lobo had smiled, a humourless stretching of thin lips. “The Republic has forgotten its sons in the hills, Captain. We are simply reminding it that we are still here.”

Now, with the sun bleeding out over the horizon and the first chill of night creeping into the air, Etxeberria felt the weight of that knowledge like an anchor chain wrapped around his soul. His first mate, a younger Basque named Arana, stood beside him, his youthful face etched with a worry that was not yet hardened into the captain’s weary resignation.

“The sea is getting angry, Captain,” Arana said, nodding towards the swell that was beginning to build from the west. “And I don’t like the look of that sky.”

Etxeberria grunted. “The sky is a woman, Arana. It has many moods. We have weathered worse.” But he knew Arana was not speaking of the weather. The boy had seen the extra hands hired for the trip, the silent, watchful men who kept to themselves and sharpened knives on the forecastle. He had heard the whispers.

They were making for a small, sheltered inlet a league from Puerto Ceniza to wait for the appointed hour. As the *María* turned, the coast rose up before them, a wall of dark cliffs and black sand beaches. It was a desolate, haunted stretch of land. Etxeberria’s hands, usually so sure, felt clumsy on the wheel. He was a mariner, a man of charts and currents, of predictable winds and known destinations. This… this was a venture into fog. The cargo below was not just steel and wood; it was hope and hatred, revolution and death. It was the beginning of a story, and he was merely the man turning the page.

He remembered his father, a fisherman from Getaria, telling him that a captain’s first duty was to his ship and his crew. But what was his duty now? To deliver these weapons to men who would use them to kill other men, some of whom he might have carried in his mail coach? To become a party to the spilling of blood on the very shores he had navigated with such care? The gold in his cabin felt like a thief’s payment.

A squall hit them just after midnight. Rain lashed the deck in horizontal sheets, and the wind howled in the rigging, a chorus of lost souls. The *María de Gipuzkoa* bucked and shuddered, a living thing fighting the sea. Etxeberria fought the wheel, his muscles screaming, his face stung by the salt spray. Through the maelstrom, he saw the faint, flickering signal from the shore—a lantern, shuttered and opened three times. They were there. Waiting.

He brought the steamer in as close as he dared, the engine groaning in protest as he held her against the pull of the tide. The black shape of a longboat emerged from the gloom, manned by shadowy figures who moved with a desperate, practiced efficiency. The crates were brought up from the hold, passed hand-to-hand along a human chain, the men grunting with the effort. Etxeberria watched from the bridge, a silent, solitary figure. He saw the face of the man in the longboat’s stern, illuminated for a moment by the lantern. It was El Lobo, his hawk-face impassive against the storm.

The last crate was lowered. The exchange was made—a small, heavy bag of gold passed up to Arana. No words were spoken. The longboat melted back into the rain and darkness. As the *María*’s engines churned and she began to pull away from the deadly shore, a single, sharp crack echoed across the water. It was followed by another, then a ragged volley. The sound was thin and distant, swallowed by the storm, but it was unmistakable. It was the sound he had carried in his hold.

Etxeberria did not look back. He turned the *María*’s bow towards the open sea, towards the horizon where, he prayed, the dawn would come. The wind was still in his face, but it felt different now. It no longer smelled of salt and freedom, but of powder and blood. He was a captain of the sea, a Basque mariner who had carried every kind of cargo for thirty years. But he knew, with a certainty that chilled him more than any winter gale, that he had just delivered a storm to the land, and he could never truly leave it behind. The ship sailed on, but the weight of the rifles remained, a cold, heavy presence in the silence of the empty hold.
