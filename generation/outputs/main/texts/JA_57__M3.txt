It is a truth very generally acknowledged that a young woman in possession of twenty thousand pounds must be in want of a suitor, though she might very reasonably wish to be in want of none. Miss Clara Fairfax, being precisely such a young woman, found herself the acknowledged centre of attraction in a house in Bath, and the object of more particular attentions than were quite consistent with her comfort.

Her guardian, Mr. Percival, a man of excellent sense and an even more excellent income, looked upon the circle of her admirers with a caution that bordered on suspicion. He had seen too much of the world to believe in the disinterestedness of a young man of small fortune, and he resolved to speak his mind to his ward without delay.

"You must be aware, Clara," said he, one morning as they sat together in the breakfast parlour, "that you are a considerable heiress, and that your present society in Bath is composed, in no small part, of gentlemen whose attentions to you are dictated by considerations of the most mercenary kind."

Clara, who had been spreading butter on her toast with a composure that suggested she was not entirely unacquainted with the idea, replied that she was not entirely ignorant of the fact.

"I am glad of it," said Mr. Percival, "for I should be sorry to think you deceived by the appearance of disinterested affection. Every man who courts you wants only your money. I speak plainly because I speak as your guardian, and because I have no patience for the affectation of romance. You would do well to receive their addresses with a degree of caution proportionate to the size of your fortune."

Clara, who had a turn for reflection that was rather more active than her guardian gave her credit for, received this advice with a seriousness that concealed a design already forming in her mind. She was not inclined to accept Mr. Percival's assertion without some proof; and she was, moreover, of a temper that preferred putting things to the test rather than taking them upon trust. She resolved, therefore, to try her suitors by a stratagem which, whatever might be thought of its propriety, was at least defensible by the plea of curiosity, and by the still stronger plea of self-preservation.

The scheme was simple enough. She would give out, by means of a carefully managed report, that her fortune had been diminished by the failure of her trustee to such a degree as to leave her with a mere pittance. She would then observe the effect of this intelligence upon the gentlemen who had distinguished her by their notice, and draw her conclusions accordingly.

The report was accordingly circulated, with all the discretion and all the speed that Bath could supply, which is to say with very little of the former and a great deal of the latter. Within two days, the whole of the Upper Rooms was in possession of the news; and within three, the effect upon her admirers was such as to afford her the most complete satisfaction in the efficacy of her experiment, if not in the result.

Mr. Thistlethwaite, a lieutenant in the militia, who had been loudest in his praises of her eyes and most constant in his attendance at her door, called once after the report spread, and then was seen no more. He was understood to have transferred his attentions to Miss Mortimer, of Wiltshire, whose ten thousand pounds had the merit of being unimpaired. Mr. Fanshawe, a young man of more polished address but equally slender means, wrote her a letter of such elaborate sympathy as left her in no doubt that his chief object was to withdraw himself with grace; and Mr. Elwyn, who had been on the point of a proposal, discovered suddenly that his health required him to try the waters at Bristol.

It was not, however, the disappearance of these gentlemen that gave Clara the most uneasiness, but the persistence of one who remained. Mr. Granville, a man of five-and-thirty, of respectable family and very small fortune, had been among her most assiduous admirers; and she had, from the beginning, found him the least agreeable of them all. He was correct in his manners, formal in his conversation, and entirely wanting in that ease of address which she had been used to admire. He had a way of saying things that were meant to be agreeable and that fell short of the mark by a distance so small as to make the failure more conspicuous than a larger one would have been. He was, in short, the very man whom she would have been least sorry to lose; and it was therefore with something between vexation and amusement that she observed his continued attendance.

He called at Camden Place the morning after the others had fallen away, and found her alone in the drawing-room. He enquired after her health with his usual precision, sat down with his usual deliberation, and after a pause of some moments, remarked that the weather was remarkably fine for the time of year.

Clara, who had expected some allusion to the report, and had prepared herself to meet it with becoming modesty, was thrown a little out of her plan by this opening; but she replied that it was indeed very fine, and that she hoped to walk in the Crescent before dinner.

"I believe," said he, "that the air of Bath is generally found to be beneficial at this season. I have myself derived considerable advantage from it."

There was another pause. Clara began to think that he had not heard the report, and was half inclined to be glad of it; but her curiosity was too strong to allow her to rest in uncertainty.

"You are very good to continue your visits," said she, "when I understand that my circumstances are so much changed. I fear I am no longer the heiress I was believed to be."

Mr. Granville looked at her with an expression that she could not quite read. "I am not aware," said he, "that my visits have been founded upon any consideration of that kind. If I have had the pleasure of calling upon you, it has been because I found your society agreeable, and not because I had formed any calculations upon the subject of your fortune."

Clara was silenced. She had prepared no answer for such a speech, because she had not expected it from Mr. Granville. She had supposed that if any man remained, it would be one whose sincerity she could at least respect, even if she could not admire him; and she found herself in the awkward position of having her prejudice confounded by the very person against whom it had been strongest.

He continued, with a gravity that was rather increased than diminished by the importance of what he was saying, "I am aware that my own fortune is small, and that I have no right to address you in terms that might appear presumptuous. But I should be wanting in candour if I did not own that my regard for you has been of long standing, and that the report of your misfortune, so far from diminishing it, has only made me more desirous of proving it."

Clara, who had begun the conversation with the calm superiority of a person conducting an experiment, found herself at the close of it in a very different situation. She had tested her suitors and found them wanting; but the one who had stood the test was the one she had liked least, and she was now obliged to ask herself whether her dislike had been founded upon anything more solid than a preference for livelier manners and a more animated address.

She was saved from the necessity of an immediate reply by the entrance of Mr. Percival, who came in with his usual unceremonious step, and who, upon perceiving Mr. Granville, looked as if he wished him at the other end of the county. Mr. Granville, who was not wanting in discernment, took his leave shortly after; and Clara was left to the reflections that her experiment had occasioned.

She could not but acknowledge that Mr. Granville's conduct had been such as to do him credit, and that her own had been such as to call for a good deal of self-examination. She had been clever, and she had been just; but she was not sure that she had been wise. The test which she had applied had proved more than she had bargained for, and she was now in the disagreeable situation of being obliged to revise an opinion which she had formed with more confidence than reflection.

Mr. Percival, who had observed her silence, and who was not slow in attributing it to some connection with the report that had been circulated, enquired whether she had heard the news of her fortune.

"I have heard it," said she, "and I have also heard the effect of it upon my admirers."

"And what is the effect?" said he, with a smile that showed he expected the answer.

"They are gone," said she, "all but one."

"And who is the one?"

"Mr. Granville."

Mr. Percival's surprise was very nearly equal to her own. He had formed no high opinion of Mr. Granville, and had rather wondered at Clara's tolerance of him; but he was a man of too much justice to refuse his respect to conduct that had been proved disinterested.

"Then I was wrong," said he, after a pause, "in saying that every man who courts you wants only your money. I am glad of it, for your sake; though I confess I should have been better pleased if the exception had been a man of more engaging manners."

"So should I," said Clara, with a sigh that she immediately checked, as if conscious that it betrayed more than she wished to own.

Whether the sigh was for the loss of the other suitors, or for the qualities in which Mr. Granville was deficient, or for the general uncertainty of human expectations, she did not stop to examine; but she was aware, as she retired to dress for dinner, that her experiment had succeeded beyond her hopes, and had failed beyond her fears, and that she was now left to the task of deciding whether to reward the only man who had proved himself worthy, or to persist in disliking him for the qualities he lacked. It was a task which she found more perplexing than any she had yet encountered, and which the society of Bath, with all its variety, was not likely to make less so.
