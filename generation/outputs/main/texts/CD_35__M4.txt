The rain, which had begun at dusk in a thin, spiteful drizzle, now set in for the night with a steady, drumming persistency, converting the cobbles of the wharf at Gaffer’s Reach into a thousand miniature looking-glasses, each reflecting a murky fragment of the scene. It was a scene of that peculiar desolation which belongs to the canal-side at night: the black, sluggish water, the tarred and glistening barges, the warehouses that stood like so many surly, tight-lipped merchants hoarding their goods in the dark, and the solitary gas-lamp, which, with its sickly halo of light, seemed rather to emphasise the surrounding gloom than to dispel it. The air was thick with the mingled odours of coal-smoke, wet rope, and the dank exhalations of the cut; and the only sounds were the hiss of the rain upon the water, the distant, melancholy lowing of a fog-horn, and the steady, patient lapping of the ripples against the wooden sides of the moored vessels.

Upon the deck of one of these barges—a stout, broad-beamed craft, bearing the name *The Perseverance* upon her stern—stood a young fellow of some eighteen or nineteen summers, whose broad shoulders and bronzed countenance bespoke a life spent in the open air. His name was Ned Crummles, and he was the adopted son of Abel Varden, the barge-master; though, to look at him, you would have sworn that the black water of the canal had been his native element, so thoroughly had he acquired the rolling gait, the keen, weather-wise eye, and the general, indefinable air of the born waterman. He was engaged in coiling a rope with the neat, methodical precision of a sailor, when his attention was arrested by a figure that came slouching along the tow-path, its form indistinct in the rain and the gathering darkness.

The figure stopped opposite *The Perseverance*, and seemed to hesitate, peering through the wet and the gloom with a kind of furtive curiosity. It was a small, wizened, shrunken figure, clad in garments that had once been black but were now of a rusty and nondescript hue, and it carried upon its shoulder a bundle of brushes and a long, jointed pole, the very ensign and symbol of its trade. Ned Crummles, pausing in his work, regarded the stranger with that frank and open curiosity which is the characteristic of a man who has nothing to conceal.

‘You’ll be wanting a night’s lodging, mate?’ said he, in a voice that was hearty and pleasant, though not without a certain rough authority. ‘The “Bargee’s Rest” is a mile further on, but if you’re a mind to take pot-luck with us, why, there’s a dry corner in the cabin, and a bit of fire to warm your bones.’

The stranger started at the sound of his voice, and, coming a step nearer, lifted his face to the light of the lamp that hung at the barge’s stern. It was a face that, once seen, was not easily forgotten: a face prematurely old, with a pinched and hungry look, and a pair of small, restless, shifting eyes that seemed to be for ever on the watch for some approaching danger. It was a face that bore the unmistakable stamp of a life of hardship, of blows, and of that peculiar, cringing servility which is the offspring of cruelty.

‘Ned?’ said the man, in a voice that was little more than a whisper, and that shook with a strange agitation. ‘Ned Crummles? Lord love me! Is it you?’

Ned started in his turn, and looked at the man more narrowly. There was something in the voice, something in the eager, hungry glance of those restless eyes, that stirred a chord of memory within him, a memory that was vague and indistinct, and that belonged to a time of which he had long ceased to think.

‘That’s my name,’ he answered, slowly. ‘But I can’t say as how I knows yours. You’ve the advantage of me, master.’

The man came closer still, and, with a gesture that was almost fawning, laid a hand upon the gunwale of the barge.

‘Don’t you remember?’ he said, with a kind of chuckling eagerness that was more unpleasant than any menace. ‘Don’t you remember the cellar, Ned? The cellar in Saffron Hill, where the rats was so uncommon bold, and the soot was that thick on the floor you might have planted taters in it? Don’t you remember old Grimes—Grimes the sweep—and the little ’un what run away one precious cold night, and was never ’eard on no more? That was you, Ned! That was you, my boy! And a precious row there was about it, I can tell you. Old Grimes, he swore as you’d been stole by the gypsies, or drownded in the river, and he got a new ’prentice out of the parish; but I knowed—I knowed as how you’d give us the slip. Lord, how I knowed it!’

The man’s voice sank into a confidential whisper. He was the sweep’s assistant—the same who had been the boy’s fellow-sufferer in that dark and noisome den, and who had shared with him the scanty crusts and the frequent blows which were the common lot of Grimes’s apprentices. Ned Crummles stood as if turned to stone. The rain beat upon his face, the wind whistled through the rigging of the barge, but he neither saw nor heard. The whole scene of his early misery rose up before him with a vividness that was almost appalling: the dark, stifling cellar, the heap of soot-bags, the brutal face of Grimes, and the little, shivering, terrified child who had fled from it all one winter’s night, and had never looked back.

‘You’ve growed,’ continued the man, with a kind of grim admiration, ‘you’ve growed wonderful. And you’ve got a barge, and a comfortable berth, and a—a—a family, like, as I may say. And me—look at me!—me as was always your friend, Ned—me as used to give you the biggest share of the bread-and-dripping—I’m a poor, broken-down, ’alf-starved cove, as ’asn’t a friend in the world, nor a roof to cover ’im, nor a—a—a crust to put in ’is mouth.’

He shivered, and drew his scanty garments closer about him with a gesture that was admirably calculated to excite compassion. But Ned Crummles, though his heart was full, was not to be deceived. He had learned, in the hard school of his early years, to read the human countenance, and he saw, beneath the man’s cringing manner and pitiful words, the same greedy, selfish, and cruel nature which he remembered of old.

‘You want something,’ he said, shortly. ‘What is it?’

The man’s face changed. The fawning look vanished from it, and was replaced by a cunning, sidelong glance, that was like a glimpse of the real man beneath the mask.

‘Well, now,’ he said, with a kind of chuckling deliberation, ‘since you’re so uncommon sharp, Ned, I’ll tell you. It’s a matter of—a matter of a little money, my boy. Only a few shillings—a sovereign, now, would be a fortune to a poor fellow like me—and I’d be off, and you’d never see nor hear of me no more. There’s a many as would be glad to know as you’re alive and thriving—the parish, for instance, as paid for your ’prenticeship; and old Grimes, he’d be uncommon glad to see you again, and to ’ear as how you’d got on in the world. And there’s the law, too, Ned—the law, as might say as how you’d run away from your master, and as how you was bound to serve him till you was one-and-twenty. It’s a wonderful thing, the law, and no mistake!’

He chuckled again, and looked up at Ned with a leer of malignant triumph. The threat was plain enough. The man had him in his power, and he knew it. Ned Crummles felt the blood rush to his face, and his hands clenched themselves involuntarily. For a moment, the old, wild, lawless spirit of the runaway apprentice rose up within him, and he would have been glad to have seized the wretch by the collar and hurled him into the black water of the canal. But he was a man now, and not a boy, and he had learned to govern his passions.

‘You’ll get no money from me,’ he said, in a low, firm voice, ‘not a brass farthing. You may go to the parish, and you may go to Grimes, and you may go to the devil, for all I care. I’m a free man now, and I’ve got friends as’ll stand by me. So be off with you, and don’t let me see your ugly face again, or it’ll be the worse for you.’

The man saw that he had mistaken his customer. He had counted upon finding the boy still the timid, cowering creature of the Saffron Hill cellar, and he had found a man. He muttered something between his teeth—a curse, perhaps, or a threat—and, turning away, slouched off along the tow-path, and was soon lost in the rain and the darkness. Ned Crummles stood for a long time looking after him, and then, with a heavy sigh, he turned and went down into the cabin, where the bright fire and the cheerful faces of Abel Varden and his daughter seemed to him, by contrast with the scene he had just passed through, like a glimpse of paradise.
