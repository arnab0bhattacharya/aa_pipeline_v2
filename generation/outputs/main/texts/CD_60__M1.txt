The smoke of Calderford had been, for a generation, the very breath of its existence—a sooty exhalation from the tall chimneys of the mills that lined the River Aire, which crept into the lungs of the people, blackened their washing, and settled on their very souls like a shroud. And of all the great, grinding, money-making machines in that grimy paradise of industry, the greatest was the Phoenix Mill, belonging to Mr. Josiah Grindstone. It was a vast, brick-faced leviathan, with a hundred windows like a hundred vacant eyes, and it roared and rattled from dawn until dusk, swallowing raw cotton and spewing out bolts of cloth, while consuming the lives, the health, and the very youth of three hundred human beings, who were known to the world, and to Mr. Grindstone’s ledgers, simply as ‘the hands’.

On a Tuesday night in November, when the fog was so thick you could have cut it with a knife and served it up as a pudding, a cry went up that pierced the fog and the din and the weary slumber of the town. “Fire!” It was a single, terrible word, but it was repeated by a thousand voices, until it became a roar to rival the mill itself. A crimson light, a monstrous and unnatural dawn, bloomed in the heart of the Phoenix, and its hundred vacant eyes were suddenly filled with a terrible, dancing fire. The people of Calderford, who knew that fire as a thief and a destroyer, poured from their damp little houses to stand in the cobbled streets, their faces lit by the inferno, their hearts a cold, hard knot of dread.

From out of the chaos and the heat, one man emerged, his face blackened by soot, his shirt singed, his eyes wide with a desperate purpose. This was Silas Thistlewood, the foreman, a man whose quiet decency was as out of place in the mill as a rose in a slag-heap. He had not run for his life; he had run *into* the peril, for he was the keeper of the books. He knew that those great, leather-bound ledgers, with their neat columns of figures, were the memory of the mill—the record of every hand’s labour, of every hour worked and every penny earned (and, more often, not earned). To lose them would be to wipe the history of three hundred families from the earth. And so he had fought his way to Mr. Grindstone’s office, a small oasis of paper in a desert of flame, and had staggered out again with the heavy volumes clutched to his chest like a mother saving her child.

When the sun rose the next morning, it rose upon a ruin. The Phoenix Mill was no more; it was a skeleton, a blackened ribcage of brick and iron, with smoke still curling from its vitals like the last breath of a dying beast. The three hundred hands stood before it, silent and stupefied. They were no longer ‘hands’; they were people without work, without wages, without a future. Their faces, pale and smudged with yesterday’s soot and today’s despair, were turned towards the one man who could tell them what was to become of them. And that man, Mr. Josiah Grindstone, arrived not in a state of mourning, but in a state of considerable, and rather smug, satisfaction.

Mr. Grindstone was a man built like a strongbox, with a broad, flat face, a small, hard mouth, and eyes that seemed to be perpetually calculating the interest on a debt. He stood before the crowd, his hands tucked into his waistcoat pockets, and surveyed the devastation with the air of a farmer looking over a field that has been conveniently cleared for new planting. “A sad business, a very sad business,” he declared, his voice as dry as a dusty ledger. “A great loss. But! I am a man of foresight. A man who provides against the vagaries of fate. The Phoenix Mill, gentlemen and—ah—ladies, was fully insured. Every brick, every beam, every bolt of cotton in the warehouse.”

A murmur went through the crowd—not of hope, but of a grim, weary understanding. Insurance was for the masters; it was a great, distant god that protected property, not people. But Mr. Grindstone was not finished. “I shall, of course, rebuild,” he announced, puffing out his chest. “For the good of the town, for the progress of the age! But I shall not rebuild here. The site is cramped. The river access is imperfect. No, I have secured a new plot of land at Grimshaw’s Hollow, five miles hence, where I shall erect a new and modern mill, with the very latest machinery!” He smiled, a thin, bloodless stretching of the lips. “It is a new beginning for all of us.”

For the masters, perhaps. For the three hundred hands, it was a death sentence. Grimshaw’s Hollow was five miles away. It meant five miles to walk each way, in the dark and the rain, for a wage that would now be paid to new, cheaper hands drawn from the surrounding villages. It meant the end of their community, the end of their lives as they knew them. A great, hollow groan went up from the crowd, a sound of utter desolation. Mr. Grindstone, misinterpreting it as a cry of despair at the loss of their benefactor, gave a little nod of magnanimous sympathy and turned to leave.

But he was stopped by the voice of Silas Thistlewood. “Sir,” said the foreman, stepping forward, his arms full of the salvaged ledgers. “A word, if you please. I have the books here. I thought it right to save them.” Mr. Grindstone waved a dismissive hand. “Very good, Thistlewood. Very diligent. You shall be rewarded. Bring them to my solicitor’s office in the morning.” He made to push past, but Silas did not move. “It is about the insurance, sir,” he said, his voice quiet but clear in the morning air. “I was just looking at the ledger of accounts, to make sure all was in order for the claim. I saw the entry for the premium. It is dated the fourteenth of October.” He paused, and looked his master full in the face. “The fire was on the twenty-first.”

A silence fell upon the crowd so absolute that the dripping of water from the blackened rafters could be heard. Mr. Grindstone’s face, for the first time, showed a flicker of something other than satisfaction. It was not guilt, for that was an emotion far too subtle for his constitution; it was the sharp, metallic glint of being caught in a lie. “What of it?” he snapped, his voice suddenly harsh. “What of it, man? It is the date the policy commenced.”

“Aye, sir,” said Silas, his voice gaining a strength it had never possessed before. “It commenced a week before the mill burned. A week! Not a year, not a month, but a single week! It is a curious thing, is it not? A most remarkable piece of fortune for the owner, and a most remarkable piece of misfortune for the hands.”

Mr. Grindstone drew himself up to his full, unimpressive height. “Are you suggesting, Thistlewood,” he hissed, “that I, Josiah Grindstone, would have a hand in this? That I would burn down my own property?”

Silas Thistlewood looked from the smug, hard face of his master to the pale, hopeless faces of his comrades, and then down at the heavy, leather-bound ledgers in his arms. They felt heavier now, as if they contained not just figures, but the weight of all their lives. He knew then that he was not merely holding books; he was holding the truth, and the truth, in Calderford, was a dangerous and flammable thing. “I suggest nothing, sir,” he said, his voice ringing with a new and terrible clarity. “I only state the facts. The mill is gone. The hands are turned out. And the insurance was bought seven days before the fire. The rest, sir, is a matter for Providence. Or for the magistrates.” And with those words, the quiet foreman, the keeper of the books, became the keeper of a secret that threatened to burn hotter and more destructively than any fire that had ever consumed the Phoenix Mill.
