I have known the port of Santa Cruz de la Soledad in three distinct phases of its existence, and it is of the second—the middle one, so to speak, when the town still believed in its own future and the foreign flags still hung limp and respectable above their consulates—that I find myself thinking now, with the curious and useless persistence of a man who has outlived his own history. It was a place of heat and inertia, of white dust and green stagnation, where the river, having carried down from the mountains its burden of silt and rumours, emptied both into the sea with the same indifferent and discoloured gesture. The houses along the waterfront leaned their shuttered faces toward the water as if in a perpetual attitude of listening, and the customs shed, with its peeling colonnade, stood like a forgotten sentinel at the edge of a dream from which no one expected to be awakened. It was there that I kept my chandlery—a dim, odorous cavern of a place, stacked with coils of manila and tins of preserved meat and the thousand nameless necessities of ships—and it was there, in the year 1896 or perhaps 1897, for in that climate the years lose their edges and run one into another like the tides, that the English consul, a weary and honourable man named Hargreaves, placed into my keeping the accumulated paper of his office.

He came to me at the hour of siesta, when the town lay prostrate under the weight of the sun and the very dogs had ceased to trouble the flies. I remember the way he stood in the doorway, his linen suit gone grey with dust and sweat, and the look in his eyes of a man who has been carrying a weight too long and has suddenly decided to set it down, not because the weight has grown lighter but because his strength has quietly and irrevocably departed. He had been in that republic, he told me, for eleven years, and in that time he had seen governments rise and fall with the regularity of the rainy season, had signed papers and burned papers, had lied to generals and dined with them, and had come at last to the end of whatever it was that had sustained him. His health, he said, was broken; his nerves, he said, were worse; and there was a packet-boat at dawn, and after that—well, after that there would be other things, other places, a grey sky perhaps, and the sound of English rain against a window, and the blessed, the inexpressible relief of being nobody in particular.

“The archives,” he said, and gestured vaguely toward the street where, in a cart of sorts, there reposed some half-dozen wooden boxes bound with iron and sealed with the arms of a remote and indifferent empire. “They are not valuable, you understand. They are merely—official. They are the record of things that have happened and things that have been said to have happened. In London they will never be read. But they are ours, and I would not leave them to be scattered in the street or used to light the fires of the next revolution.”

I asked him, with what I believed at the time to be a certain worldly wisdom, whether the government of the moment might not take an interest in them. He looked at me with a kind of weary patience.

“My dear fellow,” he said, “the government of the moment is a colonel of cavalry named Ignacio Fuentes, who believes that the British Empire is a myth invented by the merchants of Liverpool to defraud the honest soldier of his customs dues. He will not want the archives. He will want—what he always wants—money, or the appearance of it, or the submission of someone who can be made to appear to have money. Keep the boxes. Keep them in your cellar. Keep them until someone comes from my government to claim them. It will be a year, perhaps two. It will be—” and here he paused, and looked at me with that curious, sidelong, apologetic glance of the man who is about to ask a favour he has no right to ask—“it will be a kindness to me.”

What could I say? I was a chandler, a man of no particular courage and no particular cowardice, who had come to that coast in the way that such men do come—by a series of accidents that had the appearance of a design—and who had stayed because the climate, once it has entered your bones, makes the thought of any other climate seem like the memory of a dream. I said yes. I said it easily, in the manner of a man who has not yet understood the weight of his own words, and I helped him load the boxes into my cart, and I watched him drive away toward the harbour, and I thought no more about it for the better part of a month.

The colonel came in the dry season, when the river was low and the heat lay upon the town like a hand. He came not to my shop but to my house, which stood behind it, and he came not in the afternoon but at night, with two of his officers and a lantern that threw upon the whitewashed walls a light that was less illumination than a kind of aggressive and suspicious shadow. He was a small man, the colonel, with a narrow face and a moustache that seemed too large for it, and he spoke in the rapid, clipped, metallic Spanish of the coast, a language that always sounds to me like the striking of many small coins upon a counter. He had heard, he said, that the English consul had left certain papers in my keeping. He had heard it from a servant, from a clerk, from the air itself—it did not matter. He desired to see them. He desired, in fact, to take them. They were, he explained, with a smile that was worse than any threat, the property of the nation, and the nation was, at that moment, himself.

I have thought since that if I had been a braver man I might have refused him then and there, might have stood upon the dignity of a foreign flag and the inviolability of a foreign trust, and perhaps I might have carried it off, for these men, whatever their ferocity, had a certain respect for the forms of things, and the name of England was still, in those days, a word that could make a small man pause. But I was not a brave man. I was a chandler, and I had a wife who was not well, and I had a business that was not thriving, and I had a horror of violence that was not so much moral as physical, a deep and unnameable dread of the body’s pain. And so I equivocated. I said that the boxes were sealed, that I had no authority to open them, that they were, in a manner of speaking, not mine to give. I said this in the halting and indirect fashion of a man who is trying to be honest without being heroic, and I saw in the colonel’s eyes that he understood me perfectly, and that he despised me, and that he was not, in any case, in a hurry.

“Señor,” he said, “I am a patient man. I have been patient for many years. But I am also a soldier, and a soldier knows that the value of a thing is not always what it appears to be. You have some boxes. I have some men. Perhaps you will think about it. Perhaps you will think about it very carefully.”

He went away, and the night closed behind him, and I stood in my doorway for a long time, listening to the sound of the river and the distant barking of dogs, and thinking about the boxes in my cellar with a clarity that was almost painful. They were, as Hargreaves had said, merely official. They contained the record of things that had happened and things that had been said to have happened. They contained, perhaps, the names of men who had come to the consulate for protection, the names of men who had been arrested or had disappeared, the names of men who were now dead or had become other men or were waiting somewhere in the interior for a change of fortune. They contained, in short, nothing that could be sold or eaten or used to light a fire, and yet I understood, with a sudden and sickening certainty, that the colonel would kill me for them, not because he wanted them but because he had asked for them, and because in that country a man who asks for a thing and does not receive it has ceased, in some mysterious and fatal way, to be a man.

I did not sleep that night. I sat in my darkened shop among the coils of rope and the tins of meat, and I thought of Hargreaves in his grey suit, going home across the sea, and I thought of the boxes in the cellar, and I thought of the way the colonel had smiled. And I understood, in that moment, with a clarity that was not at all pleasant, that the question was not whether the papers were worth my life—for they were not, they were worth nothing, they were waste paper, they were the dust of forgotten things—but whether I was the kind of man who could, in the face of a drawn revolver and a smiling face, say no. And I knew, with a knowledge that was like a cold hand upon my heart, that I was not. I was a chandler. I was a man of no particular courage. I was a man who had come to that coast by accident and had stayed because of the climate, and I would give the colonel his boxes, and I would live, and I would remember.

But the morning brought a thing I had not expected. It brought a boy—a half-caste boy who worked about the harbour, a thin, silent, watchful boy whose name I had never troubled to learn—and he stood in the doorway of my shop with the light behind him, and he said that the colonel had gone. Gone, I said. Gone where? Gone up the river, he said, with his men, to put down a rising in the interior. And the boxes, I said. The boxes, he said, were not to be touched. The colonel had left orders. The boxes were to remain where they were, sealed, untouched, until his return. And then the boy smiled, a strange, knowing, ancient smile, and he said: “He does not want the papers, señor. He wants to know if you will give them to him. It is a game he plays. He plays it with everyone. And if you had given them to him, he would have shot you, because a man who gives up his trust is a man who has no honour, and a man who has no honour is not worth the bullet.”

I stood there for a long time after the boy had gone. The heat was rising, and the flies were beginning, and somewhere in the town a woman was singing a song that was not quite sad and not quite happy, but something in between, something that belonged to that coast and to that climate and to the long, slow, indifferent river. I thought of the boxes in my cellar, and I thought of the colonel, and I thought of Hargreaves, and I thought of myself, and I understood at last that I had been saved not by my courage but by my cowardice, and that there is a kind of irony in that which no man can bear to examine too closely. I went back into my shop, and I took down a coil of rope, and I began to measure it, foot by foot, in the manner of a chandler, and I did not think about the boxes again for a long time.
