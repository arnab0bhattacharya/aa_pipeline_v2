The carriage had been an hour upon the road, and the rain, which had begun as a soft, insinuating drizzle at the lodge gates, had settled into a steady, sullen downpour that beat against the glass and turned the dales into a blurred wash of grey and green. Miss Alderley sat with her hands folded in her lap, her small portmanteau at her feet, and opposite her, his long legs stretched out and his eyes fixed upon the streaming pane, sat Mr. Ravensworth.

They had spoken little since leaving Highfell. The business of the morning—the trunks, the tearful and somewhat hysterical farewells of little Miss Ravensworth, the instructions delivered to the housekeeper, the settling of accounts—had occupied him entirely, and she had been content to remain in her accustomed silence. It was her office to be silent, to be useful, to efface herself; she had learned that lesson in a hard school, and she had no wish to unlearn it now.

But as the miles unrolled behind them and the rain continued its monotonous assault, some barrier in him seemed to soften. He turned from the window, and his voice, when he spoke, had lost its customary curtness.

"You are cold," he said. "Take my plaid. Nay, I insist. There is no merit in shivering, and I have no patience with a false modesty that would rather perish than be comfortable."

She suffered him to wrap the heavy woollen about her shoulders. It smelt of tobacco and of something else—of him, she thought, and blushed at the thought, and was glad the grey light concealed her.

"You are thinking me a strange fellow," he continued, "to be so anxious about a child's schooling, when I might have kept her at home and hired a governess—as I hired you."

"I think nothing of the kind, sir. It is a father's duty to provide for his daughter's future."

"Duty!" He laughed, but there was no mirth in it. "You use the word as if it were a coin you had minted yourself. Duty, Miss Alderley, is a word men use to justify the cruelties they have not the courage to commit openly. I send my daughter away because I am not fit to keep her. There—you have the truth of it. I am not fit."

She was silent. The rain beat on.

"You are not shocked?" he said, turning to her with a sudden, sharp inquiry in his look. "You do not say, 'Oh, sir, you are too hard upon yourself; every father is fit for his own child'?"

"I do not think that, sir. I have seen too much of the world to believe it."

"Ah." He leaned back, and something in his face relaxed. "You are honest. I like you the better for it. There are those in this county who would tell you I am a hard man, a cold man, a man who buried his wife and did not weep enough—did not weep at all, they said. Do you believe them?"

"I believe, sir, that grief has many voices, and that silence is one of them."

He looked at her for a long moment. The carriage lurched through a rut, and she put out her hand to steady herself; he caught it, held it a moment, and then released it as if it had burned him.

"You are a strange woman, Miss Alderley," he said, and his voice was rough. "I engaged you because you were quiet and competent and asked no questions. I did not expect to find—" He stopped. "We shall be at the inn in half an hour. You will dine with me. I will not have you sit at a separate table like a servant. You are my daughter's governess, not my chattel."

That evening, in the low-ceilinged parlour of the King's Arms, he talked to her as he had never talked before. He spoke of his youth, of his travels, of the books he had read and the men he had known; he spoke of his wife, briefly and with a bitter honesty that she found more moving than any eulogy. He asked her opinion on a dozen matters, and listened to her answers with a grave attention that made her heart beat fast. When at last she rose to go to her room, he took her hand and held it.

"You have done me good," he said. "I thank you."

She went upstairs trembling, and lay awake half the night, and told herself she was a fool.

The morning brought the school—a grim, respectable establishment in a market town, with a headmistress who smelled of lavender and disapproval—and the parting with little Miss Ravensworth, who clung to her father and then, with the resilience of childhood, ran off to inspect the dormitories. And then the carriage was turned, and they were upon the homeward road.

She expected that he would speak to her again, that the intimacy of the journey out would continue, that some word or look would acknowledge what had passed between them. But he withdrew into himself as the miles lengthened. He answered her remarks with monosyllables; he stared out of the window; he took a newspaper from his pocket and read it with a fixed and stony countenance. Once she ventured to ask if he were unwell, and he replied, "I am perfectly well," in a tone that closed the door between them as effectually as if he had slammed it in her face.

She could not understand it. She reviewed every word she had spoken, every look she had given, and could find no offence in them. Had she presumed? Had she been too free? She had only answered when he questioned her; she had only been what she was. And yet something had changed—something had been withdrawn, some warmth extinguished, some door shut.

The rain had ceased, and the evening was clear and cold, with a pale sun sinking behind the hills, when they came within sight of Highfell. He roused himself at last.

"We shall be home in a quarter of an hour," he said. "You will be glad to be back, no doubt."

"Yes, sir."

"You will find your duties waiting for you. The housekeeper will have a list of the linen that needs mending. And there is the vicar's daughter—she wishes to take lessons in French. You will arrange it."

"Yes, sir."

He was silent again. Then, as the carriage turned into the drive, he said, without looking at her:

"You must not think, Miss Alderley, that because I spoke to you freely on the road, I shall do so again. A man who talks too much is a fool, and I have been a fool. You are in my house to teach my daughter, not to be my confidante. I trust you will remember that."

She felt the blood leave her face. "I have never sought to be your confidante, sir. I have only—"

"Only what?"

"Only listened when you chose to speak. I did not presume. I never presumed."

He turned and looked at her then, and for a moment his hard face was convulsed with something that might have been pain. "No," he said. "You did not. It is I who presumed. Forgive me."

And he handed her down from the carriage, and walked into the house before her, and did not look at her again.
