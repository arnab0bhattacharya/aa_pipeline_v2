Mr. Pomeroy, who was a chemist by training and a radical by temperament, had taken rooms at the ‘Smuggler’s Rest’ in the village of Netherwich on the Kentish coast for the express purpose of avoiding the distractions of London. The inn, a low, salt-crusted building that leaned away from the prevailing wind, was all but empty in the late autumn of 1893. This suited him perfectly. He required only a quiet room, a steady supply of coal, and the freedom to conduct his experiments without the prying eyes of his colleagues at the Royal College. His work, he felt, was of a significance that would one day be obvious to all, but for the present it was best pursued in solitude.

The subject of his research was a compound he had tentatively designated ‘Accelerant Gamma’. It was a complex organo-phosphorus solution, derived from a painstaking synthesis of various mineral salts and a peculiar alkaloid he had isolated from a species of nightshade. Its theoretical purpose was to stimulate cellular mitosis in plant life with extraordinary efficiency. In a laboratory, on a small scale, the results had been promising. A bean sprout, treated with a single drop, had reached maturity in a matter of days. But Pomeroy was a man of grand ideas. He required a larger canvas, a living laboratory. The inn’s neglected kitchen garden, a sad square of sandy soil behind the stable, presented itself as the ideal proving ground.

On a Tuesday, under a grey and blustering sky, he mixed a pint of Accelerant Gamma with a gallon of water and applied it to the dormant rose bushes, the leggy raspberry canes, and the barren patch of earth where the landlady, a Mrs. Gable, had intended to plant her spring cabbages. He worked with the brisk, practical air of a man sowing a field, his breath pluming in the cold air. Mrs. Gable, a woman whose face was a permanent testament to the hardships of coastal life, watched him from the scullery window with a disapproving frown. She considered all forms of eccentricity, particularly the scientific kind, to be a precursor to moral decay.

By Thursday, the change was undeniable. The garden was no longer merely tidy; it was exuberant. The raspberry canes had thickened to the girth of a man’s wrist and were already putting out new leaves. The rose bushes, which had been a thorny skeleton, were now a dense mass of green foliage, pushing out buds with an almost audible haste. Pomeroy observed this with immense satisfaction, taking notes in a leather-bound journal. The reaction was not merely successful; it was exceeding his most optimistic calculations. The energy required for such growth seemed to defy the known laws of thermodynamics, a thought that thrilled him with its sheer, delicious heresy.

By Saturday, the garden was a spectacle. The growth had become a riotous, tangled explosion. The raspberry canes had merged into a single, thorny thicket that had burst through the garden fence and was now probing the cobblestones of the stable yard. The roses had become monstrous things, their blooms the size of dinner plates, their stems as thick as rope, and their scent a heavy, cloying perfume that filled the air. A pumpkin, which had been a mere seedling on Wednesday, was now a monstrous, ribbed gourd the size of a prize-winning sow, wedged firmly between the wellhead and the scullery wall. The entire square of garden was now a miniature jungle, a riot of green that pulsed with a strange and vigorous life.

It was Mrs. Gable who finally summoned Pomeroy from his room. She was pale and wringing her hands in her apron, her usual stoicism replaced by a palpable fear. “Mr. Pomeroy,” she said, her voice trembling, “you must come and see. It’s the garden. It’s… it’s not right.”

Pomeroy descended the stairs with an air of patient condescension, expecting to show her the wonders of his discovery. But the sight that greeted him in the daylight was more overwhelming than he had anticipated. The garden had vanished, replaced by a wall of vegetation that was already climbing the inn’s brickwork. Great, fleshy leaves, like those of some prehistoric fern, pressed against the windowpanes of the kitchen. A thick vine, sprouting a series of vicious-looking purple flowers, had wound its way around the water pump. The air was thick with the smell of damp earth, chlorophyll, and that unnervingly sweet floral perfume.

“Good heavens,” Pomeroy murmured, a scientist’s wonder momentarily overriding his caution. “The reaction is proceeding at a far greater rate than projected.”

“It’s unnatural!” Mrs. Gable cried, pointing a shaking finger at the pumpkin, which now appeared to be slowly but perceptibly expanding, its rind groaning under the strain. “It’s witchcraft, that’s what it is! I want it gone. I want you gone! I’ll have no devil’s work in my house!”

Before Pomeroy could formulate a reply—a calm, scientific explanation involving phosphorus and cellular stimulation—the situation escalated. The garden gate, which had been pushed open by the advancing raspberry canes, was now blocked by the broad figure of Constable Perkis, the village’s sole representative of the law. He was a man built for inaction, and his uniform seemed a size too small for his comfortable frame. He stood, hands on his hips, surveying the botanical chaos with a look of profound bewilderment.

“Now then, Mrs. Gable,” he said, his voice a slow, official rumble. “What’s all this about? Disturbing the peace, are we?”

“It’s him!” the landlady shrieked, jabbing a finger at Pomeroy. “With his chemicals and his books! He’s made the garden… grow!”

Constable Perkis turned his slow gaze upon the chemist. “Is that so, sir? You’ve been… gardening?”

Pomeroy attempted to draw himself up. “Constable, what you are witnessing is not gardening, but a revolutionary breakthrough in botanical science. I have developed a compound that radically accelerates the natural processes of vegetative life.”

The constable blinked. “Accelerates?”

“Precisely. The growth you see is the result of a simple application of my Accelerant Gamma. It is perfectly controlled, perfectly logical, and represents a new epoch in agriculture.”

As he spoke, a thick tendril of the pumpkin vine, which had been inching its way across the yard, silently curled around the constable’s ankle. He looked down, frowned, and kicked it away with a grunt. The vine recoiled for a second, then resumed its slow, inexorable advance.

“Logical, is it?” Perkis said, his skepticism as dense as the foliage before him. “Looks more like a jungle to me. And I’ll have to ask you for the name of this… chemical, sir. And what you intend to do about it. Because as I see it, this vegetation is now obstructing a public thoroughfare and causing distress to a resident. That’s a matter for the authorities.”

Pomeroy’s mind raced. How could he explain the precise alkaloid chain of a compound so advanced it would take a Royal Commission a decade to understand? He looked from the constable’s stolid, uncomprehending face to Mrs. Gable’s terror, and then at the monstrous, vibrant, utterly impossible garden that was even now beginning to crack the flagstones of the yard. He felt a sudden, dizzying sense of his own genius and his own profound isolation. He had unlocked a power of creation, and his reward was to be questioned about public thoroughfares by a village policeman. The sheer, mundane absurdity of it was the most frightening thing of all. The jungle in the garden was growing, and he was utterly, terrifyingly alone in it.
