The cottage at World’s End—for so the fisherfolk had long named the solitary dwelling that clung to the cliff’s edge above the village of Marrowcombe—was the last refuge I could imagine for my grief, and thither I retired in the autumn of the year, when the sea, no longer the gentle companion of my girlhood, had become the cruel and insatiable enemy of my heart. I had been a wife but eleven months when the waves took my Edward from me; and now the sound of them, which had once been music to my ear, was become a perpetual knell, tolling the death of all my hopes. I would suffer no one to visit me—not my aunt in Exeter, whose letters I returned unopened, nor the well-meaning vicar of the parish, who called twice and was twice refused. I wished only to be alone with the sea that had bereaved me, to watch it from my window in its every mood of sullen calm or furious tempest, and to feel that I was as desolate as it was boundless.

The cottage was small and low, with walls of whitewashed stone and a roof of heavy slate that groaned beneath the gales. Its windows faced the west, commanding a prospect of the vast Atlantic, and at evening I would sit and watch the sun descend into that watery grave which held my husband, and I would speak to him as though he could hear me across the waste of waters. “Edward,” I would whisper, “the light is fading, and with it fades the little courage that remained to me. The sea is dark and cold, and I am dark and cold within. Would that I had been taken with you, that we might have sunk together into that silent deep, and never more been parted!” Thus I indulged my melancholy, and found a bitter pleasure in its indulgence; for grief, when it is nursed so tenderly, becomes a companion, and I had no other.

It was in the third week of my seclusion that I first observed the flowers. I had gone to my door at daybreak, as was my custom, to look upon the sea and to breathe the salt air that seemed to me the only breath of life remaining, when I perceived upon the stone step a little posy of wild blooms—sea-pinks and yellow trefoil, with a sprig of rosemary for remembrance, and all of them still beaded with the dew. I looked about me, but no living creature was in sight; the path that wound down to the village was empty, and the cliff beyond was bare of any human form. I confess that I was at first displeased, for I had resolved upon utter solitude, and this silent offering seemed an intrusion upon my sorrow. I left the flowers where they lay, and returned into the house.

But the next morning they were renewed, and the next, and the next; and whether the day was fair or foul, whether the wind raged and the rain beat against my casement, the little posy was ever there, as punctual as the dawn. Sometimes it was a cluster of harebells, fragile and trembling; sometimes a branch of honeysuckle, fragrant beyond measure; once a single crimson poppy, which seemed to me a drop of blood upon the grey stone. I began to watch for them; I began to wonder who it was that thus persisted in an attention which I had never invited and could not repel; and by degrees—so strange and wayward is the human heart—I found myself looking for the flowers with something that was almost pleasure, and when I had gathered them up and placed them in a little earthenware cup upon my table, I felt less utterly alone than I had done since the day of my bereavement.

At length I resolved to discover my unknown benefactor. I rose before the sun, and concealed myself behind the thicket of furze that grew at the angle of the garden wall, and there I waited, shivering in the chill morning air, until the first grey light of dawn began to spread over the eastern sky. Then I saw a figure come timidly down the path from the village—a girl, slight and slender, wrapped in a cloak that was much too large for her, with her hood drawn over her head. She approached the door with the stealthy caution of a fawn, and laid her offering upon the step, and was about to retire, when I stepped forth from my concealment and addressed her.

She started violently, and would have fled; but I called to her in a gentle voice, bidding her not to fear, and asking whether it was she who had so long honoured my threshold with her gifts. She turned, and I saw a countenance pale and delicate, with large eyes that held a sadness beyond her years, and a mouth that trembled as she answered me. “I feared you would be angry, madam,” she said; “but I could not bear to think that you were always alone, and I thought that perhaps the flowers might speak to you when no one else could.”

Her name, she told me, was Eliza Trewin, and she was the daughter of a fisherman of Marrowcombe who had been lost at sea when she was but a child of seven years. Her mother had died of grief within the twelvemonth, and she was now living with an aged grandmother, who was bedridden and could not leave the house. “I know what it is,” she said, “to sit and watch the sea, and to wait for one who never comes; and when I heard that you had lost your husband in the same cruel manner, my heart went out to you, and I longed to comfort you, but I dared not speak. So I brought the flowers, because they were all I had to give.”

I was deeply moved by her simple tale, and by the earnestness of her manner; and I felt ashamed of the coldness with which I had received her innocent offerings. I bade her enter my cottage, and I gave her bread and milk, and we sat together by the window and looked out upon the sea, and she talked to me of her father, and of the little boat in which he had sailed forth upon that fatal morning, and of the storm that had arisen in the night, and of the long days of waiting and watching that had followed, and of the hope that would not die until at last the fragments of the boat were washed ashore, and with them the certainty of her loss. As she spoke, the tears streamed down her cheeks, and I wept with her; and in that hour of mutual sorrow I felt the ice about my heart begin to thaw, and I knew that I was no longer alone in my grief.

From that day forth Eliza came daily to my cottage, and the flowers were no longer left upon the step, but gathered by her hand and placed by her own hand in the cup upon my table. She was a child in years, but she had a wisdom that was born of suffering, and she taught me, without knowing it, that sorrow is not to be cherished as a treasure, but to be borne as a burden which may be lightened by the sympathy of others. And I, in my turn, taught her to read and to write, and to understand the books which had been my companions in my happier days; and I found a new purpose in life, and a new reason for gratitude, in the care I bestowed upon her.

The spring came on apace, and the sea grew calm and blue beneath the vernal sun, and the wild flowers sprang up in abundance on the cliffs, and the birds sang in the thickets, and all nature seemed to rejoice in the resurrection of the year. And I, who had thought that I could never more take pleasure in the world, found myself listening to the song of the lark with a heart that was not wholly sad, and looking upon the sea with a spirit that was no longer bitter. For I had learned that the dead are not lost to us while we keep them in our memory, and that the living have claims upon us which we cannot disregard without sin. And when I thought of Edward, it was no longer with the wild anguish of despair, but with a tender and solemn sorrow, as of one who has gone before me into a far country, and who waits for me there, and who would not have me mourn as those who have no hope.

Yet I do not pretend that my grief was healed; it was rather softened and made bearable, like a wound that has ceased to bleed, though the scar remains for ever. Often in the twilight, when the sea was calm and the western sky was flushed with gold, I would sit at my window and think of my husband, and weep; but the tears were gentle now, and not without a certain sweetness. And Eliza would sit beside me, and hold my hand in hers, and say nothing; and I felt that in her silent sympathy there was a balm more precious than any words. So the days passed, and I ceased to count them, and the cottage at World’s End was no longer a place of exile, but a home; and the sea, which had been my enemy, became once more my friend, and I looked upon its vast and solemn beauty with a heart that was at peace.
