The wind off the North Sea did not merely blow; it assaulted. It tore at the hems of Elara Vane’s traveling dress and whipped the rain into horizontal stings that blurred the world beyond her carriage window. As the wheels groaned against the loose gravel of the drive, the silhouette of Harrowgate Manor emerged from the mist, a jagged teeth of granite and slate jutting from the cliffside. It was a house that looked less like a home and more like a fortress built by a man who feared the sea more than he feared God. Elara had not seen the place in twenty years, not since she was a child playing in the gardens before her father’s heart gave out and the world narrowed to the walls of a London boarding house. Now, at twenty-two, she was its mistress.

The carriage lurched to a halt before the imposing oak doors. Before Elara could even lower her parasol, the door opened. It was not the butler she expected, nor the groundskeeper, but a woman of indeterminate age with a face like a crumpled map of wrinkles. Her eyes were pale, almost milky, and they fixed on Elara with a intensity that felt physical, a cold hand pressing against her sternum.

"Miss Vane?" the woman asked. Her voice was dry, like leaves scraping over stone.

"I am Elara Vane," Elara said, her voice trembling slightly, though she blamed the wind. "I have come to claim my inheritance."

The woman did not smile. She did not even nod. She stepped aside, not with an invitation, but with the weary resignation of someone blocking a draft. "We are expecting the new mistress," she said, her tone flat and devoid of recognition. "But you do not look like her."

Elara stepped into the foyer, the heat of the house shocking against the chill of her skin. The air smelled of beeswax, old paper, and something else—something metallic and stale, like blood drying in the sun. "I look exactly like her," Elara said, pointing to the massive portrait hanging above the fireplace. It depicted a woman in dark mourning velvet, her chin lifted, her eyes sharp and piercing. The resemblance was uncanny. The same high cheekbones, the same severe jaw, the same dark, defiant eyes. "This is my mother. I am her daughter."

The housekeeper, who had introduced herself only as Mrs. Thorne, looked at the portrait, then back at Elara. "That is the Lady Harrowgate," Mrs. Thorne said. "She died in 1888."

"Then you know I am Elara. I am the daughter of the deceased Lady Harrowgate and the late Lord Vane."

"A pity," Mrs. Thorne said, turning on her heel. "The master is not here. He has not been seen since the solicitor’s death. And the master is not speaking to anyone who has not been cleared by the estate lawyer. You are cleared by no one, Miss... Vane?"

"Mr. Silas Blackwood died three days ago," Elara said, the information dropping into the silent room like a stone. "I received the letter from the firm. I am the sole heir."

"Mr. Blackwood is dead," Mrs. Thorne agreed, her voice dropping to a whisper that seemed to fill the high vaulted ceiling. "Just as everyone says. But dead men do not send letters. And dead men do not open doors. You are staying in the guest wing. We will see if you are who you say you are when the police arrive."

The police. Elara felt a spike of ice in her belly. "There is no need for the police. I have the deed."

"Do you?" Mrs. Thorne asked. "I have seen no deed. I have seen no papers. I have seen only a girl with a dead man’s face and a living man’s ambition."

Elara was escorted to a room on the upper floor. It was sparse, clean, and cold. The windows faced the sea, a churning grey expanse that seemed to swallow the light. She sat on the edge of the bed, her hands shaking. She pulled her leather satchel from the floor and opened it. Inside was the letter from Blackwood & Associates, dated three days prior. It was signed with a flourish she recognized, the ink still fresh, the paper heavy and cream-colored. *My dear Elara, the estate is yours. Come at once. The house is ready.*

But the house was not ready. The staff were hostile. The master, her uncle, Lord Harrowgate, was missing. And the solicitor, the only person who could verify her claim, was a corpse in a London morgue.

Elara paced the room. The floorboards creaked under her boots. She needed proof. She needed to see the documents. She needed to see her uncle. But if she was an impostor to them, how could she get them to show her the papers? They would lock her in the guest wing until the police came, and by then, the story would be that she had broken in, that she was a fraud, that she had killed Blackwood.

She looked out the window. The rain had stopped, but the mist was thicker than ever. Below, the cliffs dropped sharply into the black water. She could see the shape of a boat, small and distant, rocking in the swell. A thought, cold and sharp, crystallized in her mind. If she could not trust the house, she could not trust the people in it. She had to leave. She had to find an investigator.

She remembered a name. Mr. Arthur Penhaligon. He had written a book on fraud and inheritance, a book her father had kept on his desk. The address was in London, but there was a branch office in Newcastle. She needed to get to Newcastle.

But she could not walk through the front door. Mrs. Thorne would stop her. She would have to go out the window.

Elara moved to the window. It was a sash window, heavy and ornate. She pushed against it. It was locked. She looked for a key, but there was none. She looked at the latch. It was iron, rusted, but sturdy. She picked up a heavy brass candlestick from the bedside table. She did not want to break the glass, but she had no choice. She struck the latch. Once. Twice. The third time, the iron snapped.

She slid the window open. The wind hit her face, cold and wet. The room was on the second floor. Too high to jump. But the ivy. Thick, dark vines covered the side of the house. They were tangled and dry, brittle with age, but they reached down to the stone wall below.

Elara climbed out. The wind threatened to tear her from the ledge. She gripped the ivy, her fingers slipping on the wet leaves. Below her, the stone wall was slick. She had to be careful. One slip, and she would be dead. But the alternative was worse.

She descended, hand over hand, until her feet touched the ground. The impact jolted her ankles. She stood in the shadow of the house, the rain beginning to fall again, soft and persistent. She looked up at the window. A light had flickered on in the room above. Someone had seen her.

Elara ran. She ran across the wet grass, her dress soaking through, her hair plastered to her face. She ran toward the drive, toward the road that led to Newcastle. She did not look back. She knew they would come. She knew they would bring the police. But she also knew that she had the letter. She knew that she had the truth. And for the first time since she had stepped out of the carriage, she felt a small, fierce spark of something that was not fear.

It was resolve.

She reached the road. A cart was coming, loaded with fish, the driver hunched against the cold. Elara raised her hand. The cart stopped. She climbed into the back, sitting on the wet crates, the smell of brine and rot filling her nose. She pulled out the letter from Blackwood. She unfolded it, her hands steady now.

*My dear Elara,*

The ink was black. The paper was cream. It was real.

She looked back at the manor. It was just a shadow in the mist. But she could feel it watching her. She could feel the eyes of Mrs. Thorne, the cold gaze of the portrait, the silence of the missing uncle. They were all there, in the dark.

But she was not in the dark. She was on the road. And the road led to answers.

Elara Vane closed her eyes and breathed in the salt air. She was an heiress. She was an impostor. She was a survivor. And she was going to Newcastle.
