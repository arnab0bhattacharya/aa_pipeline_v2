It was upon a raw, foggy morning in the latter part of November that my friend Mr. Martin Hewitt received a visitor at his comfortable chambers in Craven Street. I was seated by the fire, glancing through the morning paper, when Mrs. Grier, our elderly housekeeper, ushered in a young woman. She was plainly but neatly dressed in a dark serge costume, bearing the unmistakable look of intelligent London womanhood. Her gloves were mended at the fingertips, and she carried a damp, shabby umbrella, but her bearing was unexceptionably quiet and self-respecting.

"You are Mr. Hewitt, the investigator?" she asked, depositing her umbrella in the stand. "My name is Vance. I keep a small florist’s shop in Russell Street, Covent Garden."

Hewitt waved her to the armchair opposite his own and regarded her with his usual alert, good-natured gaze. "Pray be seated, Miss Vance. I see by the small callus on the edge of your right forefinger that you are much accustomed to handling pruning-shears, and the faint yellow stain upon your cuff suggests the pollen of the Lilium candidum. But I will not weary you with trifles. What is your trouble?"

"It is a very singular and uncomfortable trouble, sir," said she, taking the chair. "For the past three years I have supplied cut flowers to the Hotel Belvedere, in the Strand. It has been my practice to deliver the orders myself each morning. Among the standing orders was a singular one. Every morning, without fail, a single white lily was to be sent to Room 412 and placed upon the dressing-table. The order was originally placed by an old gentleman named Mr. Colby, who occupied the room. I never saw much of him, save that he was a frail, grey-bearded man, very precise in his speech, and wore a heavy sealskin coat whatever the weather."

"And this order has been countermanded?" I asked, from my corner.

"No, sir, it has not. That is the strangeness of it. Three months ago, I inquired at the hotel office, as I occasionally did, concerning a slight arrears in the monthly account. The clerk consulted his ledger and informed me, with some surprise, that Room 412 had been vacant for a fortnight. Mr. Colby, it appeared, had left without leaving an address."

Hewitt leaned forward, his grey eyes kindling. "Yet the order for the lily was still standing?"

"Still standing, and paid for every Saturday at the office, in gold, by an unseen hand. I thought it a harmless eccentricity and continued the deliveries. My errand boy, a sharp lad named Tim, took the flower up each morning. He told me that the chambermaids never entered the room; indeed, they told him it was kept locked, and the chambermaid on that floor had strict orders from the manager never to touch it. Tim said the room was always in profound darkness, the heavy green curtains drawn, but that he had merely to turn the handle of the door, which was never locked, place the lily in a glass vase that stood ready filled with water upon the washstand, and depart."

"And yesterday morning?" Hewitt prompted, his fingers drumming lightly on his desk.

"Yesterday morning, Tim was turned away. He went up the grand staircase as usual, but upon reaching the fourth-floor corridor, he was met by a porter he had never seen before—a heavy, sullen-looking man, with a low, receding forehead and a curious, deep scar across his chin. This man stood squarely in the corridor and told Tim, with a rough oath, that no more flowers were wanted, and that if he showed his face there again he would be thrown down the stairs. The boy, frightened by the man’s manner, came back to me. I was greatly vexed, not only by the affront to the boy, but by the mystery of the thing. I went myself to the hotel and asked to see the manager. He assured me that he knew nothing of any new porter, and that Room 412 was still officially vacant. He even sent a clerk to the fourth floor, but the corridor was entirely empty, and the door of 412 was fastened with a padlock which the manager declared had been placed there by the proprietors for structural repairs."

"And the scarred man?"

"Vanished, sir, as if the thick carpets had swallowed him."

Hewitt arose and fetched his ulster. "You have done very well to come to me, Miss Vance. The case has elements which are ugly, though I dare say we shall soon get to the bottom of it. You say the curtains of the room are always drawn. Are they of a heavy green rep?"

"Yes, sir."

"And the hotel faces south, does it not, with a fire-escape balcony running along the fourth floor?"

"Why, yes," said Miss Vance, with surprise.

"I thought as much. Now, Cyril," Hewitt turned to me, "you have nothing pressing this morning. I propose that we pay a visit to the Hotel Belvedere. Will you accompany me?"

We walked the short distance to the Strand. The morning fog had thickened, and the omnibuses loomed like shapeless monsters through the yellow murk. Arrived at the Hotel Belvedere, Hewitt did not enter by the main doors. He passed round the corner into a narrow side alley, which led to the mews and the iron staircases of the fire-escape.

"Observe, Cyril," he said, pausing at the foot of the damp iron steps. "The frost has lain thick these two days past. Yet here, upon the third and fourth steps, the frost is entirely cleared, and the iron is wet, as if recently trodden by heavy boots."

We ascended to the fourth-floor balcony. The metal grating creaked under our weight. Hewitt walked softly along until we reached a window corresponding to Room 412. The heavy green curtains were closely drawn, but at the junction of the two centre panels there was a tiny rift.

Hewitt applied his eye to the gap for a full minute. Then he drew back, his face very grave.

"Look, Cyril," said he.

I peered through. The interior was in deep twilight, but I could make out the dim outlines of the furniture. Upon the bed there lay the figure of a man. He was fully dressed, even to his boots, and his face was half-turned towards the window. Even in that dim light I could see that he was a man of advanced years, with a grey beard. He lay perfectly, ominously still, and on the dressing-table before him stood the glass vase, empty now of its bloom.

"He has been dead some hours," whispered Hewitt. "The scarred man who turned away the florist’s boy was no hotel porter, but a watch, set to prevent discovery. We must force an entrance here, Cyril, without an instant’s delay."

With the aid of a jemmy he always carried in his pocket, Hewitt forced the catch of the French window. In a moment we had stepped into the chill, close air of the room. The man on the bed was beyond all human aid. It was the frail old gentleman of the sealskin coat. Upon his breast, pinned to the lapel of his coat, was a small piece of white cardboard, upon which was written in a cramped, uneducated hand: "He talked too much of the Western Railway shares."

Hewitt glanced at the paper and then at the dead man’s hand, where a large signet ring was missing. "Robbery was partly the motive, Cyril," he remarked, "but the card is the real signature of a revengeful mind. This is a case for Inspector Larkins of the Yard."

By evening, the scarred man had been apprehended at a low lodging-house in Bermondsey. He was a discharged railway employee named Griggs, who had borne a bitter grudge against the dead man, Colby, whose evidence years before had led to his dismissal and imprisonment for embezzlement. The padlock upon the corridor door, placed there by Griggs under a forged order, had given him undisturbed possession of the room and the body for nearly three days. It was the singular persistence of a daily white lily, paid for in advance by a man already in his grave, that had brought the hidden crime to light.
