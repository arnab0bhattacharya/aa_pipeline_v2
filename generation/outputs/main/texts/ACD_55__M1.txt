The autumn of 1893 had laid a damp and melancholy hand upon the county of Worcestershire, and the village of Nether Wyckham seemed to crouch beneath the grey skies like a wet hen. It was in this dismal season that I found myself seated opposite Sherlock Holmes in our Baker Street chambers, a letter in my hand which I had received from an old college acquaintance, the Reverend Arthur Penrose. The letter was of a nature to arrest the attention of even the most casual observer, but to Holmes, who was at that time suffering from the ennui of a crimeless week, it appeared as a veritable tonic.

“You read it, Watson,” he said, waving a long, thin hand towards the document. “Your narrative style is invariably more picturesque than my own dry recital of facts.”

I cleared my throat and did as he requested. “It concerns a theft at the parish church of St. Michael’s, in Nether Wyckham. The object in question is the silver communion chalice, a piece of some antiquity and considerable value. The village, it seems, is in an uproar. A family of gypsies—the Petulengros, who encamp on the common—have been accused of the crime. Their reputation is not spotless, and the local constabulary has been searching their caravans with a will.”

Holmes steepled his fingers. “A natural assumption, though rarely a correct one. The obvious suspect is usually the scapegoat. Proceed.”

“The Reverend Penrose, however, is a man of liberal mind. He doubts their guilt. He notes that the gypsies have been in the vicinity for a fortnight and have been scrupulously honest in their dealings. But he mentions two other circumstances which he feels may be relevant. Firstly, Mr. Silas Grimes, the churchwarden, has recently discharged a heavy debt of honour to a money-lender in Worcester—a sum of fifty pounds, which it was thought he could ill afford. Secondly, the bell-ringers, who were practicing for the Harvest Festival on the night of the theft, swear that at the stroke of midnight, as they descended from the tower, they heard the distinct sound of a horse’s hooves upon the stone path leading to the vestry door.”

Holmes sprang from his chair and paced the room. “Excellent! Excellent! The vicar is a man of perception. The debt is a motive, the horse is a means of escape, and the gypsies are a blind. We must go to Nether Wyckham, Watson. The game is afoot, though the quarry is not yet in sight.”

We took the train from Paddington the following morning, the drizzle giving way to a watery sun as we steamed into the Worcestershire countryside. Nether Wyckham was a cluster of grey stone cottages gathered around the spire of St. Michael’s, which rose like a finger pointing a silent accusation at the heavens. The air smelled of wet earth and decaying leaves.

We were met at the station by the Reverend Penrose, a nervous, bird-like man with kind eyes and a stoop.

“Mr. Holmes, Dr. Watson, this is a sad business,” he said, wringing my hand. “The Bishop is most distressed. The village is divided. The gypsies have been threatened with violence, and Mr. Grimes is most indignant that his financial affairs should be mentioned.”

“We shall endeavour to restore the balance, Mr. Penrose,” Holmes said briskly. “Take us first to the scene of the crime.”

The church of St. Michael’s was a fine example of Early English architecture, though the vestry door was a sturdy, iron-studded affair of a later date. Holmes examined the lock with his glass.

“Picked,” he said curtly. “A professional job, but not a clumsy one. There are scratches upon the brass, fresh ones. Now, show me where the chalice stood.”

We entered the chancel. The silver had stood in a niche behind the altar. Holmes examined the dust upon the shelf.

“The thief was careful not to disturb the dust elsewhere, but here there is a distinct smear. He wore gloves. And observe, Watson, the height of the shelf. A man of average stature could reach it easily, but he would have to stretch. If he were mounted upon a horse, as the bell-ringers suggest, he would not be able to reach the chalice from the saddle.”

“He might have dismounted,” I suggested.

“He might,” Holmes conceded, “but then why the haste? Let us speak to the bell-ringers.”

We found the ringers in the local inn, consuming ale with the solemnity of men engaged in a religious rite. There was old Higgs, the foreman, a man with hands like hams and a voice like a foghorn.

“Aye, we heard the horse,” Higgs said, wiping foam from his lip. “A sharp clip-clop on the flagstones. It was just as we come down the stairs. Midnight it was, for the clock struck the hour over our heads.”

“Did you see the animal?” Holmes asked.

“Nay, it were too dark. But it were a shod horse, not a cob. Sounded like a thoroughbred, it did. And it went off towards the Worcester road, not towards the common where the gypsies be.”

Holmes nodded. “And Mr. Grimes? He is a horseman, I hear.”

“Aye, he rides a grey mare,” Higgs said. “But he’s the churchwarden, sir. He wouldn’t steal the holy cup.”

“Men have been known to mortgage their souls, Higgs, let alone their church plate,” Holmes replied enigmatically.

We left the inn and walked towards the manor house where Grimes resided. The churchwarden was a stout, red-faced man with the aggressive manner of one who has been recently embarrassed.

“I have paid my debts, sir, and that is my own affair!” he snapped when Holmes gently broached the subject. “As for the chalice, I shall have it back if I have to pull the village down about its ears. Those gypsies have it. I warrant we shall find it in one of their pots.”

“You ride a grey mare, Mr. Grimes?” Holmes asked innocently.

“I do. What of it?”

“Nothing, save that she is a fine animal. I saw her in the paddock. Her shoes are new.”

Grimes looked suspicious but said nothing. We took our leave.

“The man is a boor,” I remarked as we walked back towards the village.

“He is frightened, Watson. But he is not our thief. Look at his boots. He wears a size ten, and the mud upon them is the red clay of his own paddock. The footprints we saw by the vestry door were made by a smaller boot, and the mud there is the grey loam of the riverbank.”

We made our way to the common, where the Petulengro family had pitched their painted caravans. The scene was one of squalid dignity. A fire burned in the centre of the camp, and a woman with eyes like jet beads sat mending a harness. A tall man, presumably the patriarch, stood watching us approach.

“We are looking for the missing chalice,” Holmes said, addressing the man directly. “The village says you have it.”

The man, Jasper Petulengro, spat into the fire. “The village says many things, master. It says we steal the children and the sheep. Now it says we steal the silver. We have nothing of yours.”

“You have horses,” Holmes observed. “Fine ones, too.”

“We trade in horses,” Petulengro said. “It is our way.”

“Did you sell a horse recently? A grey, perhaps, or a bay with a white star?”

Petulengro looked at Holmes sharply. “We sold a bay three days ago. To a man from the town. He paid well.”

“Describe the man.”

“Small. Dark coat. He walked with a limp. He did not give a name.”

Holmes turned to me. “The limp, Watson! And the size of the boot! We are on the scent.”

We left the gypsies and hurried back to the churchyard. The light was failing now, and the shadows of the yew trees stretched long across the grass. Holmes led me not to the vestry, but to the old lych-gate.

“Observe, Watson,” he whispered. “The earth is soft here. Do you see these tracks? They are the same as those by the vestry. But look where they lead. Not to the road, but to the vicarage garden wall.”

We crept along the wall. Suddenly, Holmes stopped and held up his hand. From the other side came the sound of a shovel striking stone.

We peered over the wall. In the gloom, a figure was digging frantically in a flower bed. It was the verger, a quiet, unassuming man named Caleb Thorne, whom I had barely noticed during our inquiries.

“Lost something, Thorne?” Holmes called out.

The man froze, then dropped the shovel and made a bolt for the gate. But Holmes was upon him in an instant, tripping him neatly onto the turf. I rushed to assist, and together we pinned the struggling man down.

“It’s no use, Thorne,” Holmes said calmly. “We know about the horse you borrowed from the inn, and the limp you feigned to throw suspicion on the gypsies.”

Thorne ceased his struggles and began to weep. “It was the debt,” he sobbed. “I owed money to the same man as Grimes. I thought... I thought if the gypsies were blamed, no one would look at me. I sold the chalice to a dealer in Birmingham. I have the money here.”

He produced a heavy purse from his coat. Holmes took it and handed it to me.

“You have been a fool, Thorne,” Holmes said, helping the wretched man to his feet. “But the law will decide your punishment. As for the gypsies, they have been vindicated.”

The following morning, as we prepared to leave Nether Wyckham, the sun shone with a watery brilliance upon the wet fields. The Reverend Penrose was effusive in his gratitude.

“You have saved my conscience, Mr. Holmes,” he said. “And you have saved an innocent family from persecution.”

“A simple matter of deduction, Mr. Penrose,” Holmes replied, adjusting his scarf. “The debt was a common factor, but the horse was the key. Grimes rode his own mare, which would have been recognized. Thorne, to hide his tracks, hired a horse he did not own and rode to the church, dismounted, and stole the plate. The limp was a disguise, but the tracks betrayed him.”

As the train pulled out of the station, Holmes lit a pipe and gazed out at the passing hedgerows.

“A petty crime, Watson,” he said, “but it illustrates a universal truth. The most obvious suspect is rarely the culprit, and the most quiet man in the village is often the one with the loudest secrets.”

“Indeed,” I agreed. “And the gypsies?”

“Are free to pursue their nomadic existence, Watson. Which is more than can be said for poor Thorne, who will find his horizon somewhat more restricted for the next few years.”

Holmes closed his eyes, and I knew that the case of the Nether Wyckham chalice was already filed away in that vast and orderly index of crime which he kept in his remarkable brain. For myself, I felt a quiet satisfaction in the knowledge that justice had been done, and that the silver cup would once again gleam upon the altar of St. Michael’s.
