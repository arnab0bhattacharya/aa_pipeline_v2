The heat of the afternoon lay upon the station of Puerto Fuego with a weight that seemed almost material, a palpable oppression that pressed upon the chest and dimmed the edges of the world. It was the hour of siesta, when the very dogs had abandoned the streets, and the few souls to be seen moved with the languid, sliding gait of men who have long since ceased to struggle against the equatorial sun. Yet there was a certain unnatural stillness upon the platform, a silence that was not of sleep but of expectation. The crowd of citizens—merchants in their alpaca coats, women with fans of peacock feathers held motionless in their laps, clerks and idlers of every degree—had gathered in a compact, dark mass under the corrugated iron roof, their faces turned towards the empty line of rails that cut a silver path through the shimmering heat of the plain.

At the edge of this assembly, somewhat apart from the rest, stood a tall man in a suit of grey linen, whose pale, European face was marked by the deep lines of the high altitudes and the years of solitary labour. This was Mr. Harwick, the engineer. He had come down from the mountains only the day before, bringing with him the dust of the Sierra Blanca and a lingering chill in his bones that the coastal dampness could not quite dispel. He stood with his hands clasped behind his back, his gaze fixed upon the horizon with an intensity that made the idle chatter of the natives seem like the buzzing of flies. For him, this moment was not merely a civic event; it was the culmination of seven years of suffering, of malaria, of mutinies among the coolies, of the constant, grinding friction of the incompetent bureaucracy in the capital. It was the validation of a faith.

He had believed in the railway. He had believed that the iron road would be a ligament of civilization, binding the scattered, starving villages of the interior to the commerce of the world. He had seen in his mind’s eye the corn of the valleys flowing down to the ships, and the tools of progress ascending to the miners. It was a grand, utilitarian vision, clean and bright, and he had pursued it with the cold fanaticism of the mathematician who sees only the solution, not the human cost. Now, as he stood there, he felt a sense of possessive pride in the very rails, which gleamed with the fresh polish of use. They were his. The whistle, when it came, would be a summons to his own triumph.

It began as a vibration, a tremor in the soles of his boots that sent a ripple of electricity through the crowd. A low, mournful sound drifted across the plain, growing rapidly into a piercing shriek that shattered the stagnant air. The crowd surged forward, pressing against the iron railing with a sudden, collective excitement. Harwick did not move. He watched the smoke, black and voluminous, rising against the white glare of the sky like a signal of war. Then the locomotive itself burst into view, a monster of black iron and brass, breathing fire and steam, dragging behind it a string of carriages that seemed impossibly long.

But as the train slowed, its brakes hissing like a giant serpent, a strange pall fell over the gathering. The expected cacophony of cheering voices, of market women crying out to their cousins in the hills, of merchants shouting for their goods, did not materialise. Instead, a murmur of unease ran through the throng, a sound like wind moving through dry leaves.

Harwick stepped forward, his eyes narrowing against the glare. The doors of the carriages were not opening to release a tide of farmers and traders. Instead, the windows were filled with faces that were not the faces of the people of the Sierra. They were young, hard, brown faces, uniform in their expression of bored fatigue. And then he saw the glint of sunlight on steel. The doors slid back with a clatter, and the platform was invaded—not by commerce, but by force.

Soldiers. They poured out of the carriages in a grey and khaki stream, their boots striking the wooden planks with a rhythmic, martial thunder. They were the troops of the Republic, the instruments of the President’s will, and they moved with a discipline that was utterly alien to the slack, chaotic life of the coast. They carried Mauser rifles, the stocks polished to a dull sheen, and their belts were heavy with ammunition. There were hundreds of them, an endless column of armed men, their bayonets forming a bristling thicket that caught the afternoon light and threw it back in cold, sharp flashes.

Harwick stood frozen. He felt a sudden, inexplicable chill that had nothing to do with the altitude. He looked at the soldiers, and he looked at the line he had built. He saw how the carriages, designed to carry the produce of the earth, were now packed with the machinery of death. He saw how the sidings, meant for the loading of ore, were being used to offload crates of shells and powder. The railway was functioning perfectly. The engineering was sound. The gauge was true. But the cargo was wrong.

An officer, a man with a monocle and a sword at his hip, stepped down from the first-class carriage and barked a command in a harsh, guttural dialect. The soldiers formed up, their movements precise, their faces impassive. They were not looking at the beauty of the coast, nor at the wonder of the train; they were looking at the crowd with the predatory assessment of men who have come to impose order upon chaos.

Harwick turned his head slowly and looked at the people of Puerto Fuego. The excitement had drained from their faces, replaced by a mask of sullen apprehension. The women had lowered their fans. The merchants had stepped back. They knew what this meant. It meant that the rebellion in the mountains, the petty squabbles of the caudillos, had reached a point where the capital had decided to crush them. It meant that the railway, the friend he had brought to them, was now a leash around their necks.

He felt a profound, crushing sense of irony, a moral vertigo that made the platform seem to tilt beneath his feet. He had wanted to bring them prosperity. He had wanted to bring them the light of the modern world. He had spent his youth and his health hacking a path through the rock, dreaming of the day when the whistle of the train would sound like the trumpet of progress. And here it was. The trumpet had sounded, but it was the trumpet of the apocalypse. He had opened the veins of the mountains, and the blood that flowed down was not the life-giving current of commerce, but the poison of tyranny.

He watched as a squad of soldiers took up positions at the end of the platform, their rifles resting on their hips, guarding the iron road that led back into the interior. They were guarding his work. They were ensuring that the route remained open for the passage of more troops, more guns, more subjugation. The villages he had envisioned, with their new schools and their bustling markets, would now become garrisons. The corn he had hoped to see would be requisitioned to feed the army.

Mr. Harwick straightened his shoulders, a reflex of his training, but the weight on his soul was heavier than any girder. He looked at the locomotive, which was panting softly in the heat, its fire still hot in its belly. It was a magnificent machine, a triumph of iron and steam. He had built it. He had made it possible.

He turned away from the scene, unable to bear the sight of the soldiers drilling in the dust of his creation. He began to walk slowly towards the exit, his steps heavy, his face pale. The crowd parted to let him pass, but they did not look at him. They were watching the soldiers, their eyes filled with the ancient, weary dread of the conquered. Harwick walked on, a man who had built a bridge to the future, only to find that he had merely paved the way for the executioner. The heat of the sun beat down upon his back, but he felt as though he were walking through a deep and frozen shadow.
