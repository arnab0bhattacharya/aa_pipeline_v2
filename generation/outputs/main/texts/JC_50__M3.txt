The afternoon had closed in with a stifling stillness over the roadstead of Kota-Tiga—a stillness that seemed to hold its breath, as it were, in expectation of the night. I stood upon the gallery of the harbour office, watching the heat shimmer rise from the tiled roofs of the native town and dissipate into the heavy, luminous haze that lay upon the waters like a gauze of woven brass. For three days the vague shape of a ship had been hovering at the extreme edge of the horizon, a mere smudge of darkness against the violent clarity of the sky, neither advancing nor retreating, but lingering there with an ominous indecision. It was as though she were afraid to commit herself to the overwhelming brightness of the open roadstead, or perhaps to the heavy responsibility of our local justice. I had received my orders that morning. The Residency, acting upon telegraphic advices from Batavia, had instructed me, in the stiff, mechanical phrasing of officialdom, to refuse entry to the vessel. She was to be treated as a pestilence. She flew no flag; her name was obliterated or deliberately obscured; she was, in the eyes of the colonial government, a phantom, a thing without a country, and therefore without rights. I was to communicate nothing, to supply nothing, and to turn her away into the desolation of the high seas.

I had carried out these orders with a grim punctuality that surprised even myself, for I am not, by nature, a hard man. I had dispatched the police boat with a verbal message to the stranger, delivered by Sergeant Barendz, who returned two hours later, sweating profusely in his tight uniform, his broad, honest face creased with a perplexity he dared not voice. The ship had not moved. She sat there, a dark, crouching shape upon the trembling water, surrounded by an unnatural silence, as if the very heat had struck her dumb. The sun was setting, and the sky was beginning to bleed into the sea, when a native boat put off from her side, pulling sluggishly towards the shore. I watched it from the gallery, leaning upon the rail, with a vague unease that was not yet fear, but something akin to the premonition of a profound moral test. The boat drew near, and I observed that it contained only one man, a Malay, who rowed with a desperate, stumbling energy, as if pursued by unseen furies. He landed upon the slimy steps of the stone jetty, and after a brief, muttered parley with the sentry, was brought up to the office. He was a small, emaciated creature, with the trembling, hunted look of a man who has been long at sea under a bad master. He held out a folded piece of paper, dirty and crumpled, and as I took it, I noticed that his hand was shaking violently, as though he were in the grip of a fever.

I opened the letter. The writing was cramped, uneven, the characters formed with a laboured deliberation that spoke of weakness or haste. But before I had grasped a single word of its message, a sudden, profound shock passed through me, a shock that seemed to still the very beating of my heart. I recognized the hand. It was the hand of my brother, Elias. I had not seen him for twenty years. I had not heard from him, nor of him, except through the vague, disquieting rumours that drift through the colonial world like the miasmas of a swamp. He had vanished, years ago, into the vast, shadowy interior of the Eastern archipelago, swallowed up by that obscurity which seems to gather, like a fog, around the lives of men who have severed themselves from the moorings of their past. And now, here was his writing, emerging from the dusk of the office like a ghost from a grave, and I found myself staring at the words with a fixed, unseeing gaze, as if they were written in a language I had forgotten. The letter was brief, and it was not addressed to me, but to the harbour master. It was a formal request for provisions and water, couched in the stiff, old-fashioned Dutch of our childhood, the language of the schoolroom and the parental discipline. It mentioned no name, it offered no explanation; it merely stated, in a tone of frigid politeness, that the vessel was in distress, and that the writer, her captain, appealed to the humanity of the port. And then, at the bottom, in a sudden, desperate scrawl, as if the mask of formality had slipped for an instant, there were the words: "I am here. Do not abandon me."

I stood for a long time, holding the paper, while the shadows lengthened in the room and the last light faded from the window. A profound melancholy, a sadness that seemed to have no source and no object, settled upon me. It was not the sadness of memory, though memory was there, hovering at the edge of my consciousness like a half-remembered dream. It was the sadness of a man who perceives, with a sudden, terrible clarity, the inexorable workings of fate, the blind, mechanical cruelty of the world. I had my orders. The government had spoken, and its voice was the voice of a machine, indifferent, impersonal, and absolute. To disobey was to risk my position, my livelihood, the narrow, uneventful existence I had built for myself in this remote corner of the earth. And yet, the thought of turning Elias away, of condemning him to wander the seas in that crouching, nameless ship, filled me with a vague, unreasoning horror. It was not brotherly love, I think, that moved me, for the ties of blood had been worn thin by the long years of separation. It was something deeper, more elemental—a recognition, perhaps, of the profound, mysterious bond that unites all men who have strayed from the path, all men who have, in some moment of weakness or passion, severed themselves from the ordered, respectable world and become wanderers upon the face of the deep. I saw, in Elias's letter, the whole tragedy of the outcast, the mute, inarticulate appeal of a man who has lost his way and cannot return.

The night had fallen, and the room was dark. I could hear the distant, rhythmic murmur of the sea, and the faint, intermittent chorus of frogs from the swamp. I went to the window and looked out. The lights of the town were beginning to appear, scattered, trembling points of fire in the vast, formless darkness. And there, at the edge of the roadstead, a single, faint glow—a lantern, perhaps, hung at the masthead—marked the presence of the stranger. She seemed, in the darkness, to have drawn nearer, as if the night had given her courage. I stood and watched her for a long time, while the heat pressed upon me, and the sweat ran down my face. I was aware of a great struggle going on within me, a struggle between the forces of order and obedience, and that other, darker, more mysterious force that binds a man to his own kind, to the suffering and the sin of the world. I thought of Elias, of the boy he had been, and of the man he must have become. I thought of the long, silent years, the wanderings, the disappointments, the slow, inevitable descent into obscurity and shame. And I thought, too, of myself, of my own narrow, cautious life, of the smug, respectable complacency that had settled upon me like a crust, hiding, perhaps, a deeper failure. We were brothers, after all. We had come from the same stock, breathed the same air, dreamed the same dreams. And now, he was out there, in the darkness, appealing to me, and I was here, in my office, with my orders, and the weight of the government upon my shoulders.

I turned from the window and sat down at my desk. The letter lay before me, a white, accusing square in the gloom. I reached for a match, and by its brief, flaring light, I read the words again: "I am here. Do not abandon me." The flame burned my fingers, and I dropped it with a slight, involuntary gasp. The darkness closed in again, and I sat motionless, listening to the silence. It seemed to me that the whole world was holding its breath, waiting for my decision. I was aware of a profound, almost crushing sense of isolation, as if I alone, in all the universe, was responsible for the fate of that distant, shadowy ship and the man who commanded her. I knew that whatever I did, I should never be the same again. The choice was before me, stark, terrible, and inescapable. And I knew, too, with a deep, quiet certainty, that I had already made it. I rose, and went to the door, and called out into the night for Sergeant Barendz.
