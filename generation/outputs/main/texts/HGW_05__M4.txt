The naturalist, whose name was Pritchard, had come to the atoll of Motu Roa with a specific and, he was prepared to admit, somewhat romantic ambition: to observe the behaviour of the coconut crab in its native state. He was a man of method, with a large, water-stained notebook, a set of collecting jars, and a profound belief that the world, if watched closely enough, would eventually confess its secrets. His companion was the mate of the schooner *Lily of Devon*, a practical and weather-beaten fellow named Gedge, who had a profound contempt for any creature that could not be eaten, sold, or used as bait.

For the first three days, the crabs were a disappointment. They were large, certainly, and their claws made a sound against the coral that was like the striking of a distant anvil, but their behaviour was entirely crab-like. They scuttled, they fed, they quarrelled over fallen fruit. Pritchard filled page after page with careful, dull observations. Gedge, meanwhile, sat in the shade of a pandanus tree, whittling a stick and occasionally remarking that a crab that size would fetch a pretty penny in a San Francisco curiosity shop.

It was on the fourth morning that Pritchard noticed the arrangement.

He had left his hat, a battered pith helmet, on the sand near the tide-line. When he returned for it, he found it surrounded by a perfect circle of small, white shells, spaced with a regularity that was almost geometrical. He might have dismissed it as a trick of the wind, but the wind on Motu Roa was a steady, trade-wind thing, and it did not arrange shells in circles. He looked about. A dozen yards away, a group of the crabs had paused in their foraging. They were not facing him, precisely, but their stalked eyes were turned in his direction. He had the sudden, unpleasant conviction that he was being observed.

That evening, he mentioned it to Gedge.

“Shells,” said Gedge, spitting into the fire. “They’re scavengers. They’ll pile up anything.”

“In a circle?” asked Pritchard. “With the hat precisely in the centre?”

Gedge shrugged. “Maybe they was having a dance. What’s it matter? You want to worry about something, you worry about that tide. She’ll be over the reef by midnight.”

Pritchard let the matter drop, but he began to watch more carefully. The next day, he conducted an experiment. He placed a piece of ship’s biscuit on a flat stone and retreated to a distance of twenty paces. A crab approached, but it did not go straight for the food. It stopped, turned, and made a slow, deliberate circuit of the stone. Then it approached the biscuit, took it in one claw, and carried it not to its mouth but to a small depression in the coral, where it deposited it. Within an hour, three other crabs had arrived. They did not fight. They sat in a rough semicircle, facing the biscuit, and remained utterly still.

“Gedge,” said Pritchard, that afternoon, “I want you to come and look at this.”

Gedge came, wiping his hands on his trousers. He looked at the semicircle of crabs. He looked at the biscuit. He looked at Pritchard.

“They’re waiting for something,” Pritchard said. “They’re waiting for it to—to become something else. It’s as if they were performing a ritual.”

“They’re waiting for it to soften in the sun, you fool,” said Gedge. “Any crab would do that.”

But Pritchard had seen the way the crabs had arranged themselves. He had seen the one that had acted as a scout, the others that had followed. He had seen, or thought he had seen, a division of labour. And he had noticed something else: whenever he sat down to write in his notebook, the crabs would gather at the edge of the clearing and watch him. They did not approach. They simply watched, their eyes on stalks, their bodies motionless. It was, he thought, like being observed by a jury.

The crisis came on the sixth day. Pritchard had constructed a small enclosure of sticks and wire mesh, into which he had placed a number of the crabs for closer study. He had intended to observe their feeding habits. Instead, he observed something that made him sit back on his heels and stare.

The crabs had formed a living pyramid. One had climbed onto the back of another, and a third had climbed onto the first, and so on, until the topmost crab was level with the top of the wire mesh. Then, with a careful, deliberate movement, it had reached out with one claw, hooked it over the top of the mesh, and pulled itself up. It had paused there for a moment, looking back at its companions. Then it had dropped down on the outside and scuttled away into the undergrowth.

“They’re cooperating,” Pritchard said, when Gedge came to see what all the excitement was about. “They’re solving a problem together. That’s not—that’s not crab behaviour. That’s not any behaviour I know of.”

Gedge looked at the remaining crabs, who were now dismantling the pyramid with the same methodical care. “They’re clever,” he admitted. “Cleverer than they ought to be. I’ll give you that.” He scratched his chin. “All the more reason to collect ’em. A clever crab’s worth twice as much as a stupid one. I know a man in ’Frisco who’d pay handsomely for a specimen that can build a pyramid.”

“You’re not listening to me,” said Pritchard. “They’re not just clever. They’re—organised. They’re watching us, Gedge. I’m sure of it. They’re studying us the way I’ve been studying them.”

Gedge laughed, a short, hard laugh. “Studying us? They’re crabs, man. They’ve got a brain the size of a pea.”

“A pea is a great deal, if it’s the right sort of pea,” said Pritchard. He was staring at the edge of the clearing, where a dozen of the creatures had gathered. They were not moving. They were simply there, their eyes reflecting the firelight, their claws held still. “Look at them. They’re waiting.”

“Waiting for what?”

“For us to make a mistake.”

That night, Pritchard could not sleep. He lay in his tent, listening to the sound of the sea and the occasional click of a crab’s claw against the coral. He thought about the circle of shells. He thought about the pyramid. He thought about the way the crabs had watched him writing, as if they were trying to decipher his notes. And he thought about the way they had looked at Gedge, with his collecting jars and his wire mesh, and he wondered if they understood what he intended.

In the morning, he found that the wire mesh of the enclosure had been cut. Not torn, not broken, but cut, with a precision that suggested a tool. The crabs were gone. And on the flat stone where he had placed the biscuit, there was a new arrangement: a single, perfect spiral of small white shells, at the centre of which lay his own pocket-knife, which he had left in the tent.

Gedge was all for setting traps, for smoking them out, for getting the whole lot into barrels and onto the ship before they could do any more damage. But Pritchard shook his head.

“We’re leaving,” he said.

“Leaving? We’ve barely started!”

“We’ve started something,” said Pritchard. “And I don’t care to see how it ends.” He looked at the spiral of shells, at the knife that lay at its heart. “They gave it back,” he said. “They could have kept it. They wanted us to know they could have kept it.”

Gedge stared at him. “You’re mad,” he said. “You’ve been in the sun too long.”

But when they rowed out to the *Lily of Devon* that afternoon, Pritchard did not look back at the atoll. He did not want to see what the crabs were doing. He did not want to see if they were watching him go. He sat in the stern of the boat, his notebook unopened on his knee, and he thought about the pea-sized brain, and the perfect spiral, and the way a single crab had climbed on the backs of its companions to reach the top of the wire mesh. He thought about the future, and he did not like what he saw. The world, he reflected, was a great deal stranger than he had been led to believe, and it did not always confess its secrets to the man with the notebook. Sometimes, it studied him back.
