The dusk had gathered over the goods-yard of K— with that stealthy, thickening oppression which descends upon flat lands in the last days of autumn, as if the very exhalation of the damp earth were weaving a shroud for the waning day. The signal lamps, reddish and blurred in the drizzle, had begun to burn with a sickly glare, like the eyes of wild beasts cornered in a thicket. I stood near the door of the dispatch office, watching the deposit of moisture on the iron handle of the switchgear, and I was conscious of a peculiar chill that had nothing to do with the rawness of the Baltic wind. It was the chill of an inward trepidation, a sudden, profound distrust of the very ground beneath my boots. For fifteen years I had served the Imperial Railways at K—, and if I had any guiding principle, it was an inflexible, almost superstitious reverence for the schedule. The arrival and departure of trains were, to my mind, fixed ordinances of existence, more certain than the decrees of distant ministers. A man might be a patriot or a conspirator, a saint or a fanatic, but at a railway junction, he was merely mass, velocity, and destination. I had prided myself on this detachment, this immaculate civic neutrality, which I imagined elevated me above the petty and dangerous fevers of the age. I was a mechanism in a larger mechanism, and I found in that submission a kind of austere moral safety.

Into this carefully ordered solitude, like a stone cast into a stagnant pool, had fallen my brother, Stefan. He had come from Warsaw two days prior, bringing with him the heavy, unwholesome atmosphere of clandestine affairs. I had not seen him for six years, and the man who sat opposite me in the lamplight of my quarters bore little resemblance to the volatile youth I remembered. He was heavier, his beard was unkempt, and his eyes possessed that fixed, burning intensity which I had learned to associate with men consumed by a single, destructive idea. He was, I knew, entangled in the meshes of the revolutionary committees—those shadowy brotherhoods that spun their plots in the back rooms of print-shops and the cellars of disaffected artisans. We had spoken little. His mission, he informed me, was not one of social converse. He had come to impart an instruction. The mail-train from the East, due at K— at eleven-fifteen, was carrying a prisoner of state to the fortress at the capital. The man was of immense consequence to the cause. A delay of fifteen minutes—no more, no less—would permit certain individuals, waiting in the woods beyond the water-tank, to cut the line and effect a liberation. The necessity was urgent, the opportunity unparalleled. And I, in my official capacity, was to provide the delay.

I had listened to him without a word, staring at the grain of the wooden table as if it were a map of an unknown, hostile country. He had not pleaded; that was not his manner. He had merely stated the requirements of a superior necessity, implying that my own preferences in the matter were irrelevant, a trivial obstacle to be swept aside by the imperious march of human destiny. When I had at last raised my eyes to his, I found in them a chilling, impersonal expectation. "It is a small thing, Alexei," he had said, his voice soft, almost tender, yet carrying the resonance of an absolute judgment. "A mere technicality. A signal misunderstood. The misplacing of a switch. The railway is full of such accidents."

"A small thing," I had repeated, tasting the words. I wanted to say that there were no small things in the administration of a railway, that an error was an error, and an error of fifteen minutes on a single track was a monstrous, unthinkable violation of my oath. But the words had withered in my throat. I was aware, with a sudden, sickening clarity, that his morality and mine inhabited different universes, and that to him, my sacred schedule was merely a tool, a foolish toy to be broken for the sake of a grander design. "You ask me to betray my trust," I had managed to say, but he had only smiled, a slow, weary smile that seemed to acknowledge my weakness while condemning my blindness. "I ask you to wake up, brother," he had replied. "You are asleep in the machinery of a tyranny. Do not let them turn you against your own blood."

And now he was gone, having melted into the damp evening as silently as he had arrived, leaving me alone with the ticking of the station clock and the weight of his command. The telegraph instrument in the dispatch office began to chatter, a dry, metallic staccato that sounded in my ears like the mocking laughter of fate. I walked slowly back into the room, my limbs heavy, as if I were wading through deep water. The operator, a young man with a pale, pimpled face and watery eyes, looked up at me with that vacuous, bovine expression which always irritated me. "The express is approaching the junction, sir," he said. "Running to time."

"Very good," I heard my own voice say. It sounded distant, thin, as if it belonged to another man. I took up the lamp and went out again onto the platform. The night had closed in completely now, and the rain fell in a steady, fine drizzle, blurring the outlines of the tracks and the distant semaphores. I could hear, faintly, the approaching rumble of the train, a low, rhythmic pulsation that seemed to vibrate in the very marrow of my bones. It was the sound of order, of the established, of the irrevocable. And I was to break it. I was to introduce chaos into this perfect mechanism, to set a flaw in the crystal of my own existence. For what? For a man I did not know, for a cause I did not understand, for a brother who had looked at me with the eyes of a stranger. I thought of the prisoner, sitting in his guarded compartment, perhaps gazing out at the flying darkness, a man whose life or death was of no more consequence to the vast, indifferent universe than the falling of a single raindrop. And yet, to Stefan, that life was a beacon, a sacred flame worth extinguishing a hundred such as me to keep burning. I could not comprehend it. I could only feel the pressure of his will, a crushing, invisible weight upon my shoulders.

I walked to the end of the platform and stood beside the great iron lever that controlled the main switch. The metal was cold and wet beneath my hand. The rumble was louder now, resolving itself into the definite clatter of wheels and the heavy, labored breathing of the engine. A point of light appeared down the track, growing rapidly, splitting the darkness with its piercing white eye. It was coming. It was running to time. All I had to do was to leave the switch as it was, to let the train sweep past this wretched junction and carry its burden on to the capital, to the fortress, to the fate that the authorities had decreed. I had only to do nothing. But my brother's face swam before me in the mist, and his voice echoed in my ears, and I knew that if I did nothing, I would be committing a different kind of treachery, a treachery against my own flesh, against the mute, unreasoning loyalty that binds a man to the cradle and the hearth. It was not a political choice; I had no politics. It was an act of desperate, irrational kinship, a surrender of the self to the primitive, tribal claim.

With a convulsive effort, as if I were tearing my own heart from my breast, I threw my weight against the lever. It resisted for a moment, then yielded with a harsh, grinding shriek that seemed to tear the night apart. I had done it. The switch was set for the siding, the dead end where the empty coal trucks stood. The train would be forced to halt. I stood back, panting, my hands trembling violently. The engine's whistle screamed, a long, mournful wail of protest, and I heard the screeching of brakes, the hissing of steam, and then, with a final, shuddering lurch, the train came to a standstill, its dark bulk looming over the platform like a wounded leviathan.

For a moment there was silence, broken only by the hissing of the engine and the steady drip of rain. Then voices began to shout, angry, perplexed voices, and the sound of running feet. I remained motionless by the switch, staring at the great black shape of the locomotive, feeling a strange, terrible exaltation mingled with a profound, annihilating shame. I had broken the schedule. I had betrayed my trust. I had stepped out of the safe, ordered world of the railway into the dark, turbulent chaos of human passions. And I knew, with a cold, clear certainty that froze my blood, that I could never go back. The deed was done, irrevocable, a flaw in the crystal that could never be repaired. I was no longer a servant of the order. I was a man with a brother, and a guilt, and a future as dark and uncertain as the night that pressed in upon me from all sides.
