The Princess Isadora of Valdris was not beautiful, in the way that the common people understood beauty, which was a crude affair of symmetry and soft flesh. She was beautiful in the way that a blade of ice is beautiful, or a thunderstorm: with a terrible, arresting clarity that demanded to be looked at, and then quickly looked away from. She sat upon a throne of white marble, in a hall that smelled of lavender and old money, and she had refused them all. The King of the North, with his fur-lined cloak and his hands stained by the blood of wolves, had been turned away. The Arch-Magister of the East, whose robes were woven from starlight and silence, had been dismissed with a wave of her pale hand. She had declared, with the casual cruelty of a child breaking a toy, that she would marry only the man who could bring her something she had never seen.

The courtiers smiled. They are the only creatures in the world who smile at a dilemma, for they assume that the solution is merely a matter of waiting for the correct shoe to arrive. The King of the North brought a diamond the size of a swan’s egg, cut so finely that it held a storm inside it. "I have never seen such light," the King boasted, his voice trembling with the effort of holding back his greed.

Isadora looked at the stone. It was a very nice stone. It was a very common stone. "I have seen light," she said, with the bored precision of a woman reading a menu. "I have seen it in the eyes of my tutors, in the fire of my hearth, and in the tears of my mother. You have brought me a reflection, sir, not a novelty. Take it away. It is heavy and it hurts my eyes."

The King of the North left, his dignity crushed under the weight of his own expectation. Then came the Arch-Magister, floating slightly above the floor, for he had learned to levitate as a mark of his superiority. He held up a small, glass sphere. Inside, a miniature galaxy swirled, a perfect, deadly spiral of purple and gold. "A window to the infinite," he whispered, his voice like the rustle of dry leaves. "I have never seen such a depth. It is the universe, condensed."

Isadora leaned forward. The marble under her fingers was cold. "The universe is a very dull place," she observed. "It is too large. It has no manners. It does not stop to listen to me. You have brought me chaos, sir, dressed up as order. I have seen chaos. It is what happens when a servant drops a tray. It is what happens when a husband lies. Go."

The Arch-Magister vanished in a puff of violet smoke, leaving behind the scent of ozone and embarrassment. The courtiers laughed, a low, tinkling sound, like glass beads dropping onto a table. They knew the game. They knew that the Princess was not looking for a gift. She was looking for a failure. To be chosen was to be bound, and Isadora preferred the freedom of being unchosen. She preferred the safety of being impossible.

It was on the third day, when the sun had begun to bleed into the forest beyond the palace walls, that the shepherd boy arrived. He was not a prince in disguise. He was not a beggar with a secret. He was merely a boy, with dirt under his fingernails and a tunic that had been mended so many times it was more thread than cloth. He did not walk; he shuffled, for his feet were bare and the marble floor was cruel to them. He held nothing in his hands. He carried no jewel, no spell, no wonder.

The guards moved to bar his way, but Isadora raised a finger. "Let him pass," she said. "He looks as if he has been walking a long way. And I am bored."

The boy came to the foot of the dais. He did not look up. He was looking at the floor. In the polished white marble, which was so clean it seemed to glow, there was a small, dark puddle. It was a drop of water that had fallen from the crystal chandelier above, or perhaps a tear from the weeping stone of the wall. It sat there, a perfect, trembling circle.

The boy reached into his pocket and pulled out a small, flat mirror, no larger than a coin. He held it over the puddle. The reflection of the puddle appeared in the mirror, and the reflection of the mirror appeared in the puddle, and the reflection of the puddle in the mirror, and so on, until it became a dizzying spiral of dark and light, a tunnel of nothingness that seemed to go down into the earth and up into the sky.

"I have brought you your own reflection," the boy said. His voice was quiet, but it cut through the silence of the hall like a knife. "You have never seen it, Your Highness. You only know it. You have seen jewels, which are hard and cold. You have seen stars, which are far and indifferent. But you have never seen yourself. Not truly. You have only seen what you think you are. This is what you are. It is small. It is dark. And it is alone."

The courtiers gasped. It was an insult. It was a blasphemy. To suggest that the Princess, who was the jewel of the kingdom, was merely a dark, lonely thing, was to invite execution.

Isadora did not move. She stared at the boy. She stared at the mirror. She stared at the puddle. For the first time in her life, she felt a sensation that was not boredom, and not anger, and not the cold comfort of superiority. It was fear. A sharp, prickling fear, like the feeling of a needle sliding under the skin.

She looked at the reflection. It was not beautiful. It was not terrible. It was just a face. A young face, with eyes that were tired, and a mouth that was set in a line of defiance that was, she realized, just a habit. A shield. She had spent her life building a fortress of pride, brick by brick, jewel by jewel, and now she saw that the fortress was empty.

"You are a fool," she said. Her voice was steady, but it was thinner than before. "You are a poor, dirty fool. And you have no right to speak to me."

The boy did not flinch. He simply held the mirror up a little higher. "I am not speaking to you, Princess," he said. "I am showing you. And now you know. And now you must decide what to do with knowing."

The silence in the hall was absolute. It was the silence of a held breath. The King, who had been watching from the shadows, stepped forward. "Guard," he barked, his voice cracking with rage. "Throw this beggar out. He has offended the Crown."

The guards rushed forward, their hands outstretched to seize the boy’s ragged shoulders. But Isadora raised her hand. They stopped.

"Wait," she said.

She stood up. Her robes, of silk and gold, rustled around her. She walked down the steps of the dais. The marble was cold under her feet. She walked until she was close to the boy, close enough to smell the sheep and the earth on his clothes. She looked into the mirror. She saw the boy’s face, and behind it, the dark, swirling depth of the puddle. She saw her own eyes, and for a moment, the mask of the Princess slipped. She saw the girl who was afraid. She saw the woman who was lonely.

"Take it," she said.

The boy looked at her, confused. "Take what?"

"The mirror," she said. "And the puddle. I don't want to see it. I don't want to know. I want to be the Princess again. I want to be the one who is never seen, only seen by others."

She turned and walked back up the dais. She sat on the throne. She smoothed her skirts. She lifted her chin.

"Throw him out," she said to the guards. "And make sure he does not return. If I ever see that face again, I will have his eyes put out. I am not interested in truth. Truth is a vulgar thing. It is for shepherds and saints. I am a princess. I deal in beauty, and beauty is a lie that we agree to tell."

The guards dragged the boy away. He did not struggle. He only looked back at her, once, with a look that was not angry, and not sad, but simply full of a pity that was more dangerous than any curse.

Isadora sat on her throne. The hall was quiet. The courtiers resumed their whispers, their smiles, their comfortable ignorance. The sun was setting, and the shadows were lengthening, creeping in from the corners of the room like dark, silent guests.

She looked at the empty space where the boy had stood. The marble floor was bare. There was no puddle. There was no mirror. There was only the reflection of her own face, distorted and cold, in the polished stone.

She touched her cheek. It was smooth. It was perfect. It was a mask.

"I have never seen it," she murmured to herself, a strange, small smile playing on her lips. "And so I have never really died. And so I have never really lived. How convenient. How safe."

She leaned back into the cushions. The lavender scent was stronger now, cloying, thick, filling the room until it was hard to breathe. She closed her eyes. She did not want to see the dark. She did not want to see the depth. She wanted to be the jewel, the star, the thing that was far and indifferent.

And so she sat, in the marble palace beside the great forest, waiting for the next suitor to arrive, bringing her something she had never seen, so that she could throw it away, and feel, for just a moment, the thrill of being the one who had chosen, and the one who had been chosen, and the one who had been left alone.
