The light in the department store was not the sun, or at least, it did not feel like it. It was a pale, artificial glow that seemed to hover just above the floor, catching the dust motes that swirled in the stagnant air, making them dance like tiny, suspended ghosts. Elara stood behind the counter, her fingers idle on the velvet ribbon, watching a woman examine a hat with the solemnity of a priest inspecting a relic. The woman turned the brim, frowned, turned it again, and finally dismissed it with a wave of her hand that was both dismissive and definitive. Elara felt the motion in her own bones, a slight tremor that traveled up her wrist. It was Tuesday. It was always Tuesday. The days had a way of blurring into one another, a long, unbroken stretch of grey wool, soft to the touch but heavy on the shoulders.

She looked down at her hands. They were pale, thinner than they had been a year ago, when the world had still held the promise of something new. But that was before the dance. Before the music had started, that low, humming pulse that seemed to come from the floorboards themselves. Before he had taken her hand. She remembered the feel of it, not the look of him, not his face, which was a blur of shadow and candlelight, but the hand. The warmth of it. The way it had fit into hers, as if the two had been waiting, patiently, for this particular alignment of fingers and skin.

*He called me Clara,* she thought. *No, that is wrong. He did not know my name. I did not tell him.*

The woman with the hat was still there, now looking at a pair of gloves. Elara watched the woman’s lips move, shaping words that did not reach Elara’s ears. The sound of the shop was a constant murmur, a low tide of conversation, the rustle of silk, the clink of buttons. It was a sound that filled the empty spaces in her mind, pressing against them, trying to keep the memory of the dance at bay. But the memory was persistent. It floated up like a bubble from the depths of the afternoon, breaking the surface just when the pressure was greatest.

Lunch came, as it always did, in the staff room. It was a small, windowless space, smelling faintly of boiled cabbage and damp wool. Elara sat on a wooden chair, the back of which had been scratched by generations of nervous employees. She held a cup of tea, the liquid dark and still. Beside her sat Mrs. Gable, the senior assistant, who was cutting her sandwich into tiny, precise squares.

"The rain is coming," Mrs. Gable said, not looking up. "You can feel it in the legs."

Elara nodded. She did not speak. To speak would be to admit that she was thinking of something else, and that was a danger. She watched the steam rise from her cup, a thin, grey thread that twisted and dissolved. She thought of the floor at the dance. The way the light had fallen across it, creating pools of gold and shadow. The way he had moved, not with the stiffness of the other men, but with a kind of fluid grace, as if he were drifting rather than dancing.

"You are not eating," Mrs. Gable observed.

"I am not hungry," Elara said.

"Hunger is a sign of a healthy body," Mrs. Gable said, with the authority of one who has survived many winters. "Eat the bread. It is fresh."

Elara took a piece of bread. It was dry, hard. She chewed it slowly, feeling the texture of it against her teeth. It tasted of nothing. Outside, the sounds of Oxford Street were muffled by the thick glass of the windows, but she could hear the rumble of a bus, the distant honk of a taxi. The city was moving, turning over its giant wheels, indifferent to the small, quiet thoughts of the women who worked within its walls.

When the shop closed, the air changed. It became cooler, sharper. Elara walked out into the street, her collar turned up against the chill. The evening was falling, a soft, violet twilight that softened the edges of the buildings. The street was crowded, a river of faces flowing in both directions. She moved with the current, her eyes fixed on the pavement, on the pattern of the paving stones.

She had not seen him since the dance. She had told herself she would not look for him. She had told herself that it was better to let the memory fade, to let it become just another impression, like the smell of the hat in the shop, or the taste of the dry bread. But as she walked, she felt a pull, a magnetic attraction that drew her towards the corner of Regent Street. It was a foolish impulse, she knew. But she could not resist it.

She turned the corner. The street was emptying. The lights of the shops were coming on, casting long, orange reflections on the wet pavement. And there he was.

He was standing under the awning of a bookshop, talking to a girl. The girl was wearing a blue dress, her hair pinned up in a way that Elara had never seen. She was laughing, her head thrown back, her face illuminated by the light from the shop window. He was smiling, his head bent slightly towards her, listening with an intensity that Elara recognized. It was the same intensity she had felt at the dance. The same warmth. The same fit.

Elara stopped. Her feet felt heavy, as if they were anchored to the pavement. The crowd flowed around her, a stream of bodies that did not acknowledge her presence. She watched them. The way they leaned into each other. The way the girl touched his arm, lightly, briefly. The way he nodded, as if agreeing with something important.

Time seemed to stop. The noise of the street faded into a distant hum. The only thing that existed was the image of the two of them, framed by the light of the shop window. Elara felt a coldness spread through her, a sudden, sharp clarity. It was not pain, exactly. It was a recognition. A confirmation. The memory of the dance, the warmth of his hand, the blur of his face—it had all been real. But it had been a moment, a single point in time, isolated from the rest of the world. It had been a bubble, and now the bubble had burst.

The girl said something, and he laughed. The sound did not reach Elara, but she saw the movement of his shoulders, the lift of his head. It was a familiar gesture. She had seen it a thousand times in the shop, in the faces of the customers, in the faces of the other staff. It was the gesture of a person who is at home in the world.

Elara turned away. She did not look back. She walked on, her steps quickening, until the rhythm of her walking matched the pulse of the city. The cold air bit at her cheeks. The lights of the shops blurred into streaks of color. She thought of the staff room, the dry bread, the grey tea. She thought of the artificial light, the dust motes, the woman with the hat.

It was better, she thought. It was better to be in the shop, where the light was pale and the air was still. Where the days were long and unbroken. Where the memory could be kept at bay, just below the surface, a dark thing that did not need to be named. She reached her street. The door was locked, as it always was. She knocked. The sound echoed in the silence.

As she waited, she looked up at the sky. The stars were coming out, one by one, faint and distant. They were so far away, they might as well have been in another world. She felt a small, quiet sadness, but it was not the sadness of loss. It was the sadness of realization. The world was large, and she was small. And the moment, whatever it had been, was gone.
