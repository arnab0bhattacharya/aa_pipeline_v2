The year that had passed since the fever first crept, silent and unseen, into the lanes of Wexcombe had been a year measured not in seasons but in silences. I had grown accustomed to a world from which the human voice had been withdrawn, as though some great hand had lifted the needle from the instrument of creation and left the air to hum with nothing but the wind among the beeches and the fretful calling of the rooks. When the last of my neighbours—old Margaret Thorne, who had nursed the sick long after her own strength had failed her—was laid in the churchyard by my own hands, I believed that I had sounded the final depth of solitude; and in truth I had come to love my desolation a little, as the shipwrecked mariner comes at last to love the barren rock that is his only refuge, because it is familiar, and because to leave it were to trust the sea.

I lived, therefore, in a kind of waking dream, tending the garden that fed me, reading the books that remained in the vicarage, and walking each evening to the crest of the hill above the village, whence I could survey the whole of that green and folded country which once had been so populous and was now so still. The cottages stood with their doors ajar and their hearths cold; the mill-wheel hung motionless above the stream; and the great house at Ashdown, where I had gone as a girl to fetch eggs and hear the piano, kept its blind windows turned upon the valley like the eyes of the dead. I had ceased to expect any alteration in this landscape, and had schooled myself to a melancholy peace, telling myself that the world had ended not with a trumpet but with a sigh, and that I, its last and least inhabitant, was but a ghost who had forgotten to lie down.

It was upon an evening in the latter part of October, when the mist was rising from the water-meadows and the light had that amber tenderness which seems to belong to the memory rather than to the eye, that I heard the bells. They came faint and clear across the intervening hills, from the direction of Nethercombe—a village I had never visited, though I had often seen its spire from the ridge—and they rang not the slow and single note of mourning to which I had myself grown used, but a peal, a wild and tumbling peal, as though some hand, drunk with joy or with terror, pulled the ropes without method or restraint. I stood transfixed, my heart beating against my ribs like a bird against the bars of its cage; and then, as the sound died away into the hush of the evening, I was seized with a trembling that shook me from head to foot, and I wept—I, who had not wept for a twelvemonth—as a child weeps who has been long lost and hears at last the voice of its mother.

Yet even as the tears ran down my cheeks there rose in my breast a darker and more dreadful thought, which I could not put away. What if the bells were rung by no living hand, but swung by the wind in some broken tower? What if I should descend into that valley and find only the same desolation I had left, with the added bitterness of a hope betrayed? What if—and here my fancy, ever too quick for my reason, took fire—what if the ringers were madmen, or robbers, or some remnant of that cruel and desperate sort who, in the first months of the sickness, had gone from door to door with violence in their hearts? I had heard such tales before the silence fell. I stood upon the hill until the last light had drained from the sky, divided between a longing that drew me onward like a cord and a fear that held me back like a hand upon my shoulder; and I knew, in the secret chamber of my soul, that the fear was not of what I might find, but of remaining forever in the terrible safety of my solitude, and never knowing whether the world had died or only slept.

I returned to the cottage and made my preparations with a calm that astonished me. I filled a satchel with bread and a flask of water, took my father’s old walking-stick, and wrapped my cloak about me; for the nights were growing cold, and the way to Nethercombe lay over two hills and through a wood of which I knew nothing but its darkness. I confess that when I opened the door and looked out upon the black and silent lane, my resolution almost failed me; the familiar objects of my little world seemed to reproach me for deserting them, and the wind, sighing in the eaves, seemed to whisper of the perils of the road. But the memory of that peal rang in my ears louder than any warning, and I stepped forth, closing the door behind me, and set my face toward the hills.

The night was clear and cold, and the moon, now near the full, cast so brilliant a light upon the frosty grass that I could see my shadow walking beside me, long and thin, like a companion who mocked my errand. I crossed the meadow and climbed the first hill, and from its summit I looked back upon Wexcombe, lying so peaceful in the moonlight that it might have been a village in a dream; and I felt a pang of tenderness for it, as for a place I should never see again. Then I descended into the valley, and the wood closed about me, and for a time I walked in a darkness so profound that I was obliged to feel my way along the trunks of the trees, and the silence was broken only by the rustle of my own garments and the quick beating of my heart. I thought of the wolves that were said to have come down from the hills in the worst winter, and of the stories my nurse had told me of the ghosts that walk in woods at night; but I thought also of the bells, and pressed forward, and would not turn back.

It was near midnight when I came out from among the trees and saw below me the roofs of Nethercombe shining in the moonlight, and the spire of its church rising above them like a finger pointing to heaven. My heart leaped within me, for there were lights in the windows—lights, and the red glow of a fire upon a hearth—and as I stood trembling upon the edge of the wood I heard, borne upon the night air, the sound of voices, and then a strain of music, faint and sweet, as of a violin played by some hand that knew not I was listening. I could not move; I could only stand and gaze, and the tears streamed down my face, and I lifted up my heart in gratitude to that Providence which had not, after all, abandoned the world to silence. Then I gathered my cloak about me and went down into the valley, toward the lights and the voices, no longer afraid, but with a strange and solemn joy, as one who, having long wandered in a wilderness, beholds at last the gates of a city, and knows that beyond them there are human hearts to meet her own.
