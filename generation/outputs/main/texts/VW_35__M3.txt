The leather of the chair, worn thin in the center and cracked at the corners, yielded to him with a sound like a long-held breath released. Colonel Haverford sank into it, arranging his knees, which ached with the damp of the city, and unfolding the newspaper so that the great white sheet rose like a wall between himself and the room. But the print swam. The black letters detached themselves from the page, marching like tiny soldiers across the field of white, only to dissolve before they could form words. It was the light, perhaps; the peculiar yellow light of a London October afternoon, filtering through the heavy curtains and pooling on the mahogany table in a way that made everything look both over-exposed and dim, as if the world were being viewed through a piece of old glass. Or perhaps it was the heat, which was excessive, baked into the room by radiators that hissed and murmured like old men in their sleep.

The room itself was empty, save for the presence of Major Trent, who sat in the far corner by the fire, turning the pages of a magazine with a deliberate, heavy slowness that suggested he was not reading at all but listening—listening to the silence, which was not really silence but a composition of small sounds: the rattle of a spoon against a saucer from somewhere beyond the baize door, the distant ringing of a telephone, the soft ticking of the long-case clock in the hall, and beneath it all, the steady, rhythmic hiss of the gas fire. These sounds floated in the air like motes of dust, which were visible now, turning slowly in the shaft of yellow light, rising and falling as if they were not particles of dirt at all but something alive, something with purpose, though what purpose Colonel Haverford could not say.

He set down the newspaper. The headlines screamed of matters that seemed to belong to another planet: strikes, treaties, the endless reshuffling of men in offices. He had read these same headlines thirty years ago, or headlines identical in everything but the names, and they had mattered then, had seemed urgent and fixed, but now they were like stones thrown into a deep pool, sinking without leaving so much as a circle on the surface. What mattered was the taste of the leather chair, which was bitter and old; the smell of the room, which was the smell of cigars and coal and something else, something sweet and faintly decaying, like the smell of a church; and the weight of his own body in the chair, which was heavier than it used to be, as if age were not merely a passage of time but an accumulation of substance, of memories and losses that settled in the bones and pressed one down into the furniture.

He thought he would eat lunch. The thought was not born of hunger but of a need for movement, for the passage from one state to another, as a ship crosses a meridian and finds the date changed without the sea having altered. He rose, folding the newspaper with a precision that was automatic, a habit of the hands that required no instruction from the mind, and crossed the room. His shoes made a sharp, decisive sound on the polished floor, and he was aware of it, of the sound of his own footsteps, as if he were hearing them from the outside, as if the Colonel Haverford who walked were a figure observed from a window above, moving across a courtyard, growing smaller, and finally disappearing into a doorway.

The dining room was long and narrow, panelled in dark oak, and at this hour almost deserted. Two men sat near the window, eating in silence, their faces bent over their plates with an expression of concentrated privacy, as if the act of eating were something not to be witnessed. Colonel Haverford took his usual table, by the wall, where a small lamp cast a circle of amber light that did not reach the corners. The waiter, a man whose face was so smooth and unlined it seemed to have been wiped clean of all experience, appeared at his elbow.

"Consommé," said the Colonel, "and the sole." He did not look at the menu, which lay on the table like a folded hand. He had eaten the same meal on the same day of the week for so many years that the words had detached themselves from desire and become a form, like the words of a prayer, recited not for meaning but for the comfort of their shape.

The soup arrived, pale and trembling in its white cup, and he ate it slowly, watching the way the liquid moved, parting around the spoon and closing again behind it, smooth, continuous, as if nothing had disturbed it. A drop fell from the spoon back into the cup and sent out a tiny ripple, a circle of rings that widened and vanished. He thought, for no reason he could name, of a stone dropped into water, of the ripples spreading outward, and then of a morning long ago, a morning in 1898, when he had stood on the bank of a river in India, watching the surface of the water break into circles where fish rose to feed, and the air had been thick with a smell of flowers and damp earth, and he had felt, with a certainty that was like the certainty of one's own heartbeat, that the world was full of meaning, that every leaf and every stone was a word in a language he would one day learn to read. But he had not learned it. The years had passed, and the language had remained foreign, its alphabet visible everywhere—in faces, in weather, in the way light fell on a wall at certain hours—but its grammar unreadable, so that he had lived surrounded by signs he could not interpret.

The sole was brought and removed. Coffee came, black and bitter, in a cup so thin that the heat of the liquid could be felt through the porcelain, as if the cup were a membrane and the coffee a pulse. He drank it and sat for some minutes with his hands on the table, looking at the pattern of the cloth, which was a pattern of small blue diamonds on a white ground, repeating endlessly, each diamond identical, so that the eye could find no beginning and no end, only the same shape turning and returning like a thought that will not resolve.

A boy entered. He wore the club's livery, a dark coat with brass buttons, and he carried a telegram on a small silver tray, holding it out before him as if it were something fragile, or something dangerous. Colonel Haverford took it. The envelope was yellow, the colour of the afternoon light, of old paper, of things preserved past their natural life. He opened it with a slow tearing of the flap, and the words inside were brief and plain, printed on a thin strip of paper glued to the page.

The words said that his son was to be married. The bride's name was given, a name he had never seen written, never heard spoken, a name that existed for him only as a collection of letters on a yellow slip, without a face, without a history, without a voice. He read it again. The letters were black and precise, each one distinct, yet together they formed something that was not distinct at all but blurred and shifting, like a reflection in water that has been disturbed. Who was she? Where had she come from? The telegram did not say, and the Colonel did not ask, because there was no one to ask, and because the question, which should have been simple, seemed instead to open onto a vast darkness, a space in which a woman moved whom he could not see, whose footsteps he could not hear, who was at this moment, perhaps, standing in a room he would never enter, touching an object he would never touch, speaking words in a voice that would never reach him.

He folded the telegram and placed it in his breast pocket, beside his watch, and the weight of it, which was almost nothing, seemed to him enormous, as if the paper had absorbed the weight of the event it announced and now pressed against his chest with a steady, insistent force, like a hand. He rose, leaving a coin on the saucer, and walked back through the long corridor to the reading room, where the light had deepened from yellow to amber, and the shadows in the corners had thickened, and the gas fire still hissed its low, continuous note, and Major Trent still sat in his corner, turning the pages of his magazine with the same deliberate slowness, as if the world had not moved, as if nothing had changed, and perhaps nothing had, the Colonel thought, taking his chair again; perhaps a marriage was only a word, a name written on a slip of paper, and the thing itself, the life it implied, was as remote and unreadable as the headlines, as the small blue diamonds on the dining-room cloth, as the language of leaves and stones that he had stood among on a riverbank in 1898 and had never learned to read. The newspaper lay where he had left it, folded, and he picked it up and held it before him, but he did not read it. He held it, and the room held him, and the afternoon passed, and the light went out.
