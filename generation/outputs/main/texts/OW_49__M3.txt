In the matter of correspondence, as in the more delicate art of marriage, Lord Dovetail believed in the absolute necessity of surveillance. He had opened his wife’s letters since the very first week of their honeymoon, an habit he justified to himself on the curious philosophical ground that a woman who is spared the trouble of reading her own mail is thereby left with infinitely more time to devote to the perfection of her toilette. Lady Dovetail, possessed of that sublime and rather costly indifference which is the true mark of high society, was perfectly aware of his espionage, and found it vastly convenient. It saved her from the tedious necessity of answering begging letters, and it lent to her husband’s character a pleasing air of melodramatic authority, which, in a man who was otherwise entirely occupied with the management of a perfectly appointed stable, was rather becoming.

It was on a particularly soft Tuesday morning, when the yellow fog of London had been sufficiently coaxed away by a pale, wintry sun, that Lord Dovetail entered the morning-room holding a singular-looking envelope in his hand. It was of a violent and un-English shade of violet, and it was sealed with a great blotch of crimson wax, stamped with the emblem of a broken arrow.

"My dear," said Lord Dovetail, pausing before the fire, which was composed entirely of foreign woods and smelled faintly of a perfumed garden in the South, "I fear young Lord Cyril Vane has entirely lost his head."

Lady Dovetail, who was engaged in the fascinating task of trying to match a rather obstinate shade of blue silk to an antique Chinese teacup, did not look up. "He lost it some time ago in the conservatory at the Marquess of Steyne’s ball," she murmured, gently rubbing the silk against the porcelain. "He was attempting to quote Swinburne to me, and tripped over a watering-can. It is a great misfortune to have a romantic disposition and clumsy feet."

"He has lost more than his head; he has lost his reason," said Lord Dovetail, unfolding the letter with the careful, critical touch of a man examining a doubtful share certificate. "Listen to this: 'I can endure it no longer. The sun has turned to a dead thing in the sky, and the music of the world is but a tedious noise of grinding stones. Unless you will grant me one hour, one single hour, in the little conservatory behind the Orchid House at half-past four this afternoon, I shall, at that precise moment, put an end to my miserable existence. I have purchased a phial of prussic acid from a chemist in Bond Street, and it lies on my writing-table beside your photograph. Do not think I am jesting. One must be terribly serious about a joke of this nature. Yours in utter despair, Cyril.'"

Lord Dovetail folded the letter neatly along its original creases and placed it on the mantelpiece, weighting it down with a small statuette of bronze. He looked at his wife with an expression of grave displeasure.

"My dear Eleanor," he said, in that tone of voice which he generally reserved for the discussion of a failing investment, "this is a very serious matter. The young man is evidently quite deranged."

Lady Dovetail finally looked up, a faint, amused smile playing about the corners of her mouth. "Deranged, Arthur? I think you are too severe. He is merely twenty-two, and has read too much of the French symbolist poetry. At twenty-two, one is naturally convinced that the appropriate accompaniment to an unrequited passion is a violent death. By thirty, one realizes that a slight cold is far more effective in procuring sympathy. He is not going to poison himself."

"You are entirely too careless," said Lord Dovetail, pacing the length of the Turkey carpet. "The boy is an orphan, and he has just come into a very fine estate in Northamptonshire. His blood is among the bluest in England, and his debts are, I am told, exceptionally heavy. A young man in his position, burdened with a sense of his own theatrical importance and a perfectly unmanageable income, is quite capable of anything. He might actually do it, if only to annoy his creditors."

"Nonsense," said Lady Dovetail, returning her attention to the blue silk. "Cyril is far too vain to die. He has magnificent eyes, and he knows exactly how well they look when they are melancholy. One cannot stare dramatically into eternity with eyes like his; eternity would not appreciate them half so much as the women in the front row at the Opera. He is merely trying to frighten me into meeting him."

"Well, he has succeeded in frightening me," said Lord Dovetail, stopping abruptly before her. "I cannot have a suicide occurring in my orchid house. The scandal would be unbearable, and besides, the plants are just coming into bloom. The heat and the fumes of the prussic acid would be utterly fatal to the Cattleyas."

Lady Dovetail sighed, a little sigh of aristocratic weariness. "You are always thinking of your flowers, Arthur. One would imagine you were married to a Dendrobium."

"I am thinking of my name," he retorted, with an unusual flash of spirit. "If young Vane expires in our conservatory, the fact that he did so over a bed of rare Brazilian orchids will not prevent the newspapers from coupling my name with a scandalous romance. I shall go to the conservatory myself at half-past four."

"And do what, precisely?" asked Lady Dovetail, raising her delicate eyebrows.

"Reason with him. If he is determined to die, I shall point out to him that it is exceedingly bad form to choose a venue that belongs to somebody else. A gentleman should always shoot himself in his own library, or, if he is of a particularly literary turn of mind, in the bathroom of a friend’s country house, where the plumbing is modern and the mess easily attended to."

"You are quite heartless, Arthur," said Lady Dovetail, a little indignantly. "You speak of a human life as though it were a mere matter of plumbing."

"Everything is a matter of plumbing, my dear, if one goes deeply enough into it," he replied, adjusting his cravat before the mirror. "I shall go at half-past four. I shall be armed, naturally, with a stout walking-stick, in case his despair should take a homicidal turn."

At precisely half-past four, Lord Dovetail descended upon the conservatory. It was a magnificent glass structure, filled with a hot, sweet, heavy air that smelled of damp earth and exotic blossoms. The light filtering through the glass was a soft, greenish gold, and everywhere the strange, fleshy flowers of the tropics lifted their gorgeous heads. Lord Dovetail walked down the central aisle, his footsteps muffled on the tiled floor, his eyes scanning the verdant gloom for the tragic figure of young Lord Vane.

He found him at last, seated on a small wicker chair beside the great stone basin where the floating water-lilies grew. Cyril Vane was exquisitely dressed in a suit of dark grey velvet, a single white camellia in his buttonhole. He was holding a small glass phial in his hand, and gazing at it with an expression of profound and somewhat aesthetic melancholy.

"Lord Cyril," said Lord Dovetail, advancing with a severe countenance, "I have read your letter. I must request that you put that phial away."

Young Lord Vane looked up, a start of genuine surprise crossing his handsome face. "Lord Dovetail!" he cried. "I—I did not expect you. I was waiting for Lady Dovetail."

"My wife," said Lord Dovetail, coldly, "is engaged in trying to match a piece of blue silk to a teacup. She does not take your letter seriously."

"She does not take anything seriously," said Cyril, a sudden bitterness creeping into his musical voice. "She is a statue of marble, a beautiful, heartless statue. She laughed at my verses, and she told me that my eyes were too large for my face. I cannot live. The world is a grey and barren desert."

"My dear boy," said Lord Dovetail, taking a seat on the opposite wicker chair and tapping his boot with his walking-stick, "the world is only a barren desert to those who have not got the key to the hothouse. You are merely in love with the idea of being miserable. It is a very charming idea, and I have no doubt it affords you a great deal of pleasure, but it is entirely inconvenient to choose my orchid house as the scene of your demise."

"It is no mere idea," said Cyril, raising the phial dramatically. "See, I have the poison here. In a few minutes, I shall be beyond the reach of her coldness, beyond the reach of everything. I shall be a memory."

"You will be a great deal of trouble to the servants," said Lord Dovetail. "Besides, you have just come into your estate. Think of the inconvenience to your tenants. Who is to manage the draining of the Northamptonshire fens if you are lying here dead among my water-lilies?"

"I do not care for the fens," cried Cyril, passionately. "I care only for her! She is the sun, the moon, and the stars. Without her, I am nothing."

"You are a peer of the realm," said Lord Dovetail, firmly. "That is a great deal more than the stars, which are, after all, perfectly useless bodies, and quite invisible in London. Come, give me the phial. You are acting like a sentimental schoolboy."

Cyril hesitated, his large, beautiful eyes filled with tears. "If I do not die," he whispered, "she will think me a coward. She will think I was merely playing a part."

"She will think you a sensible fellow," said Lord Dovetail, stretching out his hand. "And a sensible fellow is, in this age of absurdity, the rarest thing in nature. Give it to me, Cyril."

There was a long pause. The hot, silent air of the conservatory hung heavy around them. Then, with a sudden, weak gesture, Cyril held out the phial. Lord Dovetail took it, slipped it into his pocket, and rose to his feet.

"You will dine with us tonight," he said, in a tone that brooked no refusal. "And you will sit next to my wife. You will talk to her about the new French plays, and you will tell her that her hair is looking remarkably well. You will not mention death, or the stars, or the barren desert. And tomorrow, you will go down to Northamptonshire and drain your fens. It is a prosaic occupation, but it is wonderfully conducive to longevity."

Cyril rose slowly, looking very pale and very young. "I suppose I must," he said, in a small voice.

"You must," said Lord Dovetail. "And now, if you will excuse me, I have to go and see my gardener about a new heating apparatus. Good afternoon, Lord Cyril."

He left the young man standing there, a trifle foolishly, beside the stone basin, and walked back down the aisle, whistling softly an air from a comic opera. When he reached the morning-room, he found his wife still engaged with the blue silk.

"Well?" she said, without looking up.

"The matter is settled," said Lord Dovetail, replacing the bronze statuette on the mantelpiece. "He has given me the phial. He is dining with us tonight."

Lady Dovetail smiled, a slow, satisfied smile. "Poor Cyril," she said. "I suppose he is terribly disappointed."
