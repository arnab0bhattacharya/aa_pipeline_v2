To suppose that the absence of outward rebuke is invariably a mercy to the offender is to misunderstand the deeper mathematics of human conscience. We are often more heavily taxed by the silent endurance of those we have wronged than we should have been by their loudest indignation; for anger, in its very heat, provides a forge in which our own stubbornness may be tempered, whereas a quiet grief offers no such resistance, leaving the transgressor to turn endlessly in the vacant chambers of his own moral isolation. It was precisely this taxing silence that Markham Merton found himself unable to endure, as he sat opposite his guardian in the wainscoted study at Lowick Rectory, having just announced his intention to abandon the academic life for the unpredictable sweep of foreign travel.

Markham was a young man of undeniably brilliant parts, possessing a mind that moved through classical construction and abstract philosophy with a rapid, luminous ease that had caused his Cambridge tutors to prophesy a stellar university career. Yet this very celerity of thought was married to an impatient spirit, a restless vanity that could not brook the slow, cumulative drudgery required for supreme distinction. He had gone up to Trinity with the expectation of carrying off the Chancellor’s Medal, but he had not sufficiently calculated the dull, unglamorous weight of the second candidate—a plodding Northumbrian lad whose lack of native genius was offset by a mechanical endurance that ground his translations into a flawless perfection. When the list was published and Markham saw his name placed second, the sudden deflation of his pride was a sensation he could not survive. He did not blame his own scattered reading or his late-night indulgences; rather, he conceived the entire machinery of academic competition as a gross insult to his innate capacities. If the laurels were to be distributed by mere endurance, he would gather no more leaves in such barren soil.

The Rectory parlor was scented with the damp, earthy perfume of late November, a chill that crept through the ill-fitting casements, and was mitigated only by the large, peat-driven fire that cast a ruddy, unsteady glow over the worn Turkey rug and the stacks of sermonic folios. Mr. Silas Arabin, Markham’s guardian and the rector of Lowick, was a man who had entered the Church not out of any fiery, proselytizing zeal, but driven by a profound, melancholy sense of duty. He had sacrificed the leaner years of his own youth to the education of his dead sister’s child, stinting himself in secret so that the boy might possess the very best grammars, the most authoritative lexicons, and the unencumbered leisure necessary for intellectual greatness. He had looked forward to Markham’s academic triumph with a quiet, latent passion. It was not the worldly glory of the medal he coveted, but the assurance that his own suppressed intellectual life had found a magnificent resurrection in the mind of his ward. He had built, as it were, a temple to his own sacrificed hopes, and had furnished it with Markham’s promise.

When Markham had finished speaking, laying out his resolution to quit Cambridge directly, to decline the fellowship that might have been his, and to use a small legacy from a distant aunt to finance a journey through the Levant, Mr. Arabin did not immediately reply. He took off his spectacles, breathed upon the lenses with a slow, deliberate puff, and polished them with the corner of his dark kersey waistcoat. The silence was prolonged to a degree that began to feel architectural, as if the very walls of the Rectory were holding their breath.

“Yo’ mun do as yo’ see fit, mun,” said a gruff voice from the shadows near the doorway, where Mrs. Glegg, the rector’s aged and irascible housekeeper, had been listening with an unblinking severity. Her dialect, broad and unpolished, cut through the tension with the blunt edge of a rusted sickle. “It’s a poor thanks for all the brass spent on your book-learning, I reckon. A power o’ Latin and Greek to go a-gadding about in heathen lands! The maister might ha’ kept his pennies in the strongbox, and there’s them as could ha’ put ‘em to a better use than tracing of old stones.”

“Peace, Mrs. Glegg,” said Mr. Arabin, his tone mild but carrying the quiet authority of a man long accustomed to governing his own small parish. “Leave us, if you please, and close the door.”

The housekeeper retreated with a disapproving sniff, and the heavy oak door clicked shut, leaving the two men alone with the ticking of the long-case clock in the hall. Mr. Arabin replaced his spectacles on his long, thin nose, and folded his hands over the worn leather of his breviary. He looked at Markham. There was no anger in his deep-set grey eyes, no twitching of the firm, pale lips to indicate suppressed wrath. There was only a profound, weary sadness, an expression of a man who has suddenly seen a long, arduous road come to an unexpected and precipitous end.

“You have thought deeply on this, Markham?” he asked, his voice pitched in the same measured, deliberate cadence he used for the more solemn parables.

“I have, sir,” Markham replied, his own voice made slightly uneven by the very lack of resistance he encountered. He had prepared for an argument; he had marshaled his reasons, his statistics regarding the unprofitability of a fellow’s life, his disdain for the narrow scholasticism of the colleges. He had not prepared for acquiescence. “I feel that my mind is not suited to the cloister. I must see the world, its living history, before I can settle into any definite groove. The medal—the failure to obtain it—has merely precipitated a decision I was already leaning toward.”

“Do not lay the burden of this departure upon the medal, my boy,” Mr. Arabin said gently. “A man does not forsake a calling because he has been beaten in a single race. He forsakes it because the calling was never truly his. If your intellect, which I have nurtured, and which I believed to be of the highest order, could be so easily derailed by a momentary check, then the fault was not in the university, but in the original grain of the timber. You have chosen to be a traveler, and you must travel.”

Markham felt a sharp, involuntary contraction in his chest. It would have been infinitely easier if Mr. Arabin had upbraided him, had spoken of the pinched economies, the denied luxuries, the long nights spent correcting exercises so that Markham might have the best tutors. A torrent of reproach would have allowed Markham to assume the posture of the injured artist, misunderstood by a prosaic world. But this calm, analytic acceptance was a mirror held up to his own soul, reflecting the uncomfortable truth that his failure was not a stroke of ill fortune, but the natural consequence of an unstable character. Mr. Arabin was not merely forgiving him; he was comprehending him, laying bare the very anatomy of his weakness, and finding him wanting.

“I am sorry, sir,” Markham said, the words coming forth with a clumsy heaviness entirely unlike his usual fluent speech. “I know what you have done for me. I know what you hoped.”

“I hoped to make a scholar of you,” Mr. Arabin replied, turning his gaze toward the fire, where a crumbling peat-block was settling into ash. “But I perceive that I have only equipped a traveler. You shall have your journey, Markham. The small sum your aunt left is yours, and I shall not withhold the quarter’s allowance due at Michaelmas. You shall go to the Levant, and you shall observe the ruins of older civilizations. Perhaps, among the shattered columns of Athens or the dust of Ephesus, you will find a patience that Cambridge could not teach you.”

The young man rose from his chair, his throat aching with a sudden, sharp tenderness that he found entirely unmanageable. He had wanted to escape the heavy, musty air of the Rectory, the oppressive weight of Mr. Arabin’s unspoken expectations; but now, standing in the dim light of the dying fire, he felt a profound reluctance to leave. The silence of his guardian was not the silence of indifference, but the silence of a profound and final surrender. Mr. Arabin was letting him go, not with a blessing, but with a sorrowful understanding that was harder to bear than any curse.

“Good night, sir,” Markham said, his hand lingering on the back of the chair.

“Good night, Markham,” the rector answered, without turning his head. “May God go with you, wherever your wandering steps may lead.”

Markham left the room, closing the heavy oak door behind him with a careful, muffled click. He walked out into the cold, damp passage, listening to the slow, inexorable ticking of the long-case clock, feeling the full, unrelenting weight of a liberty that was beginning to feel remarkably like exile.
