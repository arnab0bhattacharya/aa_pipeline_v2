The rain in London, at least in the City, does not fall so much as it hangs, a grey, damp veil that settles into the lungs and the wool of one’s stockings. It was the evening before the wedding, and the air tasted of coal smoke and impending change. I walked down Threadneedle Street with the particular urgency of a woman who is about to be swallowed up, my steps quick against the wet pavement, my umbrella tilted against the drizzle. I was not going to my fiancé’s house to prepare for the feast; I was going to the office. One last time.

Mr. Halloway, my betrothed, had been most particular about this. He was a man of gentle habits and conservative views, a sort of benevolent patriarch who believed that a woman’s place was in the domestic hearth, and that the typing room was a temporary inconvenience, a stopgap until the proper order of things was restored. He had bought me a trousseau of such exquisite, suffocating softness that I felt I was being packed for a long voyage into a country where no one spoke my language. But the office—well, the office was my own.

I pushed open the door of the firm of Vane, Crisp, and Associates. The bell above the door jingled with a sound that seemed sharper than usual, a metallic note that cut through the ambient hum of the city. The office was nearly empty. The day had passed, the urgent telegrams had been dispatched, the ledgers balanced, and the clerks had vanished into the evening fog, leaving only the smell of stale ink, old paper, and the faint, acrid scent of gas.

I stood for a moment in the doorway, watching the gas lamps flicker to life along the street outside. The light was yellow and wavering, throwing long, distorted shadows across the room. It felt like a different world from the one I was about to enter. In that world, I was not "Mrs. Halloway," a title that implied a certain passivity, a certain subordination to his will. In that world, I was Miss Elspeth Vane, a woman who knew the rhythm of the keys, who could produce a clean, double-spaced letter in the space of a minute, who controlled her own destiny, however small and confined it might be.

I walked to my desk. It was a small, wooden thing, tucked into a corner near the window, but it was mine. I sat down, the chair creaking under my weight, and placed my hands on the keys. They were cold, hard ivory, worn smooth by ten years of use. I did not type. I simply rested my fingers there, feeling the familiar resistance, the precise, mechanical logic of the machine. It was a language I understood better than any spoken word. It was a language of action, of result, of clarity.

A shadow fell across the room. It was Mr. Vane, the junior partner, who had been lingering behind, perhaps to enjoy the solitude or perhaps to check a final file. He was a thin, nervous man, with eyes that seemed to be constantly searching for a fault in the paperwork. He stopped when he saw me, his face a mixture of surprise and a faint, uncomprehending pity.

"Miss Vane," he said, his voice dry as parchment. "I thought you were... well, I presumed you were occupied with more pleasant matters. The wedding, I mean."

I did not look up. "I am finishing up, Mr. Vane."

"Finishing up?" He laughed, a short, dry sound. "There is nothing to finish, Miss Vane. The books are closed. The accounts are settled. You are free."

"Free," I repeated. The word tasted of ash. "Is that what I am? Free to be bound?"

He shifted his weight, uncomfortable in the silence. He was not a cruel man, but he was a man of the establishment, a man who saw the world as a series of fixed positions, like the keys on my machine. A woman was a woman; a typist was a typist; a wife was a wife. The transitions were natural, inevitable, like the falling of the rain outside.

"It is a happy occasion," he said, attempting a tone of warmth that did not quite reach his eyes. "Mr. Halloway is a good man. He will provide for you. You will not have to worry about the cost of living, the rent, the... the drudgery."

"Drudgery," I said, and the word came out harder than I intended. "You call it drudgery? I call it work. I call it my life. I have paid my own way, Mr. Vane. I have eaten my own bread, worn my own clothes. I have never asked anyone for a penny. And now, because I have fallen in love with a man who believes that love is a form of ownership, I am to give it all up. To become a ornament. A piece of furniture in his house."

Mr. Vane looked at me, then at the typewriter, and then at the window, where the rain was streaking the glass. He seemed to be searching for a reply that would satisfy his sense of propriety, but I could see he was coming up empty. He was a man of logic, and what I was saying defied the logic of his world.

"You are young," he said finally. "You will understand later. There is a peace in being provided for. A rest."

"Rest," I said. "Yes. That is the word. I am tired. Tired of being told what to do, who to be, when to stop. I am tired of the silence that comes with dependence. I want the noise. I want the clatter. I want to be useful in a way that I can see and touch."

I stood up, brushing a speck of dust from my skirt. The movement was sudden, decisive. Mr. Vane watched me, his face unreadable.

"I am going to leave," I said. "Not to marry him. Not yet. I am going to keep my job. I am going to keep my lodgings. I am going to keep myself."

He blinked, as if I had spoken in a foreign tongue. "But... the wedding is tomorrow. The invitations have been sent. The guests are expected."

"Then let them be disappointed," I said. "Or let them think I have run away. It does not matter. The truth is less important than the action. I am taking my place back."

I walked to the door, my heart hammering against my ribs, not with fear, but with a strange, electric excitement. It was the same feeling I had when I started a new line, the anticipation of the next word, the next thought, the next life. I looked back at Mr. Vane, who stood frozen in the dim light, a statue of conventional doubt.

"Goodnight, Mr. Vane," I said.

"Goodnight, Miss Vane," he replied, and his voice sounded very far away, as if it were coming from the bottom of a well.

I stepped out into the street. The rain was heavier now, soaking through my coat, chilling my skin. But I did not mind. I pulled my umbrella up, but I held it low, letting the water touch my hair, my face. I walked away from the office, away from the safe, warm, suffocating world of the City, and into the dark, wet, uncertain night. I did not know where I was going, but for the first time in ten years, I knew where I was. I was in the middle of the street, alive, and entirely my own.
