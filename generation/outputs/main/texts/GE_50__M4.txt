It was a fact well known in the village of Cottesby—and, indeed, in the surrounding hundreds—that Mr. Silas Marner, had he been born to a different station, might have been a scholar, for there was no man more tenacious of a difficult point, more patient in the unravelling of a tangled matter, or more apt to hold his tongue concerning what he had discovered, until the proper season for speaking. But being born the son of a wheelwright, and having early taken to the land, he had never been taught his letters; and this deficiency, which in a man of less native shrewdness might have passed unremarked, was in him a source of constant, silent mortification. He could count a flock of sheep as swiftly as any man in the county, and could estimate the yield of a field of wheat with an accuracy that made him the envy of his neighbours; but the written word remained to him a locked door, and the key to that door had never been placed in his hand.

It was therefore with a mixture of pride and inward trepidation that he received the news, in the autumn of the year 1815, that the vestry had elected him overseer of the poor for the parish of Cottesby. The office was one of considerable dignity in those days, when the poor rate was a heavy burden and its administration a matter of daily and anxious concern; and Silas, who had a conscience as tough and serviceable as his own best ploughshare, felt the honour keenly. Yet no sooner had he accepted it than a cold misgiving crept over him. The overseer was required to keep books: to enter the sums received from the ratepayers, the sums disbursed to the poor, the payments for the relief of the aged, the sick, and the unemployed. These books were open to the inspection of the vestry and of the magistrates; and Silas Marner could not read a single line of them.

For some days he said nothing of this difficulty, hoping that a way might open itself. He carried the great leather-bound ledger home with him, and set it on the table in the kitchen, where it lay like a silent accuser. His daughter, Nancy, a girl of two-and-twenty, with her mother’s quick eyes and her father’s steadiness of purpose, observed the book and observed her father’s avoidance of it, and said nothing for a while, after the manner of one who has learned that speech is not always the readiest key to a difficulty.

But at length, one evening in November, when the wind was howling in the chimney and the rain beat against the small panes, Silas took up the ledger with a sigh, and said, half to himself, “I mun have it out wi’ somebody. I canna make head nor tail o’ this.”

Nancy looked up from her sewing. “Father,” said she, “if you’ll let me, I’ll keep the book for you. I can write a fair hand, and I’ve a head for figures.”

Silas was silent for a moment. The proposal touched him in a tender place. He was proud, and the confession of his own incapacity before his daughter was bitter to him; but he was also just, and he knew that Nancy’s offer was made in kindness, and not in the spirit of one who would take advantage.

“Thee’rt a good wench,” said he at last, “but it munna be known. If the parish gets wind that the overseer canna read his own accounts, there’ll be talk enough to set the whole village by the ears.”

“It needna be known,” said Nancy. “I can write the entries, and you can carry the book to the vestry, and no one need be the wiser. I’ll keep a fair copy here at home, and you shall tell me the sums, and I’ll set them down.”

So it was agreed; and Nancy, who had been taught her letters by the old schoolmaster at the next village, and who had a natural aptitude for order and accuracy, began to keep the accounts of the poor rate of Cottesby. She wrote a clear, upright hand, and she had a method of ruling her columns and checking her totals that would have done credit to a clerk in a merchant’s counting-house. Silas, who brought home the rough notes of the day’s business—the sums received from this farmer and that, the payments made to the aged widow, the sick labourer, the family whose breadwinner had been taken by the fever—found in his daughter a secretary after his own heart. She asked no idle questions, and she made no careless entries.

But it was not long before Nancy’s attention was arrested by a discrepancy. In going over the accounts of the preceding year—for Silas had brought home the old books as well, that the new overseer might have a record of what had gone before—she found that the sums entered as paid to the poor did not tally with the sums which the ratepayers had been assessed to pay. There was a gap, small at first, but growing larger as the months went on, between the money that had come in and the money that had gone out; and the gap was not explained by any entry of a balance carried forward or of a sum reserved for a future purpose. It was, in short, a deficiency; and the deficiency pointed, with the silent and inexorable finger of arithmetic, to the man who had held the office before her father.

That man was Mr. Thomas Ketley, a farmer of good standing in the parish, who had been overseer for three years, and who had borne a reputation for sharp dealing in matters of business, though no one had ever openly accused him of dishonesty. He was a man of a plausible tongue and a hearty manner, who clapped his neighbours on the back and called them by their Christian names, and who had a way of making his own interests appear to be the interests of the parish. Nancy had never liked him; but she was not a girl to be guided by mere liking or disliking, and she set herself to examine the accounts with the utmost care.

She found that the deficiency amounted, over the three years, to no less a sum than forty-seven pounds—a sum which, in those days, was sufficient to keep a poor family in bread for a twelvemonth. The entries were not clumsy; they were made with a certain cunning, a little here and a little there, so that no single payment was large enough to excite remark. But the totals told their own tale, and Nancy, who had a mind as clear as her handwriting, saw the whole matter as plainly as if it had been written in letters of fire.

She said nothing to her father for two days. She was weighing the matter in her mind, and considering what was best to be done. She knew that if she spoke, her father would be for going straight to the vestry and denouncing Ketley; and she knew, too, that Ketley was a man of influence, and that the vestry was not always a body of impartial judges. She had heard her father say that Ketley had a cousin on the bench of magistrates, and that he was wont to stand well with the parson. It was a case in which a false step might ruin the reputation of an honest man and leave the thief in possession of his spoils.

At last, on the third evening, she laid the book before her father, and showed him the discrepancy, and explained it in her clear, quiet voice. Silas listened in silence, his brow growing darker and darker. When she had done, he brought his fist down upon the table with a force that made the candlestick jump.

“The villain!” said he. “The mean, thieving villain! And him a-talkin’ o’ the poor as if they was his own children, and a-clappin’ me on the back and sayin’ as how the parish was lucky to have a man o’ my sense at the head o’ things. I’ll have him before the justices. I’ll have him transported.”

“Father,” said Nancy, “you munna go too fast. If you accuse him openly, he’ll deny it, and he’ll have his friends to back him. You mun have the proof so plain that no man can gainsay it. Let me make a fair copy of the accounts, with the sums all set out in order, and let the vestry see it. They canna deny the figures.”

Silas was silent for a long time. He was a man of slow speech but of deep feeling, and his anger was the more formidable for being slow to kindle. At last he said, “Thee’rt right, wench. We’ll do it thy way. But I’ll not rest till that man’s name is struck out o’ the list o’ honest men in this parish.”

And so it was done. The accounts were laid before the vestry, and the vestry, after much head-shaking and many consultations, was compelled to admit that there was a deficiency which Mr. Ketley could not explain. Mr. Ketley, indeed, attempted to explain it, but his explanations were of that vague and shifting kind which serve only to confirm suspicion. He talked of sums advanced to the poor and not entered, of payments made in kind and not in money, of the confusion of the times; but the figures were against him, and the figures were not to be talked down. The matter was carried before the magistrates, and Mr. Ketley was ordered to repay the sum, and was moreover fined for his malversation; and though he retained his farm and his standing in the parish, his reputation never recovered from the blow.

As for Silas Marner, he was re-elected overseer for the following year, and he held the office for many years, and his accounts were always found to be the most accurate in the county. But he never learned to read them; and Nancy, who had saved her father’s honour and the parish’s money, continued to keep the books for him, and to keep them so well that no man ever suspected the secret of the overseer’s clerk. And if you were to ask Silas, in his old age, what he thought of the matter, he would say, “There’s many a man as can read and write as has less sense in his head than my Nancy; and there’s many a one as can cast up figures as has less honesty in his heart than the wench that cast ’em up for me.”
