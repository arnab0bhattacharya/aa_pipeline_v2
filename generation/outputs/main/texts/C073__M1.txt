The coach rattled over the cobbles of the Rue Saint-Honoré, but it was not the familiar, weary jolt of a journey’s end. This was a frantic, juddering motion, as if the driver himself were in a state of terror. Outside the small, grimy window, Paris was not the city of elegant salons and meticulous print shops that Arthur Penhaligon remembered from his last visit a year ago. It was a fever dream. A river of ragged humanity surged through the street, their faces contorted with a rage he could feel through the glass. The roar of their voices was a physical thing, a low, grinding thunder that vibrated through the carriage floor and into the soles of his sensible English shoes.

Arthur clutched the leather satchel on his lap. Inside lay the promissory note, the sole reason for his presence in this maelstrom. One hundred and twenty livres tournois, owed to him by the printer, Monsieur Dubois, for a consignment of paper and ink. It was a sum that would keep his own modest bookshop in London solvent through the winter. Arthur Penhaligon was a man of accounts, of neat columns and balanced ledgers. He had never done a brave thing in his life. His greatest adventures were confined to the yellowed pages he sold: the voyages of Captain Cook, the perils of Robinson Crusoe. His own world was one of predictable routines and the quiet rustle of turning pages.

The coach lurched to a halt, far from its intended destination. The driver, a man with the wild eyes of a cornered animal, shouted through the hatch. “It’s no use, monsieur! The barricades are up near the Palais-Royal. You’ll have to walk from here.” Before Arthur could protest, the man had released the brake and was whipping his horses, turning the vehicle around in the chaotic flow. Arthur was left standing on the pavement, a small, portly Englishman in a brown coat, clutching his satchel as if it were a life raft. The air stank of sweat, coal smoke, and something else, something coppery and sharp that he refused to name.

He navigated by memory and a desperate sense of direction, keeping to the smaller alleys where the crowd was thinner. The roar was always there, a constant backdrop to his thumping heart. Finally, he found it: the narrow street of the Imprimerie Dubois. But the shopfront was a ruin. The glass of the window was shattered, and the elegant gold lettering of the sign had been smashed to splinters. A group of sans-culottes stood guard, pikes resting on their shoulders, their faces grim and watchful. Arthur felt a cold dread wash over him. He had come to collect a debt; he had not come for a revolution.

He was about to turn and flee, to lose himself in the city and find the first coach back to Calais, when a side door to the printing house creaked open. A boy slipped out, no older than sixteen, his face pale and smudged with ink and grime. His eyes, wide and dark, darted around before locking onto Arthur’s. He recognised him.

“Monsieur Penhaligon?” the boy whispered, his voice trembling. It was Luc, Dubois’s apprentice. A bright, clever boy who had once shown Arthur how the new iron presses worked.

“Luc? What has happened here?” Arthur asked, his own voice a dry rasp.

“They have taken him,” Luc said, his gaze flickering back to the guards. “The Committee. They came at dawn. They said his pamphlets were counter-revolutionary. Seditious. They dragged him away.” The boy’s composure cracked, and a sob caught in his throat. “He is at the Conciergerie, monsieur. They are… they are not kind to printers there.”

A wave of pity, sharp and unwelcome, pierced through Arthur’s fear. “My boy, I am so sorry. I… I had come about a small debt. It is of no consequence now.” He made to move, to retreat from this place of danger.

“No! Wait, monsieur, please!” Luc grabbed his sleeve, his grip surprisingly strong. “He spoke of you. He said you were an honest man. A man of your word.”

“I am a bookseller,” Arthur said, a little stiffly. “I deal in words on paper, not in deeds.”

“But these are words on paper!” Luc insisted, his desperation lending him a fierce energy. He darted back inside the ruined shop and emerged a moment later with a heavy, string-bound parcel of papers, perhaps a foot square. It was wrapped in brown oilcloth. “This is the last of it. The manuscript of his masterwork. A history of the rights of man, from the Greeks to now. He was printing the final chapters when they came.”

He thrust the parcel towards Arthur. “You must take it. Back to England. Keep it safe.”

Arthur stared at the bundle as if it were a venomous snake. He took an involuntary step back. “Me? Take that? Are you mad? If they find this on me… Luc, I am an Englishman. A foreigner. They would not be gentle.”

“It is just paper, monsieur. To you, it is a book. To them, it is a treasonous document. But if it is just cargo, just goods in a satchel with a promissory note, who would look twice?” Luc’s logic was that of a terrified child, but it held a sliver of terrifying sense. “My master, he has no one else. His family is gone. I am just an apprentice. They will hang me if I try to leave the city. But you… you have a pass. You have a reason to be here. You are leaving.”

Arthur’s mind raced. He pictured the damp, dark cells of the Conciergerie. He pictured a crowd, a pike, his own portly body hoisted aloft. He had never been brave. Bravery was for heroes in books. He was a man who worried about the price of vellum and the damp in his cellar.

“I cannot,” he said, the words tasting like ash in his mouth. “It is too great a risk. I am sorry.”

He turned to leave, his heart a frantic drum against his ribs. He would find an inn, hide until the roads were clear, and go home. He would write off the debt. It was only money.

“Please, monsieur,” Luc’s voice followed him, thin and broken. “For the love of God.”

Arthur kept walking. He had gone perhaps ten paces when the boy spoke again, his voice suddenly clear and steady, cutting through the noise of the street.

“He said you were a man who loved books more than money.”

Arthur stopped. He stood frozen in the middle of the alley, the roar of Paris fading to a dull hum in his ears. *A man who loved books more than money.* It was the highest compliment Dubois had ever paid him, during a long night of shared brandy and talk of Caxton and Gutenberg. It was a simple statement of fact. His shop was his life. The feel of a well-bound spine, the smell of old paper and leather, the quiet reverence of a true reader—these were the things that gave his life meaning. This parcel, this bundle of ink and paper, was not a bomb. It was a book. A book that would be silenced, destroyed. The thought was a physical pain, a violation of some sacred, unspoken law he had lived by his entire life.

Slowly, deliberately, Arthur Penhaligon turned around. He walked back to the boy, his steps heavy with a resolve he did not know he possessed. He did not say a word. He simply opened his satchel, took out his own clean shirts and a copy of *The Spectator* he had brought to read on the journey, and placed them on a nearby crate. He took the heavy parcel from Luc’s trembling hands and slid it into the satchel. It fit perfectly.

Luc stared at him, tears streaming freely down his face. He opened his mouth to speak, but Arthur held up a hand. “Go,” he said, his voice quiet but firm. “Disappear. Do not come back here.”

He slung the satchel over his shoulder. The weight of it was immense, heavier than a hundred and twenty livres, heavier than his own life. He did not feel brave. He felt terrified, a fool, a man walking knowingly into the mouth of a lion. But as he walked away, leaving the boy and the ruined printing house behind, he felt something else too. For the first time in his life, Arthur Penhaligon felt the solid, terrifying, and profound weight of a man of his word. He clutched the satchel to his side and walked on, a small, portly Englishman carrying a revolution through the streets of Paris.
