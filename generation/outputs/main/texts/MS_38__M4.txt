The silence that had fallen upon Ashcombe was not the silence of peace, but the silence of a tomb sealed and forgotten. When the fever had passed through our valley like a scythe through ripe corn, it left behind it a stillness so profound that I could hear the blood beating in my own temples, and the sound was a reproach. I had watched my mother and my sisters taken by the sickness, one after another, their faces growing pale as wax beneath the flickering lamplight, and I had been powerless to stay the hand of death. When at last I stood alone in the empty cottage, surrounded by the relics of a life that was no more, I felt as one who has been buried alive, and who hears above him the footsteps of the living world, but cannot make himself heard.

It was upon the third day of my solitude that I encountered her. I had gone to the well at the crossroads, for the water in the house had grown foul, and there I found a figure kneeling upon the stones, her hands clasped before her, her lips moving in what I took at first to be prayer. She started at my approach, and rose to her feet with a wild look in her eyes, as though she feared I might be some spectre conjured by her grief. Her name, she told me, was Elara, and she was the last of the household at the mill; her father, the miller, had been the first to sicken, and she had nursed him and her brothers until the fever had claimed them all, and she had been left alone with the dust and the silence.

We stood for a long while regarding one another, each of us, I think, uncertain whether the other were real, or merely a vision born of our mutual desolation. The sun was sinking behind the hills, and the shadows lengthened across the empty street, and in that fading light we spoke together of the dead, and of the living, and of what remained to us who had been spared. It was Elara who first gave voice to the thought that had been growing in my own heart like a seed in darkness. "We cannot remain here," she said, and her voice, though low, was firm. "This place is a grave, and we are the ghosts that haunt it. There must be other villages, other towns, where the plague has not reached. We must go and find them."

I confess that the proposal filled me at once with hope and with terror. To leave the valley where I had been born, where every stone and tree was dear to me, and to set forth into a world I did not know, seemed a kind of death in itself. Yet the alternative was to remain in a house of shadows, to tend the graves of my family until my own strength failed, and to die at last alone and unremembered. I looked at Elara, at her face pale with grief but resolute, and I felt that I could not let her go alone into the wilderness. "I will go with you," I said, and she nodded, and we clasped hands in the failing light, two strangers bound together by a common sorrow.

We set out at dawn the following day, carrying what provisions we could gather, and a small bundle of clothes, and the few books that had belonged to my father. As we passed the churchyard, Elara paused and knelt by the graves of her family, and I stood beside her, and we were silent for a long while, for there was nothing to be said. The dead had no need of our words, and the living had no words that could suffice. Then we rose and walked on, and the village of Ashcombe fell behind us, and we did not look back.

The road wound through the hills, and the autumn had already begun to touch the leaves with gold, and the air was sharp and clean after the foulness of the sick-rooms. For a long while we walked in silence, each of us occupied with our own thoughts, and I found myself studying my companion with a curiosity that I could not suppress. She was tall, and her hair was dark, and there was in her bearing a quiet dignity that spoke of a spirit unbroken by suffering. She had nursed her father and brothers with her own hands, she told me, and had watched them die, and yet she had not lost her reason, nor her will to live. I marvelled at her fortitude, and I wondered whether I should have been capable of the same.

We spoke little at first, for the habit of solitude had made us both reticent, and there was between us the awkwardness of strangers who have been thrown together by circumstance. But as the days passed, and we shared the bread and the water and the shelter of barns and hedgerows, the silence between us began to soften, and we found ourselves talking of our lives before the plague, of the small joys and sorrows that had filled them, and of the dreams we had cherished for the future. She told me of the mill, and of the sound of the wheel turning in the stream, and of the smell of fresh flour on the morning air. I told her of my father's library, and of the evenings we had spent reading by the fire, and of the arguments we had had about philosophy and poetry, and she listened with a patience that I found both humbling and consoling.

One evening, as we sat by a fire we had kindled in the shelter of a ruined wall, she asked me whether I believed that the dead could see us, and whether they would approve of what we were doing. I was silent for a long while, for I had asked myself the same question a hundred times. "I do not know," I said at last. "But I think that if they can see us, they would not wish us to die. They would wish us to live, and to carry their memory with us, and to find others who have survived, and to build something new from the ruins of what they left behind." She looked at me then with an expression that I could not read, and she said, "Then we owe them more than tears. We owe them our lives, and our hope, and our labour." And I knew that she was right, and I felt the weight of that obligation settle upon me, not as a burden, but as a purpose.

The weeks that followed were hard, and there were days when the rain fell without ceasing, and the road turned to mud, and we were cold and hungry and weary beyond words. There were nights when I woke from dreams of my mother's face, and found myself weeping in the darkness, and Elara would reach out and take my hand, and we would lie there in silence until the dawn came. And there were days when she was silent and withdrawn, and I knew that she was thinking of her brothers, and I did not press her to speak, but walked beside her and waited. We learned to read one another's moods, and to give each other space when space was needed, and to offer comfort when comfort could be borne. It was not love, not yet, but it was something akin to it—a trust that had been forged in the fire of our common suffering, and that grew stronger with every mile we travelled.

At last, after many weeks, we came to a town where there were signs of life. Smoke rose from chimneys, and we heard the sound of voices, and saw figures moving in the streets. We stopped at the edge of the town and looked at one another, and I saw in Elara's eyes the same mixture of hope and fear that I felt in my own heart. "We have come this far," she said. "We cannot turn back now." And so we walked on, together, into the unknown, carrying with us the memory of our dead, and the promise of a new beginning.
