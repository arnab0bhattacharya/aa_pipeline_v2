The letter arrived at the Baker Street rooms on a Tuesday morning in late October, when the sky above London hung low and grey, and the air tasted of coal smoke and impending rain. It was a modest envelope, unmarked save for the return address: *The Old Mill House, Dunstable Hollow, Hants*. The paper was of a thick, cream hue, and the seal, though broken, bore the distinct impression of a thistle.

Sherlock Holmes took it from my hand without looking up from the violin he was tuning. He held it to his nose, sniffed it with the precision of a chemist, and then passed it through his lens.

"Wet," he said, his voice holding that peculiar, dry inflection. "But not rain. The dampness is consistent with steam. Has it been opened?"

"I should say so," I replied. "The wax is disturbed, and the edges are slightly softened. It looks as though someone has steamed the seal to unseal it, read the contents, and then resealed it with a similar blob of wax."

Holmes’s eyes, usually so languid when he was not engaged in a problem, snapped open. He placed the envelope on the table with a sharp snap. "A letter addressed to the Squire of Dunstable Hollow, intercepted by his own postmistress. Interesting. Read it, Watson."

I unfolded the paper. The handwriting was small, cramped, and trembling, the ink occasionally bleeding into the fibers of the paper, suggesting a hand that was either nervous or wet.

> *To the Celebrated Detective of Baker Street,*
>
> *I am Mrs. Elspeth Thorne, Postmistress of Dunstable Hollow. I write this with great fear, for I am a woman of no standing, and the Squire’s influence is absolute in this parish. I have served this village for twelve years, and I have always believed that silence is the best policy for a woman of my position. But lately, the silence has become a scream.*
>
> *For the past three months, every letter addressed to Squire Josiah Blackwood has arrived at the village office slightly damp. They are warm to the touch, as if they have just come from a kettle. I have seen the wax seals, Mr. Holmes. They are not original. They have been melted and reformed. I am a careful woman; I notice the weight of the wax, the shade of the color. The Squire’s letters come from London, from his solicitor, and from his brother in the Army. They are all treated the same. Steamed, opened, read, and closed again. The paper is warped. The ink is smudged. I have not spoken of it, for who would believe a postmistress against a Squire? And besides, the Squire pays my wages, and my sister lives in his employ.*
>
> *But now, something worse has happened. Mr. Arthur Penhaligon, the Squire’s gamekeeper, has vanished. He was last seen on the moor two days ago, walking with the Squire to check the nets. He did not return. The Squire tells the constable that Penhaligon had a quarrel and likely fled to the coast. But I know better. I saw Penhaligon at the post office an hour before he left for the moor. He was pale, Mr. Holmes, paler than death. He pressed a letter into my hand, sealed with red wax, and whispered, “If I do not come back, give this to the law. Do not let them read it. Do not let them steam it.”*
>
> *I have kept the letter. It is in my pocket as I write this. I am sending you a copy, though I fear it is too late. The Squire’s men are watching the office. I must go now. If you do not come, I shall be compelled to do nothing, for my fear is too great. But I beg of you, look into the damp letters. There is something in them that has driven a man to disappear.*
>
> *Yours in desperate hope,*
> *E. Thorne*

I finished reading. Holmes was already standing, his eyes fixed on the fireplace, his hands clasped behind his back.

"The gamekeeper," Holmes murmured. "A man who carries a sealed letter to a postmistress, knowing that his employer intercepts mail. That is a man who knows he is being watched. And he knows that the Squire reads his correspondence. If Penhaligon was communicating with someone in London, or if he was sending evidence, the Squire would know. And if the Squire killed him to prevent the evidence from reaching its destination, then the postmistress is in extreme danger."

"Why does he trust you?" I asked.

"Because he knows that you are the only one in London who cannot be bought, and who is too curious to let a mystery slide," Holmes replied. He turned to me, his face grave. "We must go, Watson. Immediately. The Squire of Dunstable Hollow is not a man to be trifled with. If he has been intercepting mail for three months, he is likely involved in something that requires secrecy. Fraud, perhaps, or worse. And if he has killed a man to keep the secret, he will kill again to cover the first crime."

We were at the station by noon. The train to Basingstoke was delayed by a signal failure, a delay that Holmes regarded with the patience of a man who knows that nature often conspires with the investigator. We traveled in a second-class carriage, the only two occupants, while the rain lashed against the windows, blurring the passing fields into a grey smear.

"Dunstable Hollow," Holmes said, consulting a map. "A village of some four hundred souls, nestled in a valley. The Squire owns the majority of the land. The post office is in the main street. Mrs. Thorne is likely a woman of limited means, but of sharp intellect. She has observed details that others would have missed. The dampness of the letters, the weight of the wax. It is a subtle observation, but it is the key to the whole affair."

"And the letter Penhaligon gave her?" I asked.

"Where is it?" Holmes asked. "She said she was sending a copy, but I see no attachment in the envelope. She likely kept the original. If she has given it to the constable, it is gone. If she has kept it, she is in danger. The Squire’s men are watching the office. They will watch her home, too. We must find her before the Squire does."

We arrived in Hampshire as the sun was setting, casting long, purple shadows across the fields. The village of Dunstable Hollow was just as Holmes had described it: a cluster of stone cottages and a large manor house perched on a rise, overlooking the valley. The air was cold and sharp, carrying the scent of damp earth and woodsmoke.

We walked to the main street, where the post office stood. It was a small, whitewashed building, with a green door and a sign that read *Post Office & General Store*. The windows were dark, but a faint light glowed from the back of the building.

Holmes stopped at the corner, his eyes scanning the street. "There are two men," he whispered. "They are standing by the churchyard wall. They are not postmen. They are wearing civilian clothes, but their posture is that of guards. They are watching the post office."

I looked. Sure enough, two men stood by the wall, their hands in their pockets, their eyes fixed on the post office door. They were large men, broad-shouldered and silent.

"We cannot go in," Holmes said. "Not yet. We must wait for the postmistress to leave. She will likely go home. We can follow her."

We waited. The rain began to fall again, a fine, misty drizzle that soaked our clothes. The men by the wall did not move. They were patient, like cats waiting for a mouse.

An hour passed. The light in the back of the post office flickered, and then went out. A moment later, the back door opened, and a small, hunched figure emerged. It was Mrs. Thorne. She was wearing a dark cloak, and her head was bowed against the rain. She looked frail, but her steps were quick and determined.

"Follow," Holmes said. "Keep your distance. Do not let them see us."

We followed Mrs. Thorne down the street, past the church, and into a narrow lane that led to a row of small cottages. She stopped in front of the first cottage, a small stone building with a thatched roof. She entered, and the door closed behind her.

Holmes waited for a full minute before he moved. "She is in," he said. "The men are still at the churchyard. They are not watching her home. They are watching the post office. This is a mistake on their part. Or perhaps they believe she is safe at home."

We stood in the rain, watching the cottage. The light in the window was off. It was dark inside.

"Go in," Holmes said. "I will create a distraction. If I make a noise, or if I shout, the men will come. You will have a moment to enter the house."

I hesitated. "And you?"

"I will handle the men," Holmes said, with a smile that was more grim than cheerful. "Go. Now."

I slipped around the corner and approached the cottage. The door was locked, but the window was slightly ajar. I climbed up, pushed the window open, and slipped inside.

The room was small and cold. It smelled of damp wool and candle wax. I could see Mrs. Thorne sitting at a table, her hands trembling. She was holding a letter.

"Mrs. Thorne?" I whispered.

She jumped, her eyes wide with fear. "Who are you?"

"I am a friend," I said. "Mr. Holmes is outside. We have come to help you."

She looked at me, her face pale. "Help? What good can help do? He is out there. He knows I have the letter. He will come for it."

"He does not know you have it," I said. "That is why we are here. You must give it to us. We will take it to London. The Squire cannot touch it there."

She looked at the letter in her hand. "It is not just a letter," she said. "It is a confession. Penhaligon saw something. He saw the Squire with a dead man. A man from the coast. A man the Squire said had fled."

"A murder?" I asked.

"Yes. And the Squire has been intercepting letters to find out who knew. He has been killing anyone who might have seen. Penhaligon was the last. But I have the proof."

She held out the letter. "Take it. Go. Before he comes."

I took the letter. It was heavy, the paper thick. I slipped it into my pocket.

"Thank you," I said. "Mr. Holmes will take care of the rest."

I left through the window and ran back to the street. Holmes was standing in the rain, his face calm. The two men were still at the churchyard wall, but they were looking toward the lane, their eyes alert.

"Did you get it?" Holmes asked.

"Yes," I said. "But we must leave now. They are suspicious."
