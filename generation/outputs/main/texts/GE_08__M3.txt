Arthur Pendrath had reached the age of nine-and-twenty without having ever felt the pressure of a necessity, and it may be supposed that such an immunity from the common yoke of labour is not the most favourable preparation for a just estimate of human difficulties. He had chambers in Fig Tree Court, a respectable volume of reported cases given over more to the binding than the reading, and an income that rendered the bar a polite diversion rather than a livelihood. He had grown up in the ancient county town of Loamford, the only son of a deceased physician whose investments had prospered in the quiet, compounding way that money does when its owner is dead and cannot interfere with it. To Arthur, Loamford was a panorama of pleasant red brick, of gabled market-places, and kindly old women who gave him sweetmeats; he had no notion of the alley-ways where the smoke-grime was thick, or the low-roofed workrooms from which the town’s modest industry drew its breath.

It was in the autumn of 1862 that his friend, a charitable curate with a dangerous facility for making other people’s affairs his own, induced him to take up the defence of Joel Brent, a glove-maker accused of stealing a quantity of dressed kid-skins from the manufactory of Mr. Phipps. Joel was in prison, his wife was in despair, and the evidence hinged on a question of identity and opportunity which required a subtler analysis than the magistrates were disposed to give it. Arthur, finding a sudden appetite for the dramatic aspect of misfortune, consented to go down to Loamford and investigate.

He arrived on a Tuesday evening, the air heavy with the damp of late October, and took a room at the principal inn. The next morning he walked up Brick Lane to the premises of Mr. Phipps. The town looked much as it had always looked in his childhood, but with a difference that now struck him with a faint, disagreeable surprise: the houses seemed smaller, the footways narrower, and the faces of the people he passed wore an expression of sharpened attention, as if they were calculating the cost of the next meal against the day’s wages. He had never before observed this look, having been accustomed to see the inhabitants of Loamford only on Sundays, when the pressure of the week was temporarily lifted and folks wore their best clothes as a kind of protective armour against the world’s contempt.

He found Joel’s wife, Margaret, in a low room up a dark staircase, with the smell of stale leather and damp plaster competing for dominance. She was a small woman, with sharp features and a persistent twitch in her right eyelid.

“Ye’ll be th’ gentleman come to save my Joel,” she said, not rising from her chair, where she sat darning a stocking with a haste that seemed to belong to some other activity. “I mun tell ye straight, sir, he’s nowt but a fool, but he’s no thief. He’d give the skin off his back to ony body as axed him, but he’d not take a skin as didn’t belong to him.”

“He gave the skin off his back, did he?” said Arthur, seating himself on the only available stool, and feeling that the phrase was a poor one for a barrister to repeat. “I mean to say, was he in the habit of lending?”

“Aye, he lent, and he forgot, and he let the boys impose on him,” said Margaret, her needle moving faster. “Mr. Phipps’s foreman, he’s a hard man, sir. He’d accuse the saints o’ the Almighty if they walked off with a scrap o’ waste. Joel, he went to fetch a bucket o’ water for old Mrs. Grimes as lives by the yard-gate, and while he was gone, the skins was gone, and they say as Joel took ’em.”

Arthur learned that the evidence against Joel was the testimony of Mr. Phipps’s foreman, a man named Jowett, who declared he had seen Joel loitering near the drying-racks, and the fact that a bundle of skins had been found concealed in Joel’s own workshop. Joel’s defence—that he had found the bundle on his doorstep and, in his simplicity, had put it aside intending to return it to Mr. Phipps the next morning—was the kind of defence that is easily laughed at by men who have never been poor enough to be afraid of being accused of anything.

Arthur took down the details with a care that surprised himself. He walked back to his inn through the market-square, and the sense of his own ignorance pressed upon him with a weight that was not wholly disagreeable, for it carried the flavour of a new discovery. He had lived twenty years within a mile of Brick Lane and had never known that the glove-makers’ wages depended on the price of kid-skins in the London market, or that a bad season meant that the cutters were paid by the piece and the finishers by the dozen, and that both were liable to be dismissed without notice if the employer’s profits fell below a certain point. He had thought of Loamford as a place where people lived because they had always lived there, not as a place where they struggled to live because there was nowhere else to go.

He went to the county gaol and saw Joel, a heavy-faced man of forty, with hands that were marked by the awl and the needle, and a confused, anxious look in his eyes.

“I dunno as I can tell ye much more, sir,” Joel said, turning his cap round and round in his hands. “I were i’ the workshop till past seven, and then I went to fetch the water. I didn’t see nobody, but I heard a cart go by. I found the bundle on my step when I come back, and I thought it were waste that Mr. Phipps had sent me to sort, as he sometimes does. I’m a poor hand at remembering things, but I’m no thief, sir, and that’s the truth, and I mun hope as the justice’ll believe me.”

Arthur looked at the man’s heavy face, his clumsy, work-marked hands, and felt a stirring of that pity which is not mere sentiment but a recognition of the fundamental kinship of human weakness. He thought of his own life, how he had never been tempted to steal because he had never wanted anything he could not buy, and how easy it was for a man with a full purse to be honest. He reflected that the law, which he had been accustomed to regard as a majestic system of principles, was in its practical operation a thing of details and accidents, and that the difference between a respectable man and a convicted felon might be no more than the difference between a door that locked and a door that did not.

The trial came on at the Loamford Assizes, and Arthur, in his wig and gown, made a speech that was felt by the reporters to be “able and eloquent.” He showed that Jowett’s evidence was inconsistent, that no one had seen Joel take the skins, and that the finding of the bundle on his doorstep was susceptible of a perfectly innocent explanation. The jury, after a retirement of twenty minutes, brought in a verdict of Not Guilty, and Joel, blinking in the light of the court, was taken back to his wife by a side-door.

Arthur returned to London with a sense of having done a good deed, but also with a sense of having seen something that he could not forget. He had looked into a world which had been close to him all his life and which he had never seen, and he knew now that the comfort of his ignorance had been bought at a price, though the price had been paid by others. He sat in his chambers in Fig Tree Court, with the fire burning brightly and the gas lit against the November fog, and thought of Margaret Brent’s needle moving fast in the dark room, and of Joel’s heavy face and turning cap, and of all the men and women in Loamford and a thousand other towns who were living in the shadow of a necessity that he had never known. And he was aware, with a clarity that was almost painful, that this knowledge, once acquired, could not be laid aside; that it would sit in his mind like a new sense, making him aware of dimensions of experience that had previously been invisible, and that his old comfort was gone forever, as a man’s childhood is gone when he has once understood that the world is not arranged for his convenience.
