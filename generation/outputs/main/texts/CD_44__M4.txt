The accident, as it was called at the coroner’s inquest, had occurred some twelve years prior, in the winter of the year ’93, upon a stretch of the Paris road near the village of Saint-Cloud; and the man who was now called upon to give his evidence concerning it was one Mr. Abel Pinnock, a coachman of considerable bulk and greater wind, who had driven in his time for a French nobleman of the old régime, and who now, in the year 1805, sat in a public-house in the Borough, with a pot of porter at his elbow and a cloud of perplexity upon his brow.

“You must understand, sir,” said Mr. Pinnock, addressing the attorney’s clerk who had come to take his deposition, “that I was not always the man you see before you. There was a time when I sat upon a box so high above the common run of mortals that I looked down upon the world as a man might look down from the gallery of a theatre upon the pit below; and the horses I held were not the poor shambling hacks of a London cab-stand, but a pair of greys—ah! such greys as would have made a bishop forget his sermon—with shoulders like a guardsman’s and tails like a lady’s fan, and the leather of my harness was so polished that it winked at you in the sun like a saucy eye.”

He took a long draught of his porter, and wiped his mouth upon the back of his hand, and looked at the clerk with an expression in which the pride of the past contended strangely with the humility of the present.

“The Marquis de Vauvray—for that was the name of my master, and a finer gentleman never wore a powdered wig—the Marquis de Vauvray had a daughter, Mademoiselle Céleste, who was the very apple of his eye, and the light of his household; and it was my honour to drive her, upon the Sunday, to the church at Saint-Cloud, and upon the Wednesday to the house of her aunt at Versailles, and upon any day that the fancy took her, to the shops in the Rue Saint-Honoré; and I would have driven her, sir, into the very jaws of the Revolution itself, had she but said the word, and thought it an honour to be eaten for her sake.”

“The accident, Mr. Pinnock,” said the clerk, who was a young man of a dry and methodical habit, and who had a great many more depositions to take before the evening.

“I am coming to it, sir,” returned the coachman, with some dignity, “if you will but have the patience to let me come to it in my own way. For the truth is, that when a man has driven a great man’s carriage, and has had the handling of a great man’s horses, and has been, as you might say, a part of the establishment, he cannot separate the accident from the establishment, nor the establishment from the family, nor the family from the whole course of his former life; and if you ask him to give you the bare facts, he will give you the bare facts, and no doubt you will be satisfied; but he will give you a great deal more than the bare facts, because the bare facts are not the whole of the matter, and never were, and never will be.”

The clerk sighed, and dipped his pen, and Mr. Pinnock, taking this as a permission to proceed, proceeded.

“It was upon the twenty-first of December,” said he, “and the snow lay upon the road so thick that the wheels went through it with a soft, muffled sound, like the rolling of a drum that is draped in black; and the air was so still that you might have heard a sparrow sneeze a mile away; and the greys—for I had the greys that day, sir, and I would have you to know it—the greys were in such fettle that they champed upon the bit and tossed their heads, and the steam went up from their nostrils like the smoke from a pair of chimney-pots, and I said to myself, ‘Abel Pinnock, there is mischief in the air this morning, for the horses are too lively for the weather, and the weather is too dead for the horses.’ And just as I said it, out of the white mist that hung upon the road, there came a child—a little girl, sir, with a hood upon her head and a basket upon her arm—and she ran across the road, and she slipped upon the ice, and she fell; and I pulled at the reins with all the strength that God had given me, and I cried out, ‘Hold! hold!’ but the greys were too fresh, and the road was too slippery, and the wheel went over her.”

He stopped, and passed his hand across his eyes, and the clerk, who was not without feeling, though he was methodical, waited in silence.

“And the Marquis,” said Mr. Pinnock, after a pause, “the Marquis was in the carriage, and Mademoiselle Céleste was in the carriage, and they both cried out together, and I got down from the box, and I took the child in my arms, and she was dead, sir, dead before I had lifted her from the ground; and I stood there in the snow with the dead child in my arms, and the greys plunging and rearing, and the footman as white as the snow itself, and I said to myself, ‘Abel Pinnock, this is the end of your fine driving, and you will never sit upon a box again.’”

“And what happened then?” asked the clerk.

“Why, sir,” returned Mr. Pinnock, “the Marquis got out of the carriage, and he looked at the child, and he looked at me, and he said—and I shall never forget it to my dying day—he said, ‘Pinnock, this is a misfortune, and not a fault; and if there is any man in France who says otherwise, I will answer him with my sword.’ And he took off his own cloak and he wrapped the child in it, and he went himself to the cottage where the mother lived, and he told her the truth with his own lips, and he gave her a purse of gold, and he said, ‘Madame, I am the cause of your sorrow, and I will be your friend so long as I live.’ And the mother—a poor widow, sir, with a face like a walnut and a heart like a church—the mother fell upon her knees and blessed him, and said that the child was in heaven, and that the Marquis was an angel upon earth; and I stood by and I wept, sir, like a great baby, and the footman wept, and the greys stood still and hung their heads as if they were ashamed of themselves.”

“And the child’s name?” asked the clerk.

“I never knew it, sir,” said Mr. Pinnock, “and I have often thought it strange that I should have driven over a child and never known her name. But the Marquis knew it, and the mother knew it, and God knew it, and that was enough for the time. But now, sir, you come to me after twelve years, and you ask me to give evidence about it, and you tell me that there is a man in London who says that the child was not killed by the wheel at all, but by the hand of the footman, and that the Marquis bribed the mother to hold her tongue, and that I, Abel Pinnock, was a witness to the bribery, and have been silent all these years for the sake of the Marquis’s money.”

“Those are the allegations, Mr. Pinnock,” said the clerk, “and it is for you to say whether they are true or false.”

“And I say they are false,” returned the coachman, “as false as a Frenchman’s compliment and as black as the inside of a hat. But I will tell you what troubles me, sir, and I will tell you plainly, for I am not a man to hide my thoughts. The truth is, that I have thought about that morning so often, and I have told the story so often, and I have heard other people tell it, and I have dreamed about it, that I am not always sure whether I remember the thing itself or only my memory of it. There is a spot in my mind, sir, where the child falls, and there is another spot where the wheel goes over her, and there is another spot where the Marquis wraps her in his cloak; and sometimes the spots run together, and sometimes they change places, and sometimes I see the footman’s face as plain as I see yours, and sometimes I cannot see it at all. And I have heard men say that the mind is like a slate, and that memory is what is written upon it; but I say that the mind is like a coachman’s box, sir, and that memory is like the road behind you, which you see not as it is, but as the horses have made it, and the mist has hidden it, and the years have worn it away.”

He drank off the rest of his porter, and set down the pot with a bang, and looked at the clerk with a kind of angry honesty.

“But this I will swear to,” said he, “and I will swear it before any judge in England, and I will swear it with my hand upon the Book, and I will swear it if I am to be hanged for it the next minute: the child was killed by the wheel of the carriage, and by nothing else; and the Marquis de Vauvray was as innocent of her blood as the child herself; and if there is any man in London who says otherwise, he is a liar, sir, and I will tell him so to his face, though I am an old man and he is a young one, and though I have not sat upon a box these ten years, and am only a poor coachman in the Borough, with a pot of porter and a memory that plays me tricks.”

The clerk wrote it all down, and Mr. Pinnock, having signed his name with a great many flourishes, sat for a long time in silence, looking into the fire, and seeing, perhaps, the greys and the snow and the white face of Mademoiselle Céleste, and hearing, perhaps, the voice of the Marquis saying, “This is a misfortune, and not a fault.”
