The grey light of dawn lingered over the Gulf of Siam, suspended in sluggish layers of grey luminosity. To the east, a faint, sickly gleam lingered near the horizon, as if exhausted by the effort of piercing the thick vapors. Upon the bridge of the coasting steamer *Mahiddhara*, Captain Elias Whaites stood motionless, his hands resting on the brass rails. The dampness of the morning air seemed to condense upon his iron-grey beard. He presented an imposing immobility, leaning forward as one striving to decipher a cryptic message written in the shifting water. He had stood thus for an hour, his sightless eyes, obscured by the darkness of his irises, fixed upon a point in the mist where the mangrove coast ought to have been, though to him it was nothing. He possessed a profound silence, an imposing physical stillness that imposed itself upon the vessel, as if his own blindness had cast a pall over the ship’s compass.

Below him, the deck was yet empty but for the Malay seamen moving spectrally in the semi-darkness, and Serang Dol, who paused near the skylight, casting a glance of furtive inquiry upward. Dol’s face was a dark mask of impenetrable reserve, his eyes shining with a peculiar vigilance. The Serang knew. He had watched the Captain’s hesitant step on the gangway, the fumbling for the rail, the slow, cautious turning of the head which listens rather than sees. But Dol’s loyalty was not to the ship, nor to the white men who owned her; his loyalty was to the equilibrium of his own existence, a silent compact with the sea, and he waited, motionless and watchful, to see what the white man’s folly would yield.

From the shadows of the chart-room door, Mr. Kearns, the mate, emerged. He was a man of slender build, with a narrow, sallow face and eyes that seemed to be perpetually calculating some distant, unattainable sum. His step was measured, deliberate, and he carried the ship’s log under his arm with the air of a man bearing a sacred and terrible document. He mounted the ladder with a tread that was almost soundless, and stopping near the Captain, said in a low, even voice:

"North by east, sir. Depth twenty fathoms. We are clear of the Mearon shoals."

Captain Whaites turned his head slowly. "The glass is falling," he said, his voice deep and hollow, as if echoing from a cavern. "I feel it in the air. You are sure of the position, Mr. Kearns?"

"Quite sure, sir." Kearns’s eyes gleamed with a sudden, cold light, a flash of something akin to triumph, which was instantly suppressed. "I took the bearings myself not ten minutes since. The mist is lifting a little to the southward."

"Very good." The Captain’s hand moved involuntarily, as if to touch his own eyes, but he checked the gesture, his fingers curling into a fist. "Keep her as she goes. I will rely upon your sight, Mr. Kearns."

Kearns inclined his head, a subtle, almost mocking obeisance. "You may, sir," he murmured. He turned away, and descending to the main deck, he encountered the Serang, who still lingered near the hatch. The two men exchanged a glance that was charged with a mute, intense understanding. It was the look of men who share a dangerous secret, not in brotherhood but in mutual distrust. Kearns spoke a few words in Malay, sharp, peremptory, and the Serang nodded slowly, his face remaining devoid of expression. Kearns continued on his way, his step lighter, his head held higher, as if the very act of guiding a blind man through a treacherous channel had imbued him with a sudden, intoxicating sense of power.

In his cabin below, Whaites sat at his table, his hands spread out upon the chart. He could feel the smooth paper, the raised lines of the coast, the faint indentations of previous courses. But the chart was a darkness to him now, a darkness as profound as the darkness that had settled over his world. He remembered the first time he had noticed the dimness, a slow fading of the horizon, a blurring of the stars. He had told no one. To tell the owners—the firm of Van der Put and Helder, in Singapore—would be to surrender his life, the only life he knew. They were men without imagination, who saw a ship only as a balance of freight and insurance, and a captain as a unit of production, to be discarded the moment his efficiency faltered. He had seen them do it to old Macallister, casting him aside like a broken spar. No, he would not be discarded. He would hold on, relying on his memory of the coast, on his knowledge of the sea, and on the eyes of his mate.

But Kearns was ambitious. Whaites knew this. He had known it from the first moment he had seen the man, standing at the rail, staring at the *Mahiddhara* as if she were a prize to be won. Kearns was of that class of men who believe that the world’s goods are owed to them, and who chafe under the authority of those they consider their inferiors. Whaites had felt the mate’s growing ascendancy, the subtle shifting of power, the way the crew looked to Kearns now, rather than to him. It was a strange, creeping thing, this transfer of allegiance, invisible to the casual eye but as real as the tide. And Whaites, in the darkness of his cabin, felt the weight of it pressing upon him, a moral darkness more profound than the physical blindness that had befallen him.

The day wore on, the sun climbing through the haze, casting a coppery glare over the water. The *Mahiddhara* plowed through the sluggish swell, her engines throbbing with a monotonous, tireless beat. Whaites remained on the bridge, a silent, brooding figure, his face turned perpetually toward the unseen horizon. He listened to the sounds of the ship, the creak of the derricks, the wash of the sea against the hull, the distant voices of the crew. He heard Kearns come up the ladder again, and stop beside him.

"The mail boat from Singapore is in sight, sir," Kearns said, his voice betraying a faint tremor of anticipation. "She is making for the anchorage at Port Durland. Mr. Van der Put is likely aboard."

Whaites felt a cold hand close around his heart. Van der Put, the senior partner, the man whose suspicion was as keen as a blade. He had been expecting this, dreading it. A letter had gone from the office, he knew, a letter that perhaps contained a question, a doubt, a whisper. Perhaps the insurance agents in Penang had talked. Perhaps the last cargo had been delayed too long. He did not know. He only knew that the shadow of Van der Put’s suspicion was falling upon him, as the shadow of the *Mahiddhara* fell upon the water.

"You are certain it is Van der Put?" he asked, his voice betraying nothing.

"I heard it in the office before we sailed, sir," Kearns replied. "He was to make a tour of inspection. I—"

"You did not think to inform me of this, Mr. Kearns?" Whaites interrupted, a faint edge of iron in his tone.

Kearns paused, and in that pause, a whole world of ambition and calculation seemed to hold its breath. "I did not wish to trouble you with office gossip, sir," he said smoothly. "But now that the boat is in sight—"

"Keep her on her course," Whaites said. "I will go below and—"

He stopped. He had been about to say "look at the chart," but the words died on his lips. He felt Kearns’s gaze upon him, a gaze that was almost a touch, a physical pressure upon his blindness. He knew that Kearns was waiting, waiting for him to stumble, to reveal the truth, to surrender the command that was his life. And in that moment, a profound, brooding bitterness rose up within him, a bitterness against the sea, against the owners, against the very men who walked his deck. He turned slowly, his hands grasping the rail, and moved toward the ladder. His foot found the first step, and he descended with a careful, deliberate tread, feeling the weight of the ship, the motion of the sea, and the silent, watching eyes of the crew.

In his cabin, he sat again at the table, his hands upon the chart. He could hear the mail boat’s whistle, a distant, mournful sound, and the answering blast of the *Mahiddhara*. He knew that in a few hours, Van der Put would be aboard, asking questions, demanding to see the logs, the charts, the position. And he, Captain Whaites, would have to face him, blind and helpless, relying on the words of a mate who desired his place. The moral darkness of his situation closed in around him, a suffocating embrace. He thought of the sea, of the vast, indifferent expanse of water that had been his home for forty years, and he felt a strange, bitter kinship with it. The sea did not judge. It did not suspect. It simply was. And he, too, would simply be, for as long as he could.

On deck, Kearns stood by the rail, watching the mail boat draw nearer. The Serang came up to him, and the two men stood in silence. Then Kearns spoke, his voice low and meditative.

"The old man is finished," he said. "He knows it, and I know it. When Van der Put steps aboard, he will see. The Captain is—"

He paused, as if searching for the right word. The Serang’s face remained impassive, his eyes fixed on the approaching vessel.

"Blind," Kearns said, almost whispering the word. "He is blind."

The Serang nodded slowly. "Yes, Tuan," he said. "But the sea does not care."

Kearns looked at him, a flicker of annoyance crossing his features. Then he turned back to the rail, his face set in a hard, determined expression. The mail boat was close now, her hull gleaming in the coppery light, and Kearns could see the figures on her bridge, the glint of brass, the flutter of flags. He straightened his collar, smoothed his hair, and waited. The game was about to begin.
