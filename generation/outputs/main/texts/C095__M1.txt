The afternoon sun, thick and golden as honey, slanted through the tall windows of the drawing room at Number 7, Lansdowne Terrace. It caught the dust motes dancing in the air and illuminated the precise, silent world of Mrs. Eleanor Vance. Her hands, resting in her lap, were still. The only movement in the room was the slow, rhythmic tick of the longcase clock in the hall, a sound as steady and reassuring as the beat of her own heart. It was the sound of order, of predictability, of a life painstakingly built from the wreckage of another.

Mrs. Vance was the proprietor of this respectable lodging house, a fact she wore like a suit of armour. The silver on the sideboard was polished to a mirror shine, the damask curtains were without a single speck of dust, and the quiet murmur of her lodgers—a retired colonel, a pair of spinster sisters, a young clerk from the bank—was the only music she required. Her own past was a locked room in the attic of her memory, the key long since thrown away.

The sharp, metallic jangle of the front doorbell cut through the quiet. It was an unfamiliar sound; her visitors were always expected. A moment later, Mrs. Gable, her housekeeper, appeared in the doorway, her face a mask of slight confusion.

“A gentleman to see you, ma’am. He has no appointment.”

Before Eleanor could formulate a reply, a man stepped around the housekeeper and into the room. He was of middling height and build, dressed in a coat of decent but slightly worn broadcloth. His face was unremarkable, save for a pair of eyes that were too quick, too bright, like a magpie’s, assessing the value of everything they saw.

“Mrs. Vance,” he said, his voice smooth, with a faint hint of a London accent that she did not place. “A pleasure. I am Mr. Silas Croft.”

Eleanor inclined her head, her posture rigid. “Mr. Croft. I am not accustomed to receiving callers without prior arrangement. To what do I owe the pleasure?”

Croft smiled, a thin, bloodless affair. He took a step further into the room, his gaze sweeping over the polished rosewood table, the tasteful watercolours on the wall, the silver-backed hairbrush on her escritoire. “A fine place you have here. Very respectable. One must wonder how a woman alone affords such… tranquility.”

A cold prickle ran down Eleanor’s spine. “My business is my own, sir. If you have no lodging enquiry, I must ask you to leave.”

“Lodging? No, no,” he said, waving a dismissive hand. “I am here on a matter of a more… personal nature. A matter of family, you might say.” He paused, letting the word hang in the air. “Or rather, of a former family. A matter of a Mr. Arthur Croft.”

The name struck her like a physical blow. The air left her lungs in a silent rush. Arthur. The name she had buried so deep she sometimes forgot it herself. Her hands, still in her lap, clenched, the knuckles turning white. She kept her face a mask of polite incomprehension.

“I believe you have the wrong person, sir. I know no Mr. Croft.”

The man’s smile widened. “Oh, I think you do. Eleanor Croft. Or was it Eleanor Hayes before that? It gets so confusing, doesn’t it? All the names. But the eyes, you see. They never change. You have the same wary look you had the day the constables came for him in Cheapside.”

He knew. The locked room in her mind burst open, and the stench of cheap gin and despair flooded her senses. The cramped rooms above a chandler’s shop. The smell of ink and fear. Arthur, with his charming smile and his hands that could duplicate any signature, his dreams of a life they could never afford. And then the trial, the hushed courtroom, the sentence: fourteen years, transported to the penal colonies of New South Wales.

“You see,” Croft continued, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. “Arthur was my brother. I’ve just returned from a long trip myself. And in my travels, I heard a curious story. The story of a well-to-do widow in Bath, a Mrs. Vance, who arrived some ten years ago with a tidy sum and an impeccable reputation. A story that piqued my interest, given my own family history.”

Eleanor found her voice, though it was thin and strained. “My husband, Mr. Vance, was a merchant. He left me a modest inheritance. That is all.”

“Modest?” Croft chuckled, a dry, rattling sound. “He left you enough to start a new life. A life you built on a lie. A life you purchased with the silence about your convict husband.” He took another step, closer now, and she could smell the stale tobacco on his coat. “My poor, dear brother, rotting in the heat of the Antipodes. And his loving wife, living in the lap of luxury, forgetting all about him.”

“What do you want?” The question was a whisper, all pretence of defiance gone.

“Justice,” Croft said, his eyes gleaming. “Or, failing that, compensation. For the family that was ruined. For the brother that was lost. My travels have been… expensive. I find myself in need of funds. Five hundred pounds should suffice to settle my accounts and ease my conscience.”

Five hundred pounds. It was a fortune. It was the entirety of her savings, the capital that kept the lodging house afloat, the security that was the foundation of her entire existence. It was her life, the life she had clawed from the ashes.

“That is an impossible sum,” she breathed.

“I think not,” Croft countered smoothly. “I think this house, this life, this name you wear like a borrowed gown… I think it is worth every penny. If the Colonel upstairs, or the Misses Albright in the front room, were to learn that their respectable landlady is the wife of a forger and a felon… well. I imagine the quiet you value so highly would be rather disturbed. Your reputation would be in tatters. You would be left with nothing but the memory of a man you were so desperate to escape.”

He let the threat settle. He knew he had her. She saw it in the confident set of his jaw. He had not come for justice, or for his brother’s memory. He had come for money, and he had found the perfect lever to prise it from her.

Eleanor looked past him, out the window at the sun-drenched street. A carriage rolled by, its occupants laughing. The world outside was bright and clean and ordered. Her world. The world she had earned with years of scrubbing floors and managing accounts, of smiling at lodgers when she felt like weeping, of burying Eleanor Croft so deep that she had believed her dead.

Was this life worth the price? The alternative was ruin. Scandal. The workhouse. She would be dragged back into the mire from which she had pulled herself. She would become that frightened, desperate girl again.

Slowly, deliberately, she rose from her chair. Her movements were stiff, as if her bones were made of glass. She walked to the escritoire, her back to him, and opened a small, locked drawer. She took out a heavy cash box. Her hands did not tremble as she opened it. She counted out the banknotes, the crisp paper a final, bitter betrayal of the security she had so carefully cultivated. She placed them in a neat pile on the desk.

Croft was already moving, his magpie eyes fixed on the money. He scooped it up, counting it quickly before tucking it into an inner pocket.

“A pleasure doing business with you, Mrs. Vance,” he said, his voice slick with satisfaction. “You will not see me again.”

He turned and left the room without a backward glance. She heard his footsteps cross the hall, the front door open and close. The jangle of the bell was a final, mocking punctuation.

Eleanor stood perfectly still for a long time, the empty cash box on the desk before her. The sun had moved on, and the room was falling into shadow. The silence that descended was no longer peaceful. It was a heavy, suffocating thing, the silence of a vacuum, of something vital and irreplaceable having been sucked out of the world. The ticking of the longcase clock in the hall continued, steady and relentless, but it no longer sounded like a heartbeat. It sounded like a countdown.
