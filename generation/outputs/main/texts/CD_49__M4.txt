If there was one thing in this world that Mr. Obadiah Grimp knew, it was the art of sorrow; and if there was one thing he did not know, it was how to get out of a house once he had been mistaken for a mourner. His sorrow, I should explain, was of the professional sort—a decent, well-kept, hireable grief, purchased by the hour like a hackney-coach, and worn with a gravity that had never yet been known to crack. As a mute in the establishment of Messrs. Tallow & Sons, Undertakers to the Genteel, he had stood at the elbows of some two hundred bereaved families, had wept with his eyes while keeping his countenance, and had never once, in the whole course of that melancholy service, been asked his opinion upon anything more consequential than the arrangement of a hatband.

It was upon a raw November afternoon, then, that he found himself standing, in a black cloak and a hat with a weepers, in the churchyard of St. Botolph’s-in-the-Fields, attending the obsequies of one Mr. Ambrose Pinhorn, a gentleman of Kensington, who had amassed a considerable fortune in the tallow trade and had departed this life with the same gravity he had brought to the weighing of candles. The day was of a piece with the occasion: a low, sulky sky; a wind that went through a man’s coat as though it had a private grudge against him; and a fine thin rain that settled upon the black plumes of the hearse and made them look like the feathers of a drowned raven. Mr. Grimp stood with his hands folded, his chin upon his chest, and his eyes cast down at that exact angle which suggests a grief too deep for utterance but not too deep for observation, and he was thinking—I am sorry to say—not of the departed Mr. Pinhorn at all, but of a mutton chop and a pint of porter.

Now, it happened that Mr. Pinhorn’s relatives were few and scattered, and those who had come to see him laid in the earth were chiefly of that class which attends a funeral as it attends a public execution—for the spectacle, and the sensation, and the comfort of being alive. Among them, however, was a certain Mr. Josiah Pinhorn, a cousin of the deceased, a small, brisk, consequential man, with a face like a russet apple that has been kept too long, and a manner of such bustling importance that he seemed to be conducting the funeral, the churchyard, and the weather, all three, upon his own responsibility. This gentleman, chancing to glance along the line of mutes, fixed his eye upon Mr. Grimp, and started as though he had been shot.

‘Good heavens!’ cried Mr. Josiah Pinhorn, in a whisper that carried further than many a sermon. ‘Is that not—can it be—my dear Mr. Grimp? The very image of poor Ambrose’s eldest boy! The nose—the melancholy—the very cast of the countenance! I had thought him lost in the Indies!’

Mr. Grimp, thus addressed, made a small and ineffectual motion with his mouth, as though to say that he was not the person supposed; but the wind was against him, and Mr. Josiah Pinhorn was already at his elbow, pressing his hand with a warmth that quite undid the professional gravity of a lifetime.

‘Say no more, sir, say no more,’ whispered that gentleman, with tears in his eyes. ‘You are overcome. It is natural. It is beautiful. You shall come home with me to the reading of the will, and we shall hear what poor Ambrose has done for you, and you shall dine with me, and we shall be cousins together.’

‘Sir,’ said Mr. Grimp, ‘I am much obliged, but I am not—’

‘Not a word!’ cried Mr. Josiah Pinhorn. ‘Not a syllable! The grave has claimed him, but the family claims you. Into the coach with you!’

And into the coach went Mr. Obadiah Grimp, mute, hireable, and terrified beyond the power of speech, with his black cloak about him and his heart going like a watchman’s rattle.

The house in Kensington was one of those solid, square, comfortable, self-satisfied residences which the tallow trade, in its prosperity, loves to build for itself: a house with a portico, and a gravel sweep, and a dining-room in which a man might have dined a regiment, and a library in which he might have slept a regiment, and a general air of having been paid for on the nail. Into the dining-room were ushered the mourners and the curious, some twenty in number, and there, at the head of the long mahogany table, with the fire roaring and the rain beating upon the windows, sat Mr. Josiah Pinhorn, with Mr. Grimp beside him, holding that unhappy mute’s hand as though he were a child at a pantomime.

The will was read by a solicitor of a dry and cautious habit, who cleared his throat at every paragraph as though he were laying down the law and the law were a dusty carpet. There were legacies to servants, legacies to charities, legacies to a nephew in the Isle of Man, and then, in a voice that made the whole company sit up, the solicitor came to the following clause:

‘“And to my dear nephew, Obadiah Pinhorn, whom I have not seen these fifteen years, and whom I take to be somewhere upon the salt seas, I bequeath the sum of twenty thousand pounds, together with the house in Kensington and the whole of my stock in the tallow business.”’

There was a rustle, a murmur, a turning of heads. Mr. Josiah Pinhorn sprang from his chair and embraced Mr. Grimp before the whole company.

‘There!’ he cried. ‘There! Did I not say so? Did I not know him at a glance? Twenty thousand pounds! The very image of his father! Oh, the providence of it! Oh, the beautiful, beautiful nose!’

Mr. Grimp sat as one turned to stone. His mouth opened; his eyes rolled; his hands, which had been folded in professional sorrow for twenty years, now clutched the arms of his chair in an agony of private terror. He was a mute—a hired mute—a man who had never owned a house, a business, or a nephew, and who had not the faintest notion of what was expected of a man with twenty thousand pounds. He thought of Messrs. Tallow & Sons, and their displeasure; he thought of the police; he thought of the mutton chop and the porter, which now seemed the simple and honourable joys of a vanished age. He rose to his feet.

‘Ladies and gentlemen,’ said he, in a voice like a rusty hinge, ‘I am not—’

‘He is overcome!’ cried Mr. Josiah Pinhorn. ‘The dear fellow is overcome! Give him a glass of wine!’

A glass of wine was given him. He drank it. He sat down again.

‘I am not,’ he began once more, ‘the person—’

‘The very person!’ cried Mr. Josiah Pinhorn. ‘The very person we have been looking for these fifteen years! Three cheers for the nephew!’

The company gave three cheers. Mr. Grimp, who had never in his life been cheered by anybody, burst into tears—not professional tears, but the real, hot, frightened tears of a man who has lost his way in a world he never made—and the company, observing these, cheered again, and said that blood was blood, and that nature would out.

And so it was that Mr. Obadiah Grimp, mute, of the establishment of Messrs. Tallow & Sons, became, in the space of a single afternoon, the proprietor of a house in Kensington, a tallow business, and twenty thousand pounds; and so it was that he sat, long after the company had gone, in the great dining-room, with the fire burning low and the rain upon the windows, staring at the solicitor’s copy of the will, and wondering whether, if he confessed the truth upon the morrow, they would hang him or merely lock him up; and whether, in either case, he might be permitted to bring his own black cloak, and to stand, sorrowful and silent, at his own trial, as he had stood at so many funerals, with his chin upon his chest and his eyes cast down at that exact angle which suggests a grief too deep for utterance but not too deep for observation.
