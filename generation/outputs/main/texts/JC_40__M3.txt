I set it down here as though I had been an eyewitness to the events in that narrow room, though my information was fragmentary, fitted together from the halting confessions of a dying man and the mute, heavy silence of official archives. I wonder how he would have related it himself. He has confided so much in me that at times it seems as though he must come in presently and tell the story in his own words, in his careful, weary voice, with his precise foreign manner, a little puzzled, a little bothered, a little hurt, but now and then giving one of these glimpses of his very own self that were never any good for purposes of orientation. It’s difficult to believe he will never come. I shall never hear his voice again, nor shall I see his smooth, pale, ascetic face with that perpetual line of anxious thought on the forehead, and the youthful eyes darkened by some hidden perplexity to a profound, unfathomable grey.

It all begins with a remarkable document that fell into the hands of a man called Holst, who stole with complete success a dying revolutionary’s testament out of a small lodging house near the Carouge quarter. Till I discovered the fellow my information was incomplete, but most unexpectedly I did come upon him a few hours before he gave up his arrogant ghost. Fortunately he was willing and able to talk between the choking fits of asthma, and his racked body writhed with malicious exultation at the bare thought of Vassili. He exulted thus at the idea that he had “paid out the stuck-up beggar after all.” He gloated over his action. I had to bear the sunken glare of his fierce crow-footed eyes if I wanted to know; and so I bore it, reflecting how much certain forms of evil are akin to madness, derived from intense egoism, inflamed by resistance, tearing the soul to pieces, and giving factitious vigour to the body. The story also reveals unsuspected depths of cunning in the wretched Holst, whose abject and intense hate acts like a subtle inspiration, pointing out an unerring way towards revenge.

“I could see directly I set my eyes on him what sort of a fool he was,” gasped the dying Holst. “He a man! Hell! He was a hollow sham. As if he couldn’t have said straight out, ‘Hands off my father’s name!’ blast him! That would have been like a man! Rot his superior soul! He had me there—but he hadn’t devil enough in him to make an end of me. Not he! A thing like that letting me off as if I wasn’t worth a kick! . . .” Holst struggled desperately for breath. . . . “Fraud. . . . Letting me off. . . . And so I did make an end of him after all. . . .” He choked again. . . . “I expect this thing’ll kill me, but I shall die easy now. You . . . you here . . . I don’t know your name—I would give you a five-pound note if—if I had it—for the news—or my name’s not Holst. . . .” He grinned horribly. . . . “The publisher Holst.”

He said all these things in profound gasps, staring at me with his yellow eyes out of a long, ravaged, pallid face; he jerked his left arm; a grey matted beard hung almost into his lap; a dirty ragged blanket covered his legs. I had found him out in that wretched alley through that busybody Schaffner, the concierge, who had, confidentially, directed me where to look. It appears that a sort of loafing, fuddled vagabond—a Swiss printer living amongst the foreigners with a worn Valaisan woman—had considered it a great privilege to give a shelter to the last days of the famous publisher Holst. While he was talking to me in the wretched hovel, and, as it were, fighting for every minute of his life, the woman, with big bare arms and a stupid coarse face, sat in a dark corner chewing bread stolidly. Now and then she would get up for the purpose of shooing a pigeon away from the sill. The whole floor shook when she walked. An ugly pale child, thin and pot-bellied like a little heathen god, stood at the foot of the couch, finger in mouth, lost in a profound and calm contemplation of the dying man.

He talked feverishly; but in the middle of a word, perhaps, an invisible hand would take him by the throat, and he would look at me dumbly with an expression of doubt and anguish. He seemed to fear that I would get tired of waiting and go away, leaving him with his tale untold, with his exultation unexpressed. He died during the night, I believe, but by that time I had nothing more to learn.

So much as to Holst, for the present.

Eight months before this, coming into the offices of Holst & Brenner, I went as usual to see old Brenner in his private room. On the landing a lean, dark-eyed fellow in a long shabby coat greeted me shyly, and I remembered that I had seen him in the Russian colony, in Vassili’s lodgings, amongst other émigrés who used to come in the evening to talk interminably over their revolutionary reminiscences and to discuss politics. Vassili had pointed him out to me once as a respectable petty agitator owning a small printing press, who had showed himself “one of the best at the distribution of the proclamations.” I was not very surprised to see him, since any exile venturing as far as the publishing house would naturally find his way there. I returned his greeting and passed on. At the door of Brenner’s room I came upon another man in whom I recognised Korvin.

I asked him at once what he was doing there; it occurred to me that some new commission was afoot, some pamphlet to be issued, and I was curious, for Korvin was a man of fierce convictions, whose sallow, clean-shaven face, with its burning dark eyes, had always struck me as the very image of relentless thought. He looked at me strangely, with a sort of guarded, hesitating attention, as though he were not certain whether to speak or to remain silent. He said, with an odd intonation, that he had brought a manuscript—some memoirs—and he added, after a pause, that they were “very important for certain people.” Then he passed me, with a curt nod, and went down the stairs. I stood for a moment looking after him, and then I turned the handle and went in to see old Brenner.

He was sitting at his desk, a short, thick, bald man, with a heavy, pale face and loose, bluish lips, and he held in his hand a sheaf of papers. He looked up at me with a peculiar expression, half wary, half compassionate, and I felt at once that something unusual had happened. He motioned me to a chair, and, as I sat down, he laid the papers gently on the desk, and said, in his thick, deliberate voice: “I have here a piece of work for you, Vassili Alexeievitch. It is a translation—from the Russian. The memoirs of a man called Lomonov. You have heard of him, perhaps?”

I had heard of Lomonov. He had been a revolutionary of the old stamp, a man of implacable will, who had spent years in the fortress and had died, it was said, in exile, in some remote Siberian town, of cold and hardship. His name was a legend amongst the émigrés. I said that I had heard of him, and Brenner nodded slowly, his loose lips moving as though he were tasting something bitter.

“He was a great man, in his way,” he said. “And he has left these memoirs. They will make a book. It will be published. You will translate it.”

I asked him why he had chosen me, and he looked at me for a long time before he answered. Then he said, with a careful, deliberate emphasis: “Because you are the fittest. Because you are young, and because you are a Russian, and because—because you will understand certain things better than another.”

I did not know what he meant, but I took the manuscript and went away. It was not until I had read the first few pages that I understood. Lomonov, in his memoirs, had denounced, by name, a certain police agent who had, he declared, betrayed him and his comrades to the authorities. And the name of that agent was Alexei Vassilievitch K——. It was the name of my father.

You may imagine the effect of those words upon me. I sat in my room, with the manuscript before me, and I stared at the page as though it were a living thing, a venomous creature that had crept out of the darkness to sting me. My father had been dead for five years. He had been a man of quiet, studious habits, a teacher of languages, who had come to Geneva for his health, and who had lived there, in a small, modest lodging, respected by all who knew him. He had been a gentle, reserved man, with a certain sad dignity of manner, and I had always thought of him as a man of honour, of scrupulous, almost painful, honesty. And now this dead man, this Lomonov, this fanatic, hurled his accusation from the grave, and I was asked to translate it, to give it to the world, to make it my own, in a sense, by putting my words to it.

I went back to Brenner. I told him that I could not do it. He looked at me with his heavy, pale face, and he said, very quietly: “I thought you would say that. But you must. It is not a question of what you like or what you feel. It is a question of the truth. The book will be published. If you do not translate it, someone else will. And if you do not do it, it will be said that you are afraid—that you know something—that you are hiding something. You understand?”

I understood. I understood that I was caught in a web of circumstances, of suspicions, of half-truths, from which there was no escape. I understood that I had to translate the book, that I had to sit down, day after day, and put into French, with careful, precise, and scrupulous accuracy, the words that denounced my father as a traitor. And I understood, too, that I did not know whether the accusation was true or false. I had no means of knowing. My father had never spoken to me of his life in Russia, of his political opinions, of his friends or his enemies. He had been a silent man, and his silence had been, I had always thought, the silence of a man who had suffered and who wished to forget. But now that silence seemed to take on a different aspect—a darker, a more sinister, a more equivocal meaning. It might have been the silence of guilt, or it might have been the silence of a man who had been falsely accused and who disdained to defend himself. I did not know. I shall never know.
