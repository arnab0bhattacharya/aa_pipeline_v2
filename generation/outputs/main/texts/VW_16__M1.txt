There was the money; a round, solid sum, quite small, yet in her hand it felt like a stone, a weight, a pebble dropped from a height into the still, grey pond of her life. It was the price of the nursing, of the long nights, the smell of camphor and sickness, the sound of the clock ticking out the minutes of her grandmother’s last, slow breath. Now it was hers. And with it came the letter from her aunt, a thin, sharp-edged thing of reproach, its ink spidery and severe. *A journey? Alone? At such a time?* The words seemed to flutter in the still air of the parlour, dark, angular birds of disapproval. But the money was heavier than the words. It was a key, heavy and cold, to a door she had not known was there.

The journey itself was a blur of motion, a long, rattling unspooling of the thread that held her to England. Then, Palermo. She stepped from the train not into a city, but into a sound, a roar, a wave of heat that struck her face and arms and seemed to carry with it a thousand lives all speaking at once. For a moment she stood, stunned, a single drop of water in a torrent. But then, as she moved with the crowd, not understanding, not being understood, a strange calm settled upon her. The babble of the Sicilian tongue, which she could not follow, became a kind of music, a river of sound she could simply float upon. No one looked at her with the pitying, assessing eyes of her relations. No one asked her questions. She was as anonymous as a stone in a riverbed, and the feeling was not of loneliness, but of release.

Her room was high up, overlooking a narrow street where the sun fell in a brilliant, vertical shaft, illuminating the dust motes that danced in the air. From the balcony, she could see a sliver of the sea, a plate of hammered blue, and above it, the dome of a church, so golden it seemed to hold the sun’s fire within its curve. She bought oranges from a cart, their skins a dimpled, brilliant orange, and the juice ran down her chin, sweet and sharp. She bought bread, still warm from the oven, and ate it standing by the window, watching a woman on a balcony opposite shake out a red cloth, which billowed for a moment like a flag before being drawn back into the shadows. These were the events of her day, and they were enough. They were everything.

She would walk. For hours she would walk, her boots making no sound on the worn, uneven stones of the streets. The city was a palimpsest, layers of history worn smooth and visible in the stone of the walls, the Norman arches, the Saracen carvings, the peeling plaster of a more recent, more careless age. Light was the true inhabitant of the place. It poured into the squares, blinding and white, and in the deep alleys it was a cool, liquid green, filtered through the leaves of a lemon tree growing in a cracked pot. She did not need a guide. Her feet seemed to know the way, leading her towards the sea, towards the sound of the waves, towards the cool, dark interior of the Palatine Chapel, where the gold of the mosaics glowed in the gloom like a thousand watchful eyes. She would sit on a bench for an hour, not praying, not thinking, but simply being held in the quiet, ancient space, the scent of incense and old stone filling her senses.

Sometimes, on the coast road beyond the city, she would find a small cove, a crescent of pale sand between two rocks. She would take off her shoes and stockings and walk to the water’s edge. The sea was cool on her ankles, and the pebbles were smooth and round beneath her soles. She would sit on a rock, the sun warm on her back, and watch the light shatter on the surface of the water. It was here, in the silence broken only by the sigh of the waves, that the memories would come. Not as a flood, but as gentle, passing things. The weight of the water jug in her hand as she carried it upstairs. The particular, papery texture of her grandmother’s skin. The low, rattling sound of her breath in the final days. These things no longer hurt. They were simply facts, as real and as present as the salt on her lips or the heat of the sun on her hair. They were part of the landscape now, as much a part of her as the memory of the grey English rain. The nursing had been a long, grey corridor, and she had walked its length patiently. Now, at the end of it, there was this light, this sea, this glorious, unburdened solitude. She was not running away from anything. She had simply, at last, arrived.
