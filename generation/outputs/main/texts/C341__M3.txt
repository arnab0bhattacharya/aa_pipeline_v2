The first time Elias Thorne noticed the waterline on the lower stones of the boathouse, he put it down to his own exhaustion. It was the autumn of 1894, and he had been the steward of Blackmore Vale for fifteen years, managing the sprawling Shropshire estate with a meticulousness that bordered on obsession. But the mark was there again the following morning: a distinct, pale crust of dried silt some four inches below the usual waterline, as though the lake had retreated in the night and crept back before dawn. He mentioned it to Sir Arthur Blackmore over breakfast, expecting the old man to dismiss it as a trick of the late October light. Instead, Sir Arthur set down his coffee cup with a deliberate care that made Thorne's stomach tighten.

"Leave it alone, Elias," Sir Arthur said, not meeting his eyes. "The lake has its own rhythms. It always has."

But Thorne could not leave it alone. Over the following fortnight he rose at three in the morning, pulling on his greatcoat and walking down through the sleeping grounds to the edge of the water. On three separate nights he watched the lake diminish, the surface dropping steadily as though some enormous plug had been pulled from the earth beneath it. He could hear, faintly, the rush of water through stone, a sound that seemed to come from the deepest part of the lake bed, far out where the water was darkest. By four-thirty the draining would cease, and then, slowly, the level would rise again, replenished by some unseen mechanism he could not fathom. He found no pipes, no channels, no evidence of human intervention. Each morning the surface sat calm and full, reflecting the grey Shropshire sky as though nothing had happened.

When he pressed Sir Arthur a second time, suggesting they summon the county police, the old man's reaction unsettled him more than the lake itself. Sir Arthur stood from his desk and walked to the window, gripping the frame with both hands.

"I will not have constables trampling my grounds," he said, his voice low and strained. "I will not have them poking about the lake. Do you understand me, Elias? I am asking you, as a friend, to let this be."

Thorne understood then that Sir Arthur knew something. But the old man would say no more, and Thorne had never known him to be cruel or dishonest, only, in this single matter, afraid.

It was Mrs. Hale, the housekeeper, who gave Thorne the name. Her nephew had worked for a private investigator in Shrewsbury before the man had set up on his own, handling delicate matters for the gentry with a discretion that appealed to Thorne's sense of propriety. The investigator's name was Joseph Alderman, and he arrived at Blackmore Vale on a cold morning in early November, carrying a leather case and an air of quiet competence that Thorne found immediately reassuring.

Alderman was a lean man in his forties with a close beard and steady hands. He listened to Thorne's account without interruption, asking only a few precise questions, and then asked to see the lake. They walked together along the gravel path through the grounds, past the old walled garden and the stand of beeches that marked the boundary of the ornamental parkland. The lake lay before them, broad and still, its surface the colour of hammered pewter.

Alderman studied the waterline, knelt to examine the boathouse stonework, and then spent a long time staring at the centre of the lake, where the depth was greatest. He said little, but Thorne could see him thinking, fitting pieces together behind those pale grey eyes.

That night Alderman joined Thorne in his vigil. They stood in the shelter of the boathouse at half past three, watching. The draining began at ten minutes to four, the water shivering and then dropping with that same slow, deliberate steadiness Thorne had observed before. Alderman listened, tilting his head, and then did something Thorne had not thought to do. He walked to the southern shore, where the ground sloped down to the water's edge through a tangle of hawthorn and old hazel. Here, as the water dropped, a ridge of stone emerged from the shallows, mossy and slick, barely visible in the thin moonlight.

Alderman knelt and traced the stone with his fingers. It was not natural rock. It was cut, squared, set with mortar. He looked up at Thorne.

"There is a structure beneath the water," he said. "A building."

The following day Alderman went into the village and spent three hours at the parish church, speaking with the vicar, an elderly man named Crewe who had served the parish for nearly fifty years. What Alderman learned there he brought back to Thorne that evening, spreading out a hand-drawn map on the steward's desk.

"There was a chapel here," Alderman said, tapping the map. "The Chapel of St. Aldhelm. It stood on a rise of ground above a natural spring, and it served the village until the parish was consolidated in 1730. When the Blackmore family created the ornamental lake in the 1750s, they dammed the stream and flooded the hollow. The chapel was demolished, but its foundations remain. And beneath the chapel floor, according to the vicar, there was a crypt, and in that crypt a cistern fed by the spring, which the villagers considered holy."

Thorne stared at the map. "The water drains into the cistern."

"Precisely. The mechanism is natural, a siphon effect created by the geology of the hollow and the pressure of the spring. Under certain conditions, the water finds its way down through a fissure into the old cistern chamber beneath the chapel ruins. Then, as the pressure equalizes, the spring pushes the water back up into the lake. It has probably been happening for decades, perhaps longer. The question is why it has become noticeable now."

"And why Sir Arthur is so afraid of anyone discovering it."

Alderman was quiet for a moment. "The vicar told me something else. When the chapel was demolished, the Blackmore family made a point of sealing the crypt. The vicar's predecessor had recorded a note in the parish register about it. The family claimed it was for safety, but there was a local story that the crypt contained something the family wanted forgotten. A private burial, unrecorded, from the time of the Civil War. A member of the Blackmore family who had been excommunicated for some reason and could not be buried in consecrated ground."

Thorne felt the cold settle into him. He thought of Sir Arthur at the window, gripping the frame, his knuckles white.

The next morning Thorne went to Sir Arthur's study alone. He closed the door behind him and sat across from the old man, who looked up from his papers with an expression of weary resignation.

"I know about the chapel," Thorne said. "I know about the crypt."

Sir Arthur was silent for a long time. Then he opened a drawer and took out a bundle of papers, yellowed with age, tied with a black ribbon.

"My great-great-grandfather's confession," he said. "He was a younger son. His elder brother died during the siege of Shrewsbury, and the family maintained the death was from wounds received in battle. But the confession tells a different story. He killed his brother in a dispute over the estate, and he buried the body in the chapel crypt beneath the floor, where no one would think to look. When the lake was planned, he insisted the chapel be flooded. He wanted water over it, always. A seal."

"And now the water comes and goes," Thorne said quietly.

Sir Arthur's hands trembled. "If anyone were to drain the lake fully, to investigate the ruins, they might find the crypt. They might find him. The family name, Elias. Everything built since then, all of it resting on that lie."

Thorne looked at the old man across the desk, seeing him clearly for the first time in fifteen years, and understood that what he was being asked to protect was not a secret but a wound, an inheritance of guilt that had passed down through the generations like a dark heirloom. He took the papers, retied the ribbon, and slid them back across the desk.

"No one is draining the lake, Sir Arthur," he said. "The water comes and goes of its own accord. It always has. And the chapel will sleep beneath it for as long as I have anything to say about the matter."

He left the study and walked back down to the shore, where Alderman was waiting in the grey morning light. The lake lay still and full before them, keeping its old secrets, as it had done for a hundred and fifty years, and would go on doing, Thorne intended, for a hundred and fifty more.
