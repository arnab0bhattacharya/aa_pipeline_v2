The morning parlour at Blackwood Hall was steeped in a grey, creeping damp that seemed to emanate less from the Hampshire weather outside than from the temperament of its eldest resident. Sir Reginald Blackwood, a widowed baronet of declining fortunes and equally declining spirits, spent his days muttering into his port, leaving the management of the house—and the marital prospects of his daughters—entirely to the caprices of his elder child. Miss Constance Blackwood was twenty-eight, sharp-featured, and possessed of a singular, wearying rectitude. Her younger sister, Miss Eleanor Blackwood, was five-and-twenty, possessed of a modest competence in piano forte and a far less modest fund of latent stubbornness. It was upon Eleanor that the burden of the family’s revival was currently fixed, for she had attracted the attentions of two gentlemen at once. 

The first was Mr. Pendergast, a neighbour whose estate at neighbouring Brixcombe bordered their own diminished acres. Mr. Pendergast was immensely rich, possessed of four carriages, a hothouse of exotic pineapples, and an unerring capacity for tedium. He was a man of heavy brow and heavier conversation, which flowed with the sluggish inevitability of the local river, depositing silts of agricultural data and drainage statistics upon the listener. The second suitor was Mr. Edmund Grey, the new curate of the parish. Mr. Grey possessed an income barely sufficient to keep his horse in oats, but he possessed also a keen, lively eye, a ready wit, and a habit of looking at Eleanor as though she were the only soul in the county who might comprehend a jest. 

It was a Tuesday morning when Mr. Pendergast called. He sat in the very best chair in the drawing-room, his large hands resting upon his knees, detailing the precise composition of the soil in his lower meadow. Eleanor sat opposite, her face arranged into a mask of polite attention, though her mind was wandering towards the poetry of Mr. Cowper. Constance sat by the window, stitching a piece of tambour muslin with a fierce, rhythmic intensity. When Mr. Pendergast finally took his leave, bowing with the grace of a cumbersome merchant vessel, the parlour fell into a silence broken only by the snap of Constance’s thread.

“He is exceedingly attached to you, Eleanor,” Constance said, her gaze remaining fixed upon her needlework. 

“He is exceedingly attached to his lower meadow,” Eleanor replied, allowing herself a small, private smile. “I believe he views me as a potential accessory to his drainage ditch.”

“Do not be foolish,” Constance snapped, her dark eyes flashing upwards. “Mr. Pendergast is a man of substance. His character is unblemished, his fortune unencumbered, and his temper uniformly mild. A uniform temperament is the highest recommendation in a husband. You know perfectly well that our circumstances require a settlement. You have a duty to this family, and to Papa, to secure a man who can repair the roof of the east wing, which is currently leaking upon the portraits.”

Eleanor sighed, tracing the pattern on the rug with the toe of her boot. “I do not dispute the roof, Constance. But must I purchase the roof at the cost of my own sanity? If I marry Mr. Pendergast, I shall spend the remainder of my days hearing about turnips. I believe I should prefer the leak.”

Constance’s lips thinned into a bloodless line. “You are romantic, Ellie. Romance does not pay the butcher. I beg you will not let your head be turned by the other gentleman who haunts this house.”

By “the other gentleman,” Constance meant Mr. Grey, who was, at that very moment, approaching the front steps. He had come to return a volume of sermons borrowed by Sir Reginald, but his true motive was evident in the slight flush upon his cheek as he was shown into the room. Mr. Grey bowed to the sisters, his eyes lingering but a moment upon Eleanor, yet in that moment conveying a wealth of sympathy and shared amusement. 

“Mr. Grey,” Constance said, her tone possessing all the warmth of a January frost. “How good of you to call. Papa is in his study, I believe. He will be relieved to have his book returned.”

“I am afraid I waylaid him in the village,” Mr. Grey replied, taking a seat at Eleanor’s invitation. “He was engaged in a vigorous debate with the blacksmith over the quality of the local iron. I felt it prudent to leave him to it.”

Eleanor laughed, a bright, clear sound in the stuffy room. “You were wise, sir. Papa’s debates over iron could outlast a siege.”

Mr. Grey smiled, leaning forward slightly. “I have no doubt. But I confess, my interruption of his lordship was entirely selfish. I had hoped to find you at home, Miss Blackwood. I encountered Mr. Pendergast at the gate. He was kind enough to inform me that the soil in his lower meadow is exceptionally rich in marl.”

Eleanor’s eyes danced. “Did he, indeed? I am overjoyed to hear it. I was fearing for the marl.”

Constance cleared her throat, a sharp, metallic sound. “Mr. Pendergast is a most conscientious landlord. It is a great virtue to be informed of the land’s yield.”

“Undoubtedly,” Mr. Grey agreed, though his gaze remained upon Eleanor, a silent conspiracy in his eyes. “Though I find my own thoughts more occupied with the heavens than the earth of late. There is to be a meteor shower on Thursday next, Miss Blackwood. I wonder if you might do me the honour of observing it from the terrace? The night sky is a fine subject for reflection.”

Constance looked up, her needle arrested in mid-air. “Eleanor does not care for the night air, Mr. Grey. She is excessively subject to chills.”

“I am not excessively subject to chills, Constance,” Eleanor said firmly, a spark of defiance igniting in her chest. “I have never had a chill in my life. I should very much like to observe the meteors, Mr. Grey. Thank you.”

When the curate departed, the parlour descended into a brooding silence. Constance laid down her needlework with deliberate care. “You are behaving with a gross imprudence, Eleanor. A curate! A man without two hundred pounds a year to his name. You think him clever because he quotes poetry and speaks of stars. I assure you, cleverness is a poor companion when there is no coal for the fire.”

“I am aware of Mr. Grey’s circumstances,” Eleanor replied, her voice quiet but steady. “I am also aware of my own. I am five-and-twenty, Constance. If I do not marry Mr. Pendergast, I shall not be a burden to you. I can govern a house, or perhaps serve as a governess. I do not require a fortune, but I do require a companion who sees me as something other than a means to repair the east wing.”

Constance rose, pacing the floor with an agitated step. “You are blind. You think Mr. Grey is disinterested? He is a fortune-hunter, Ellie. He sees the daughter of a baronet, however impoverished, and thinks to elevate himself. I will not have it. I forbid you to meet him on Thursday.”

Eleanor looked at her sister, a sudden, cool clarity settling over her. She saw the rigid fear of spinsterhood in Constance’s eyes, the desperate need to see her sister settled in wealth so that the family might be rescued and Constance herself might be spared the indignity of acknowledging her own lack of suitors. Constance was not advising Eleanor; she was bargaining for her own peace of mind.

“You cannot forbid me, Constance,” Eleanor said gently, though the finality in her tone was unyielding. “I am of age.”

“You will ruin us all!” Constance cried, her composure fracturing. “You will break Papa’s heart. Mr. Pendergast is everything that is respectable. Mr. Grey is nothing but a charming, penniless interloper.”

“If Mr. Grey is an interloper, then he is a welcome one,” Eleanor said, rising from her chair. “He speaks to me as though I possess a mind. Mr. Pendergast speaks to me as though I possess a garden. I have made my choice, Constance. I shall meet Mr. Grey on Thursday, and if he asks me to be his wife, I shall accept him.”

Constance stared at her sister, her face a mask of cold fury. “You will regret this, Eleanor. You will live to regret it.”

Eleanor crossed to the window, looking out over the damp, grey gardens of Blackwood Hall. The east wing was, indeed, leaking. The family was, indeed, poor. But as she watched a figure on horseback crest the distant hill, she felt a strange, buoyant lightness in her chest. She had spent her life being manageable, agreeable, and guided by the stern hand of her elder sister. She had accepted her place in the shadow of Constance’s formidable authority. But now, in the quiet rebellion of choosing a poor curate over a rich neighbour, she found an unexpected, fierce joy. She was not choosing Mr. Grey to spite Constance, though spite was certainly a pleasant accompaniment. She was choosing him because, in his clever, impoverished eyes, she saw a reflection of the woman she wished to become. 

“Perhaps I shall regret it,” Eleanor murmured, not turning from the window. “But I suspect I shall regret it far less than I should regret the turnips.”

Behind her, Constance made a sound of deep disgust, gathered her muslin, and swept from the room. The door slammed with a sharp, satisfying crack. Eleanor remained at the window, watching the clouds break over the Hampshire downs, allowing herself, for the first time in years, to smile without constraint.
