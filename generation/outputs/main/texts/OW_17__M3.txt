Lord Mortimer Drexel was a young man of such excessively modern principles that he had quite abandoned the use of his reason, relying instead upon the latest philosophical fashions imported from Paris. His brother, the Honourable Cyril, was equally modern, though he expressed his modernity through a profound devotion to the colour of his waistcoats and a total ignorance of everything except the precise social standing of his acquaintances. They were, in short, perfectly typical products of the English educational system, which takes great care to ensure that young men leave school with a thorough mastery of nothing whatsoever.

It was generally agreed that their aunt, Mrs. Penelope Vane, was entirely without morals, which is perhaps the highest compliment one can pay a woman of leisure. She had survived three husbands, and buried them with such admirable composure that society suspected her of having chosen them solely for the quality of their marble. She now occupied a tall, narrow house in Bayswater, a district which possesses the unique distinction of being entirely without atmosphere, unless one counts the damp. Her drawing-room was decorated in the aesthetic manner, full of peacock feathers, sunflowers of somewhat alarming brass, and Japanese screens that concealed nothing but the wall behind them. It was here that she chose to hold her séance.

Mrs. Vane had recently discovered that she was psychic. She made the discovery one afternoon while bending over a cup of tea, when she perceived, in the arrangement of the leaves, a perfect resemblance to the Duke of Wellington. As she had never met the Duke, and as he had been dead for nearly forty years, the significance of the vision was entirely obscure, but she considered it proof enough of her occult gifts. The following Tuesday she invited her nephews to assist her in communing with the dead.

‘My dear boys,’ she said, as they entered the dim parlour, where the curtains had been drawn against the late afternoon in a manner that suggested both mystery and a desire to conceal the upholstery, ‘I have invited you because the spirits have been exceedingly restless. They have been knocking upon the wainscoting with such persistence that I fear for the mahogany.’

‘Surely, Aunt Penelope,’ said Cyril, settling his gloves with the care of a man who considers gloves the chief business of life, ‘the dead have better things to do than tap upon woodwork. If they must knock, they might at least have the decency to employ a doorbell.’

‘The dead, Cyril, have no decency at all. That is one of the many advantages of being dead. One is released from the tyranny of manners. Besides, the spirit who knocks is no stranger. He claims to be your grandfather, the old Admiral.’

‘Poor Grandfather,’ said Mortimer. ‘I was under the impression he had found eternal rest. It seems dashed inconsiderate of him to come back now, just when one had ceased to think about him.’

‘The dead are always inconsiderate. That is why the living find them so entertaining.’

Mrs. Vane bade them sit, and they joined hands about a small mahogany table that trembled with the fine, nervous energy of a fashionable hostess awaiting her guests. A single lamp cast a greenish glow over the scene, rendering the young men rather paler than was strictly fashionable, and lending Mrs. Vane the appearance of a meditative emerald.

For some minutes nothing occurred, save the distant wailing of a cat in the square, a sound which Mrs. Vane declared was of no occult significance whatever, but merely a Bayswater animal expressing its opinion of the weather. Then the table began to rock. It did so with a slow, deliberate rhythm, as though it were thinking.

‘He is here,’ whispered Mrs. Vane. ‘Admiral Drexel, are you with us?’

The table tilted sharply upon two legs and struck the floor once.

‘One for yes, dear Aunt?’ asked Cyril.

‘One for yes, two for no, three for I don’t know, and four for mind your own business,’ she replied. ‘It is the standard spiritualist code.’

A voice then arose from somewhere beneath the mahogany. It was gruff, hoarse, and possessed of that unmistakable naval authority which even death had failed to soften.

‘My grandsons,’ it declared. ‘I have returned from the other side to demand that you confess your sins.’

Cyril and Mortimer exchanged glances of genuine alarm. Not because a spirit had spoken, but because it had asked for a confession. There are few things more terrifying to an Englishman than the prospect of discussing his own transgressions in company.

‘My sins, Grandfather?’ said Mortimer, recovering himself with commendable speed. ‘I’m not at all certain I have any. I pay my tailor regularly, I never read novels after dinner, and I have never once voted Liberal. What more could be expected?’

‘You have spent,’ the voice continued, growing in resonance, ‘six hundred pounds on a single Persian rug.’

‘That is not a sin,’ said Mortimer. ‘That is an investment in beauty. Besides, it was only five hundred and eighty.’

‘And you, Cyril,’ said the voice, turning with the precision of a compass needle. ‘You have been seen at the Horse Guards wearing a brown boot with a grey suit.’

Cyril turned the colour of a very well-bred beetroot. ‘It was not brown,’ he said, in a voice of considerable agitation. ‘It was a particularly deep shade of tan. There is all the difference in the world.’

‘The dead are notoriously poor judges of colour,’ murmured Mrs. Vane, who had opened her eyes and was observing the proceedings with an expression of serene satisfaction.

‘Confess!’ commanded the Admiral. ‘Confess, or I shall tip this table upon you.’

The table gave a violent lurch, and a small porcelain vase toppled from the mantelpiece with the tragic inevitability of a fallen woman. Cyril, who had the instincts of a sportsman, lunged beneath the cloth and seized what he firmly believed to be a spectral ankle. What he actually seized was the ankle of Mrs. Vane’s butler, Higgins, who had been concealed beneath the table in a position of considerable discomfort for the better part of an hour.

‘Good heavens!’ cried Cyril. ‘It is Higgins!’

‘I beg your pardon, sir,’ said Higgins, emerging with the quiet dignity of a man whose salary is quite adequate. ‘I have a slight cold, and the Admiral appears to have taken up residence in my throat.’

Mrs. Vane regarded her butler with an expression of mild disappointment, not at the deception, but at the execution. ‘Higgins,’ she said, ‘your Admiral is entirely unconvincing. My father-in-law was a Rear-Admiral, and never raised his voice above a whisper, except when discussing the Irish question. You have made him sound like a common commodore.’

‘I did my best, madam, with the material provided.’

‘Material is everything,’ said Mortimer, who was brushing dust from his knee. ‘One cannot make a convincing ghost out of a butler. It is like trying to make a salad without oil. The essential element is missing.’

‘And what,’ asked Cyril, ‘is the essential element of a ghost?’

‘Belief, my dear Cyril. Without it, a ghost is merely a draught, and a butler is merely a servant. With it, a butler might be anything at all, though preferably still a butler.’

Mrs. Vane rose and rang for tea, which is the English response to all supernatural occurrences. ‘I am afraid,’ she said, ‘that spiritualism, like marriage, is an institution which depends entirely upon the credulity of at least one party. I had hoped, my dear boys, that you would provide that credulity.’

‘We are far too well educated to be credulous, Aunt Penelope,’ said Mortimer. ‘That is our great misfortune. A man who believes nothing can never be disappointed, but he can never be entertained either. We are, I fear, condemned to the dreary satisfaction of knowing better.’

‘Knowing better is the curse of the modern age,’ said Mrs. Vane, pouring the tea with a hand that did not tremble. ‘In my youth, we knew nothing, and we were perfectly happy. We believed in ghosts, and in the monarchy, and in the healing power of hot soup. Now nobody believes in anything except progress, and progress, I am told, is merely the process of exchanging one set of inconveniences for another.’

Higgins, having been dismissed to the kitchen, where he belonged, did not reappear, and the three sat down to their tea with the comfortable composure of people who have just witnessed the impossible and found it rather vulgar.

‘Do you know,’ said Cyril, reaching for a cucumber sandwich, ‘I rather think I prefer a live grandfather to a dead one. A live grandfather may be tedious, but at least he does not demand one’s sins before supper.’

‘My dear Cyril,’ said Mrs. Vane, ‘the living are always demanding sins. They simply call them confidences, and repeat them at dinner. The dead, at least, have the decency to keep what they hear to themselves.’

‘That,’ said Mortimer, ‘is the strongest argument for spiritualism I have yet encountered. If the dead can keep a secret, they are the only ones who can. I shall take up the practice at once, and I shall begin by believing absolutely everything Aunt Penelope tells me, which, if I am not mistaken, is the definition of true faith.’

‘True faith,’ said Mrs. Vane, ‘is merely the art of pretending that the impossible is obvious. I have practised it for forty years, and I have never been disappointed, except by other people.’

Outside, the Bayswater dusk settled over the square like a grey silk veil, and somewhere in the wainscoting, a board creaked once, softly, and was still.
