It was upon a raw November morning, with a yellow fog creeping up from the Thames, that Miss Eleanor Vance called upon us in my friend’s chambers. She was a tall, pale girl, with the straight back and quiet resolution of her calling, and her grey eyes held the heavy look of one who has watched through many sleepless hours.

“You will pardon the intrusion, Mr. Holmes,” said she, “but I am at my wits’ end. My father is the Reverend Arthur Vance, vicar of Saint Botolph’s in Southwark. He is a man of iron constitution and unshakeable nerve. Yet, a week ago, he returned from a night visit to a dying parishioner in Pinchin Street, near the river, and has not been the same man since.”

“Describe the change, Miss Vance,” said Holmes, leaning forward with his hands clasped over his knee.

“He came home past midnight, pale as marble, and with his clerical coat torn at the collar. He would not answer my questions, but locked himself in his study. On the following Sunday, he ascended into the pulpit as usual, opened the Bible, looked out over the congregation, and was utterly silent. He stood there, trembling, for two full minutes before descending the steps and retiring to the vestry. He has not preached since. Every night he sits at his desk writing a letter, and every morning at dawn he burns it in the grate. I implore you to discover what occurred in that poor street by the river.”

Holmes asked a few further questions, noting the exact hour of the Reverend’s return, the state of his boots, and the nature of the parishioner he had gone to visit. Miss Vance could only say that the man was a former clerk named Josiah Cobb, lately fallen into poverty and drink.

“We must go to Southwark, Watson,” said Holmes, when our visitor had departed. “There is a secret here which has struck at the root of a good man’s conscience. But first, let us see what Lestrade may tell us of Josiah Cobb.”

We found the Scotland Yard inspector at his office. He consulted his notes.

“Cobb,” said Lestrade. “Yes, we had our eye on him. An ex-solicitor’s clerk, convicted fourteen years ago of forgery. He was released from Dartmoor three months ago. Found dead in his room in Pinchin Street two days after this clergyman’s visit. The doctor said it was heart failure, brought on by drink and exposure. There was nothing to be done.”

Holmes thanked him, and we took a hansom southward. The fog thickened as we crossed the river, and the streets of Southwark were slick with moisture. We came at last to a narrow, reeking alley, and pushed open the door of the lodging-house where Cobb had died. The landlady, a heavy, slatternly woman, showed us the room.

It was a wretched garret, bare of furniture save for a cot, a chair, and a deal table. The window looked out upon a blank brick wall. Holmes examined the floor, the threshold, and the table, with his usual swift, methodical attention. Presently he paused, and I saw his eyes brighten.

“You had a visitor here on the night Cobb died,” said he.

“A gentleman, sir,” said the landlady. “A clergyman. He came to see poor Cobb. I heard them talking, and then I heard a great cry, and the clergyman came running down the stairs, white as a sheet.”

“Was anyone else in the house?”

“There was a man waiting outside, sir. A rough man, with a cap pulled low. I saw him from the window, standing under the lamp-post. When the clergyman came out, the man spoke to him, and then they went off together.”

Holmes nodded, and drew from his pocket a small piece of stiff paper which he had lifted from a crack in the deal table. It was a fragment, torn from a larger sheet, bearing a few words in a cramped, shaking hand.

“I perceive,” said Holmes, “that Cobb was not merely a dying man. He was a dying man with a confession to make, and a demand to accompany it.”

We returned to Baker Street, and Holmes spent the afternoon in composing a telegram. It was addressed to a Mr. Silas Blackwood, at an address in the Borough. The reply came within the hour.

“He will come,” said Holmes. “I have told him that the clergyman’s letter is complete, and that it will be placed in the hands of the police tomorrow. We may expect him by eight o’clock. I think, Watson, that you will put your service revolver where you can reach it.”

At the appointed hour our visitor was shown in. He was a large, coarse-featured man, with a heavy jaw and small, shrewd eyes. His clothes were rough, and he wore a billycock hat, which he did not remove.

“Well, governor,” said he, “what’s the game? You say as how the parson’s got a letter, and I’m to come and talk about it. I don’t know nothing about no letter.”

“No,” said Holmes, “you know nothing about the letter. But you know a great deal about the death of Josiah Cobb. You were waiting under the lamp-post in Pinchin Street when the Reverend Mr. Vance came out, and you walked part of the way home with him.”

The man started, and his face darkened.

“I don’t know what you’re talking about.”

“I think you do. Cobb, you see, had been a clerk in the office of a London solicitor. Fourteen years ago a sum of twelve hundred pounds was stolen from a client’s account. Cobb was convicted of forgery, but the money was never recovered. He served his time, and came out to die. But before he died, he sent for the vicar of Saint Botolph’s, for he had, in his last hours, a terror of going to his grave with a lie upon his soul. He made his confession. The money had not been taken by Cobb alone. There was an accomplice—a man who had planned the theft, and who had taken the lion’s share. That accomplice was you, Blackwood.”

The man’s face turned a mottled purple. “It’s a lie!” he snarled.

“It is the truth,” said Holmes, calmly. “Cobb told Vance everything, and Vance, in the horror and shock of the moment, cried out. You were waiting outside, for you had followed Cobb’s movements and knew the clergyman was there. You heard that cry, and you knew the game was up. You intercepted Vance on the road, and you told him that if he breathed a word of what he had heard, you would make it known that the Vicar of Saint Botolph’s had been consorting with a convicted criminal and had harboured stolen money. You threatened his reputation, his living, and his daughter’s future. And then, to make sure, you went back into that room and you silenced Cobb forever.”

“I never touched him!”

“Then why,” said Holmes, “did you leave your muddy thumbprint upon the edge of the deal table, and why is there a bruise upon the dead man’s throat which the doctor, in his haste, took for the mark of disease? It was no heart failure, Blackwood. It was the grip of a strong, cruel hand. The Reverend Mr. Vance saw that bruise, and he knew. But he is a man of God, and he could not bring himself to send a fellow-creature to the gallows, nor could he face the scandal which you held over him. So he has sat down every night to write a letter to the police, and every morning he has burned it, because his conscience and his cowardice have fought each other to a standstill.”

Blackwood stood for a moment, glaring. Then, with a sudden, savage movement, he flung himself toward the door. But Holmes was too quick for him, and I am proud to say that my revolver covered him before he had taken two steps.

“It is no use, Blackwood,” said Holmes. “Inspector Lestrade is waiting below. You will walk down with him, I think.”

When the man had been removed, Holmes turned to me, and I saw that look of quiet satisfaction upon his face which I knew so well.

“And now, Watson,” said he, “I think we may walk over to Southwark and relieve the Reverend Mr. Vance of his burden. The letter which he burns every morning need never be written again.”
