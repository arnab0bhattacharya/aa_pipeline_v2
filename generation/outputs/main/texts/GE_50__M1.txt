It is a common enough observation that the weight of a public office does not fall in proportion to the emoluments it confers, but rather to the private conscience of the man who bears it; and if this be true in the great world of Westminster, it is a truth felt with a peculiar and grinding pressure in the small parishes of the Midlands, where the overseer of the poor is at once the paymaster of the destitute and the bookkeeper of the rates. Mr. Josiah Grimes, a tenant-farmer of the parish of Little Kettleby, had been elected to this dignity at Michaelmas, and the honour sat upon him not unlike a borrowed coat, heavy in the shoulders and far too long in the sleeves.

Josiah was a man of substance in the way of land and stock, but he was poor in that invisible currency of letters which the modern world has made the sole key to its counting-houses. He could read the face of the sky for rain and the face of an ox for temper, but a column of figures in a ledger was to him a dark forest wherein he dared not set foot, lest he be lost and made a fool of before the vestry. It was this deficiency that weighed upon him now as he sat at his kitchen table in the evening light, the parish books open before him like a silent accuser.

"Thee know'st well enough, Martha," he said to his wife, a sharp-eyed woman who was knitting with a velocity that seemed to mock the slowness of his own thoughts, "that if I cannot check the reckonings, I am but a cat's paw for those that can. Old Bate, the clerk, he writes 'em down fast enough, but how am I to know if he has not written down a pound where a shilling should be? It is a thing that gnaws at a man."

Martha paused, her needles clicking. "Then thee must get the schoolmaster to read 'em over to thee, Josiah. It is no shame to be unlettered, but it is a shame to be cheated."

"I'll not have the schoolmaster," Josiah muttered, a flush rising to his weather-beaten cheeks. "He's a gossip, and he'll have it all round the village that the new overseer cannot do his sums. I'd sooner go to the workhouse myself."

It was at this moment that their daughter, Eliza, a girl of nineteen with a face that held more thought than beauty, set down her mending and came to the table. She was a quiet girl, but her eyes, dark and steady, missed little that passed before them.

"Father," she said, her voice soft but clear, "let me do it. I have the head for figures that thee hast for the turnips. I will keep the accounts for thee, and no one need know but we three."

Josiah looked at her, at the delicate hands and the earnest brow, and felt a sudden, fierce protectiveness mingled with a desperate hope. "Thee, lass? It is a heavy task, and full of deceit."

"I am not afraid of deceit," Eliza replied. "I am only afraid of thee being wronged, and the poor being wronged with thee."

And so the bargain was struck. Eliza took the ledger to her own room, and by the light of a tallow candle, she began the work of deciphering the hand of Mr. Bate. It was a cramped, secretive script, full of abbreviations and flourishes that seemed designed to hide rather than to reveal. But Eliza was patient; she traced the entries for bread, for coal, for the pauper nurse, for the burial of old Widow Thorne. She compared the sums with the receipts she begged her father to collect.

For a week, the columns balanced to her satisfaction. But then, in the third week, she found the discrepancy. It was a small thing at first: a shilling here, a sixpence there, noted as "incidentals" or "sundries." But they were frequent, and they never appeared in the receipts. Eliza sat back, her heart beating a slow, heavy rhythm against her ribs. She saw the shape of it now, the pattern of the spider in the web. The money was not going to the poor; it was going to the hand that wrote the words.

She did not speak of it at supper. She waited until the house was still, until the wind was rattling the casements and the fire had burned low. She found her father in the barn, checking the harness by the light of a lantern.

"Father," she said, and the tone of her voice made him look up sharply. "I have found it. The books are a lie."

Josiah set down the bridle. "What say'st thee, lass?"

"Old Bate has been stealing from the rate," Eliza said, the words falling like stones into the silence. "He has been putting money in his own pocket and writing it down as bread for the hungry. He has been robbing the widow and the orphan to fill his purse."

The colour drained from Josiah’s face. He was a man who believed in the order of things, in the honesty of his neighbours, even in the honesty of a rogue like Bate, provided the rules were followed. To hear of such cold, calculated villainy was to him like hearing that the sun had risen in the west.

"But... but he is the clerk," Josiah stammered. "He has been so for twenty years. He is a respectable man."

"He is a thief," Eliza said firmly. "And if thee signs those books, Father, thee will be as guilty as he. Thee wilt be the fence for his stolen goods."

Josiah sank onto a bale of hay, the lantern throwing long, distorted shadows against the walls. He saw the trap now. If he accused Bate, there would be a scandal, a lawsuit perhaps, and he, the unlettered farmer, would be dragged into the mire. But if he stayed silent, he would be complicit in the suffering of the very people he had sworn to help.

"What must I do?" he whispered.

"Thee must go to the magistrates," Eliza said. "Thee must show them the proof. I have written it all out, clear as day. Let them see what a girl can do that a man of twenty years' standing has undone."

Josiah looked at his daughter, this quiet girl who had suddenly become the judge of his conscience. He felt the weight of the office lift slightly, not because the burden was gone, but because he was no longer carrying it alone. He stood up, his back straighter than it had been in months.

"Aye," he said, his voice gaining strength. "Thee art right, lass. It is a bitter pill, but it must be swallowed. I'll not have the blood of the poor on my hands, not for all the Bates in Leicestershire."

He took the lantern, and together they walked back to the house, the father and the daughter, the unlettered man and the keeper of his conscience, ready to face the storm that the morning would bring. For in the moral accounting of the universe, as George Eliot might have observed, it is not the figures that matter, but the heart that balances them.
