The orders, when they arrived, had the dry, precise, and utterly final sound of a coffin lid being screwed down. They came up from the coast in the hands of a fever-stricken messenger who had little breath left for anything beyond the business of his delivery, and they were written in the immaculate copperplate of the company’s head clerk, a man who had never felt the true weight of the heat. The paper itself, a flimsy, official thing, seemed an absurdity in the great, humid silence of the station, a piece of distant, bureaucratic reality asserting itself in a world of mud, water, and green immensity. Mr. Thorne, the agent, read it standing on the veranda, the words swimming for a moment before his eyes as if the river’s mist had got into them. *Abandon Station B-7. Proceed to Kikondja with all records and company property. Await transport to the coast.* The language was a model of corporate clarity, leaving no room for interpretation, for the slow, heavy currents of feeling that had, over three years, gathered about this place in his heart. It was not a request; it was a dismissal, a recall of a soul from purgatory to an unknown, and perhaps no less purgatorial, heaven.

He stood for a long time after the messenger had been given quinine and a mat in the shade, the paper held loosely in his hand. The sun, a white, merciless eye in a pale sky, beat down upon the clearing, and the silence of the afternoon was not an absence of sound but a presence—the immense, breathing silence of the forest, punctuated only by the drone of a fat, black fly and the distant, rhythmic splash of the river boys filling their gourds. This station, with its peeling whitewash and its roof of rusted corrugated iron, was a fragile assertion of order against the overwhelming, indifferent chaos of the continent. He had come to it a younger man, full of a brittle, European certainty. He had believed in ledgers and timetables, in the company’s mandate to bring a semblance of commerce to a place that knew only the commerce of spears and silence. But the forest does not permit such certainties to remain unchallenged. It seeps into a man, works its way into the joints of his character, until the damp rot of the place is in his very bones. He had grown accustomed to the scent of the river at dawn, a smell of mud and decay and life all mingled; he knew the language of the rains, the subtle shifts in the wind that promised a storm. He had, without ever admitting it to himself, come to belong here.

It was at this moment of profound and disquieting reflection that he saw M’Kono approaching from the path that led to the village. The chief moved with the unhurried, rolling gait of a man completely at one with the earth beneath his feet. He was not a large man, but he possessed a gravity that seemed to draw the very air around him into a state of deference. His face, a mask of old scars and deep-set lines, was impassive, yet his eyes, dark and liquid, missed nothing. He did not speak at once, but came to a halt a few feet from the veranda, leaning on his ironwood staff, his gaze fixed on Thorne’s face, reading there the story the paper in his hand had written. The company’s property—the strange, silent white man—was troubled, and this trouble was a matter for the chief’s concern.

“The spirits of the river are heavy today,” M’Kono said at last, his voice a low rumble, like stones shifting in the current. His French was halting, formal, learned in fragments from a long-dead missionary, but it served. “They carry bad news.”

Thorne folded the paper carefully, the crisp sound an intrusion. “The Company calls me back to the coast, M’Kono.”

The chief’s expression did not change, but a stillness came over him, a solidification, as if he had become a part of the landscape itself. “The Company is a word on paper. It is far away. It does not know the taste of the water from our springs, or the way the leopards cry at the new moon. Why does it call you?”

“Its business is finished here,” Thorne said, the words tasting like ash in his mouth. He felt a profound sense of shame, as if he were confessing a betrayal. “They have orders from their masters in Europe.”

M’Kono took a step closer, his gaze unwavering. “And you? Are you a piece of paper to be sent here and there by a distant hand? We have made a pact, you and I. You keep the fever-medicine for my people. You show us the way of the iron tools. In return, my spears keep the peace on the river. The white men from the lower villages do not trouble you. The leopard does not take your goats. This is our understanding. It is not written on paper. It is in the air we breathe.” He gestured with his staff towards the clearing, the station, the river. “This place is yours now. Your sweat is in this soil. Your spirit is in the trees we cleared. If you leave, the peace leaves with you. The bad spirits will return.”

Thorne looked from the chief’s face to the station he had come to think of as his own. He saw the clinic, a simple mud-and-wattle hut where he had spent countless nights wiping the brows of the sick. He saw the storehouse, its shelves neatly stacked with trade goods that had become, for him, the sacraments of a fragile peace. He had been given orders to abandon a company station. But M’Kono was asking him to abandon something else entirely—a trust, a relationship, a world he had inadvertently co-created. His loyalty, he realised with a jolt of moral vertigo, was no longer a simple matter of employment. It had been fractured, divided between the abstract authority of the Company and the concrete, living authority of the man standing before him. Whose orders, in the end, did he follow? The ghost-like command of men in a city he could barely remember, or the quiet, desperate plea of the man whose life was inextricably bound to his own?

“I cannot stay,” Thorne said, and the words were a lie even as he spoke them. “The orders are clear.”

M’Kono simply watched him, his dark eyes holding a deep, weary wisdom. He had seen many things come and go on the river: traders, soldiers, missionaries, all of them full of their own importance, all of them eventually claimed by the forest or the fever. He saw in Thorne not just a white man, but the anchor of a new and precarious balance. “The paper speaks with the voice of a man who is not here,” the chief said slowly. “My voice is the voice of the man who is here. I ask you to stay. Not for the Company. For this place. For the people. For the peace that is yours and mine.”

He turned then, without another word, and began the slow walk back towards his village, his staff making soft, rhythmic thuds in the red earth. Thorne remained on the veranda, the official order clutched in his hand. The sun began to sink, casting long, distorted shadows from the trees. The air grew thick and cool, and the first of the evening’s insects began their incessant, pulsating chorus. He was a company agent, a functionary, a man bound by a contract. But as the darkness gathered, swallowing the clearing, the river, and the immense, waiting forest, he felt the terrifying weight of a choice that was not written on any paper, a choice between two masters, two worlds, and the silent, damning knowledge that to obey one was to betray the other. The river flowed on, indifferent, carrying away the light and leaving him alone with the sound of his own breathing in the profound and unsettling dark.
