The rain had not ceased for three days, and the gutters of Cawdor Street ran like millraces, carrying the filth of the town down toward the river. Inside the shop, Mr. Elias Thorne stood behind his counter, folding a length of black bombazine with the careful, deliberate motions of a man who found comfort in the texture of things. The cloth was good—it was always good—and the act of measuring and cutting and folding had for twenty years been his bulwark against the world’s disorder. He was a draper, and he was a pious man, and in the town of Ashcombe those two facts were generally spoken in the same breath, as though one were the natural consequence of the other.

The bell above the door jangled, admitting a gust of damp air and a small, sodden figure. It was Polly Meekin, one of the charity school’s girls, her bonnet drooping like a drowned bird. She had been sent for a card of buttons, but her hands trembled so badly that the coins spilled across the counter.

“Why, child, you’re all of a shake,” said Thorne, retrieving a sixpence from the floor. “Is it the cold?”

Polly shook her head, her eyes fixed on the counter. She was nine years old, thin as a spindle, and she had the particular stillness of a child who has learned that movement invites notice. Thorne, who had four children of his own and a seat on the school’s board, prided himself on noticing.

“Come now,” he said, more gently. “Has something frightened you?”

She looked up then, and he saw that her left ear was swollen and red, the lobe split where an earring might have hung. “Please, sir,” she whispered, “I’m not to say.”

Thorne felt a coldness that had nothing to do with the rain. He knew that ear. He had seen it two Sundays past at the chapel, when the child had sung in the gallery, and it had been whole then. He gave her the buttons and a penny besides, and watched her scurry out into the wet, her small boots splashing through the puddles.

That evening, at the board meeting of the Ashcombe Charity School, Thorne sat in his usual place at the long table in the vestry, the smell of damp hymnbooks and coal smoke thick in the air. The Reverend Mr. Josiah Pell presided, his voice a smooth and sonorous instrument, and beside him sat his son, Mr. Gideon Pell, the school’s master. Gideon was a young man of five-and-twenty, with a pale, handsome face and a way of smiling that never quite reached his eyes. He had been given the post, everyone knew, because he was the minister’s son, and because the minister had been the one to recommend Elias Thorne to the board in the first place, twelve years ago, when Thorne was a struggling shopkeeper with a growing family and a hunger for respectability.

The meeting concerned the price of slates and the appointment of a new washerwoman. Thorne listened, or appeared to, but his mind was on Polly Meekin’s ear. When the business was concluded and the others rose to leave, he lingered.

“Mr. Pell,” he said, addressing the minister, “might I have a word? Concerning the children.”

The Reverend Pell smiled. “Of course, Mr. Thorne. You are always diligent in their welfare.”

Gideon, halfway to the door, paused. “Is there some complaint, sir?” His tone was light, almost amused.

Thorne felt the weight of the moment. He thought of his shop, his four children, the pew he rented at the chapel, the respect he had labored twenty years to earn. He thought of the way the minister’s recommendation had opened doors for him, how a word from Pell could close them just as easily. And he thought of Polly Meekin’s split ear, and the way she had said, *I’m not to say.*

“I observed a child today,” Thorne said slowly, “with an injury to her ear. She seemed afraid to speak of it.”

Gideon’s smile did not waver. “Ah, the Meekin girl. A stubborn creature. She required correction.”

“Correction,” Thorne repeated.

“The rod, Mr. Thorne. It is the school’s method, and a biblical one. ‘He that spareth his rod hateth his son.’ You are a man of scripture, I know.”

Thorne was. He had read Proverbs many times, and had never once felt the rod in his own father’s hand. But he had also read of the shepherd who breaks the lamb’s leg to keep it from straying, and he had always thought that shepherd a poor sort of shepherd.

“The injury seemed severe,” he said.

The Reverend Pell interposed, his voice gentle. “Gideon is young and zealous, Mr. Thorne. The children are the children of the poor, and the poor require a firm hand. You know this. You came from little yourself.”

It was true. Thorne had come from little—a farmer’s son, apprenticed at twelve, married at twenty to a woman who had brought him nothing but a good name and a steady hand. He had built his shop from nothing, and he had done it by never offending the men who mattered. The minister was such a man.

“I only thought,” Thorne said, “that the board might wish to inquire.”

“The board,” said Gideon, “is satisfied with my methods. Are you not, Father?”

The minister smiled. “I am. And I am sure Mr. Thorne is as well. He is a sensible man. A man of business. He knows that a school cannot be run on sentiment.”

Thorne walked home through the rain, his coat dark with water, his mind a turmoil. His wife, Margaret, was mending by the fire when he came in, and she looked up at his face and set down her needle.

“What is it?” she asked.

He told her. She listened without interruption, her hands folded in her lap, and when he had finished she was silent for a long moment.

“You cannot speak,” she said at last. “You know you cannot. The minister would ruin us. He would turn the chapel against us, and the chapel is our custom. Half the town buys their cloth from you because you sit on that board.”

“And if I do not speak, the child will be beaten again. Others will be beaten.”

“You do not know that. Perhaps it was a single instance.”

“I know what I saw.”

Margaret looked at him, and her eyes were sad. “Elias, you have always been a good man. But goodness is a luxury for those who can afford it. We cannot afford it.”

He lay awake that night, listening to the rain. He thought of his own children, sleeping in the room above, and of the Meekin girl, whose father was a laborer and whose mother was dead. He thought of the verse in Proverbs, and of another verse, from the Gospel of Matthew, which said that it were better for a man to have a millstone hanged about his neck and be drowned in the depth of the sea than that he should offend one of these little ones. He wondered which verse the minister would preach on next Sunday.

The next morning, he rose early and walked to the school. It was a low, stone building behind the chapel, and through the window he could see the children at their lessons. Gideon Pell stood at the front, a cane in his hand, and as Thorne watched, he brought it down across the shoulders of a boy who had mispronounced a word. The boy did not cry out. He had learned not to.

Thorne stood in the rain for a long time. Then he turned and walked to the shop, and he took down his ledger, and he wrote a letter to the board of the charity school, requesting that the matter of the master’s discipline be brought before them at their next meeting. He signed it with his name, and he sealed it with wax, and he sent it by his boy before he could change his mind.

He knew what it would cost him. He knew the minister would hear of it before the day was out, and that the chapel would be cold to him on Sunday, and that some of his customers would find other drapers. He knew that Margaret would weep, and that his children would not understand.

But he had folded cloth for twenty years, and he had learned that a thing must be measured true, or it was worthless. He had measured himself that morning, and found himself wanting. He would not be found wanting again.
