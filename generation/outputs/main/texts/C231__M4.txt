The house at Thackley Rise was never quiet. Even in the small schoolroom at the top of the stairs, with its one window looking out over the mill yard and the grey stone roofs tumbling down toward the canal, there was always some distant industry to hear: the clatter of the looms, the rumble of a cart on the setts, the servants below stairs, the endless opening and closing of doors that seemed to be the pulse of the place. Miss Ellen Wray had grown used to it. After eleven months as governess to the two younger Ashworth girls, she could have traced the whole household by its sounds alone.

It was a Thursday in early October, and the light through the window was thin and yellow, the colour of the smoke that hung perpetually over the valley. Ellen sat at the table with a copybook open before her, correcting little Clara’s sums. Clara, who was nine, had a habit of humming under her breath when she was thinking, a low, tuneless drone that set Ellen’s teeth on edge and which she had long ago decided not to notice. Across the room, Beatrice, who was twelve and considered herself nearly grown, was practising her watercolours, her brush making a soft, wet scratching against the paper.

The door opened without a knock, and Mrs. Ashworth came in. She was a tall, handsome woman whose black silk dresses and quick, decisive manner made her seem, in the confined space of the schoolroom, like a visitor from a larger and more important world. She held a letter in her hand.

“Miss Wray, we shall want the girls at four o’clock. Mr. Hargreaves is coming to dine, and there is to be an announcement. The girls may come down to hear it. They will wear their white muslin.”

“Yes, ma’am,” Ellen said, rising.

Mrs. Ashworth’s gaze swept the room, taking in the watercolour, the sums, the neat rows of books, and Ellen felt as she always did under that gaze, examined and found adequate, which was somehow worse than being found wanting. “And you will keep them to their lessons until then. I do not want them excited and foolish.”

“Of course, ma’am.”

When the door had closed, Beatrice set down her brush with a deliberation that was entirely her own. “An announcement,” she said. “It will be about Edmund, will it not, Miss Wray?”

Ellen’s hands were busy with the copybook, closing it, smoothing the cover. “I could not say, Beatrice.”

“But you know it will be. Mr. Hargreaves is his particular friend. And he has been here a great deal lately.” Beatrice’s voice was knowing, and she watched Ellen with a curiosity that was far too sharp for a girl of twelve. “He is to marry Miss Hargreaves. Everyone says so. She has ten thousand pounds.”

“Beatrice, that is not a proper subject for speculation.”

“But it is true, is it not?”

Ellen looked up. The child’s face was bright with the pleasure of gossip, and something else, too, a kind of testing. Ellen had been aware, for some weeks now, of a change in Beatrice’s manner toward her, a new and unsettling watchfulness. “It is not for us to discuss,” Ellen said, and her voice was steady, which pleased her. “Now, your perspective is very poor. You have made the mill chimney quite flat. Try it again.”

She turned back to Clara’s sums, but the figures swam before her eyes. Edmund Ashworth. The eldest son. He was twenty-four, and he had been in London for most of the summer, and when he had come home in September he had brought her a book, a volume of poems by a man named Tennyson, because, he said, he had heard her mention the name. He had given it to her in the hall, in front of the servants, with a careless kindness that had made her cheeks burn. She had not mentioned the name. She was sure of it. And yet he had remembered, or pretended to remember, and the book was on her shelf now, its spine uncracked, because she could not bring herself to read it.

At four o’clock they went down. The drawing-room at Thackley Rise was a large, square room with a Turkey carpet and a fire that was always lit, even in October, and it was full of people: Mr. Ashworth, red-faced and hearty, with his watch chain straining across his waistcoat; Mrs. Ashworth, regal by the mantelpiece; Edmund, standing by the window with his hands behind his back; and Mr. Hargreaves, a stout, pleasant-looking man with a loud laugh. And beside him, a young woman Ellen had not seen before, in a dress of pale blue silk, with dark hair and a round, pretty face.

“Ah, the girls,” Mr. Ashworth said. “Come in, come in. You know Mr. Hargreaves. And this is his daughter, Miss Hargreaves. Miss Catherine Hargreaves.”

The young woman smiled at them. She had a dimple in one cheek and a way of tilting her head that was undeniably charming. “I have heard so much about you both,” she said. “Edmund tells me you are very clever.”

Beatrice simpered. Clara stared. Ellen, standing behind them, felt the floor of the room shift, very slightly, as though it had become a deck. She had known. Of course she had known. But knowing a thing and hearing it spoken are different, and for a moment she could not look at Edmund at all. When she did, he was looking at Miss Hargreaves with an expression of such open, uncomplicated happiness that something in Ellen’s chest turned over and lay still.

“Miss Wray,” Mrs. Ashworth said, “you may take the girls up now. They have heard the announcement.”

The announcement. Edmund and Miss Hargreaves were to be married in the spring. Ellen curtsied, and took the girls by the hand, and led them out. In the hall, Beatrice said, “I told you so, Miss Wray,” and Ellen said, “Yes, Beatrice, you did,” and her voice was perfectly calm.

That night, after the girls were in bed, she sat in her own small room under the eaves. The book of poems was on the shelf. She took it down and opened it at random, and read a line about a man who stood upon a world and felt the sadness of it, and she closed the book again and put it back. She did not cry. She was not, she told herself, a woman who cried. She was a governess, and she had her position to think of, and her salary, and her mother in Skipton who depended upon it. She had known men like Edmund Ashworth all her life, or thought she had: men who were kind to you because it cost them nothing, who gave you books and asked after your health and never once considered that you might mistake it for something more. It was her own foolishness, and she would not indulge it.

In the morning, she came down to the schoolroom at half past seven as usual, and laid out the books, and when Beatrice came in, sulky and sleepy, she said, “We shall begin with the history. You have not read the chapter on the Commonwealth.”

“I hate the Commonwealth,” Beatrice said.

“That is no reason not to read it,” Ellen said, and she sat down at the table, and folded her hands, and waited. Through the window, the smoke was rising from the mill chimneys, and the looms were already clattering, and somewhere below stairs a door opened and closed. The house at Thackley Rise was never quiet. And Ellen Wray, who was twenty-two years old and had her living to earn, picked up her pencil and began, once more, to teach.
