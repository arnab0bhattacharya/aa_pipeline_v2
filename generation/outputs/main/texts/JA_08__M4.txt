The village of Hartwell Green had never been remarkable for anything but its green, which was large, and its pond, which was small; and Miss Cassandra Whitmore, having lived there all her life, had long since ceased to expect that either would alter. She was the younger daughter of a country attorney, a man of good sense and small practice, who had died two winters since, leaving his family the house in which they lived, a respectable name, and very little else. It was therefore accounted a most fortunate circumstance—fortunate to the point of providential—when, in the spring of the present year, a distant cousin of her mother’s, a childless widower of retired habits and uncertain temper, was pleased to depart this life and to remember Cassie in his will to the amount of four thousand pounds.

Four thousand pounds is not a fortune to make a noise in the world; but in Hartwell Green it made a very pretty whisper indeed. Mrs. Ellinor Pemberton, the elder sister, married four years and settled at the neighbouring town of Eversham, was of opinion that it might be made to do a great deal more. She came over within the week, her carriage full of patterns and her head full of schemes, and sat down with Cassie in the little parlour as though she had been sent for.

“My dear,” said she, “you are now an heiress, and you must behave like one. Not a great heiress—I do not mean that; nobody will expect you to keep a phaeton—but enough of one to be particular. A woman with four thousand pounds may choose her acquaintance; a woman without it must be thankful for any.”

Cassie, who had been three-and-twenty years thankful for any, observed mildly that she had always found the acquaintance of Hartwell Green very tolerable.

“Tolerable!” cried Mrs. Pemberton. “My dear Cassie, tolerable is the very word I would have you unlearn. You are to be genteel now. You are to be nice. There is a difference between the woman who is civil to everybody and the woman who is civil to the right people, and it is the whole difference between being liked and being respected.”

She then produced her patterns. The blue muslin was too young, the sprigged cotton too rustic, the plain straw too plain; and before the afternoon was out, Cassie had been fitted, in imagination, from head to foot, and had promised to order the dove-coloured silk with the demi-train, which she privately thought the dullest stuff she had ever seen, and the little lace cap which Mrs. Pemberton declared indispensable for a woman of property.

For a season, Cassie did as she was bid, and did it so exactly that she became the admiration of Eversham. She wore the dove-coloured silk to the assembly and was pronounced elegant; she refused the acquaintance of the apothecary’s wife and was pronounced prudent; she listened to Mrs. Pemberton upon the subject of the Rector’s daughters—who were well enough, but pushing—and was pronounced a young woman of great propriety. She sat at the tea-table of Mrs. General Ferrers, whose husband had been in India, and said little; she walked with the Miss Lawrences, whose father had two thousand a year, and said nothing; and she returned from every visit with the same impression of having been extremely well received and not at all entertained.

It is a melancholy truth, that a young lady may be universally approved and yet be very dull. Cassie was approved of by everybody, and by nobody more than by Mrs. Pemberton, who wrote to her every Tuesday, and whose letters were a sort of moral calendar, in which every day’s conduct was entered and allowed. “I hear you were seen at church in the straw bonnet—quite right, my dear, it becomes you, and it is not too smart for the morning.” “I am told you sat next to Miss Lawrence at the concert—very judicious; her father’s interest at the county election may be of use to us yet.” Cassie read these letters, and answered them dutifully, and wondered why it was that being so much in the right should feel so much like being in the wrong.

It was in the second week of October that the matter was brought to a point. The occasion was a dinner at the house of Mr. and Mrs. Ferrers, and the subject was a small party of pleasure which the young people of Eversham proposed to make to a ruined abbey some six miles distant, a place of no great beauty, but of considerable fashion, and therefore much frequented. Mrs. Pemberton had already written upon the subject: “By all means go, my dear, if you are asked; but go in the barouche, and take care to be seen with the Lawrences. There is no harm in a picnic, properly conducted, and it will show that you are not above the amusements of the neighbourhood.” Cassie had accordingly resolved to go, and had thought no more about it, until the evening of the Ferrers’ dinner, when Mr. Ferrers himself, a jolly, careless man, who liked his dinner and his joke, began to rally her upon the subject.

“And so, Miss Whitmore, you are to be of the abbey party. I hear you are to carry the hamper. I hope you will not let the Miss Lawrences starve you, for they are all for economy, I am told, since the election.”

Cassie laughed, and said she hoped there would be enough for everybody.

“Enough! I dare say there will be. But I hear the whole party is to be under Mrs. Pemberton’s direction. She is to marshal you all, I suppose, and tell you which way to look at the ruins. Upon my word, I never knew a woman so fond of ordering things. She would order the sun out of the heavens if she could, and the moon after it.”

This was said with good humour, and the company laughed; but Cassie, to her own surprise, did not laugh with them. She felt, all at once, a little tired of being marshalled. She thought of the dove-coloured silk, and the lace cap, and the Tuesday letters, and the Miss Lawrences, and the whole long season of being so very much approved of; and it came into her head, with the force of a discovery, that she had never in her life made a decision entirely her own, and that she was now three-and-twenty, and had four thousand pounds, and might, if she pleased, decide something for herself.

“I believe, sir,” said she, with a composure that surprised her, “that I shall not go to the abbey after all.”

Mr. Ferrers stared. “Not go! Why, everybody goes. Your sister will be quite distressed.”

“My sister will be very kind, I dare say,” said Cassie, “but I have a great mind to spend the day at home.”

“At home! Upon my word, that is a strange fancy. What will you do at home?”

“I shall read a book,” said Cassie, “and walk to the pond, and be very dull and very happy.”

There was a general outcry, and a great deal of rallying, and somebody said it was a whim, and somebody else said it was a pity, and Mrs. Ferrers, who was kind, said she hoped Miss Whitmore would change her mind; but Cassie only smiled, and shook her head, and held to her resolution with a pleasant obstinacy which no one had ever seen in her before.

When she came home, she wrote to her sister. She sat a long while over the letter, for she wished to be neither unkind nor apologetic, and it is difficult to be neither when one is contradicting a person who has been in the right for a twelvemonth. At last she wrote: “My dear Ellinor,—I have determined not to go to the abbey. I know you will think it odd, and perhaps ungrateful, after all your care of me; but I find that I have been so long accustomed to do what is proper, that I have almost forgotten how to do what is pleasant, and I think it is time I learned. Do not be angry with me. I shall come to you on Friday, and you may scold me as much as you please, but I shall not go to the abbey.”

She folded it, and sealed it, and looked out of the window at the green, where the leaves were beginning to fall, and the pond, which was as small as ever. She was not at all sure that she had done a wise thing; she was only quite sure that she had done her own thing, and that it was the first time in her life, and that she was very much pleased with it. And if Mrs. Pemberton, on reading the letter, declared that Cassie was throwing herself away, and that four thousand pounds without conduct was no better than four hundred, it may be answered that conduct which is all another person’s is not conduct at all, and that a woman may be very dull at home and yet be very well employed, especially if she is reading a book, and has a fire, and is three-and-twenty, and has just discovered that her own mind is a tolerable companion.
