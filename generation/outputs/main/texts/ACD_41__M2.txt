The letter had arrived on a Tuesday, its envelope thick with the particular weight of foreign paper, and by Thursday the household of Mr. Arthur Penhaligon had changed from one of prosperous ease to one of tense, silent dread. I received the call from Miss Penhaligon on a grey morning in November, the fog hanging low over the streets of Bayswater like a damp shroud. She was a young woman of distinct nerve, her eyes bright and feverish behind their spectacles, and she did not waste time with pleasantries.

“Mr. Hale,” she said, her voice trembling slightly, “my father has not returned from his walk. It is nearly four o’clock, and the rain has been falling in sheets. He never fails to come in before three. I beg you to come at once.”

I had known the Penhaligons for some years. Arthur Penhaligon was a man who had made his fortune in the goldfields of Western Australia, a place where the sun bleached the mind as surely as it bleached the bones. He was a stout, red-faced man who spoke of London with the disdain of a barbarian viewing a polished jewel, and he had returned from his colonial sojourn with a peculiar stoicism that his wife, a gentle and anxious woman, found difficult to fathom.

We drove to their residence in a hired hansom, the wheels churning through the slush. When we arrived, the door was flung open before we could knock. Mrs. Penhaligon stood in the hall, wringing her hands, while a servant hovered uncertainly nearby.

“He was well enough at breakfast,” Mrs. Penhaligon said, her voice thin. “He took his usual stroll. But he had a letter that morning. A parcel from Australia. He locked himself in his study and did not emerge for two hours. When he came out, he would say nothing of its contents. He only smiled—a smile I had never seen in him before. It was cold, Mr. Hale. It was like the look of a man who has just signed his own death warrant.”

“Did he take his revolver?” I asked, glancing at the hall stand where the heavy, ornate weapon was conspicuously absent.

“Yes,” she whispered. “He carried it in his breast pocket. I tried to stop him, but he said, ‘I must have it, my dear. The past has a way of returning to collect its debts.’”

I followed the sound of their weeping to the study. It was a room dominated by a heavy oak desk, the air stale with the scent of pipe tobacco and old paper. On the desk lay the culprit: a small, browned piece of canvas, no larger than a handkerchief, stained with mud and what appeared to be dried blood. In the center, scratched into the fabric with a sharp point, were four letters: **J. R. V. M.**

Beside it sat a nugget of gold, rough and jagged, weighing perhaps three ounces. It glittered dourly in the dim light.

“Who are J. R. V. M.?” I asked, picking up the canvas. It was coarse, like the material used for mining sacks.

“I do not know,” Miss Penhaligon said, standing by the door. “He has spoken of the goldfields, but always in the past tense. He says he left his troubles in the dirt. But when this arrived, he changed. He became watchful. He checked the locks twice. He looked at every stranger on the pavement with a suspicion that made me shudder.”

I examined the letters. They were not carved with a knife, but scratched with a nail, deep into the weave. The hand that made them had been steady, perhaps frantic, but certainly deliberate.

“Did he have enemies out there?” I asked.

“He had partners,” Miss Penhaligon said. “A man named Corbett. They had a dispute over a claim. Arthur said Corbett died of fever. But there were rumors... rumors that Corbett was not the only one who felt cheated.”

I looked at the nugget. It was a tangible link to a world of sweat and greed, a world where a man’s word was worth less than the gold in his pocket. If Arthur Penhaligon had carried that gold, he had carried a target on his back. But why now? Why after ten years in London?

“He took a walk,” I said slowly. “Where does he usually walk?”

“Down to the river,” Mrs. Penhaligon said. “He says the smell of the water reminds him of the Wattle River. He likes to stand on the embankment and look at the ships.”

“Then that is where we must look,” I said. “The rain will have washed away any footprints, but it will not have washed away a man who is afraid. A man who is afraid does not walk; he runs. Or he hides.”

We went out into the downpour. The streets of Bayswater were slick and grey, the gas lamps burning orange circles in the mist. I walked quickly, my mind racing through the possibilities. A robbery? A murder? Or simply a man driven to distraction by a sudden, terrifying memory?

We reached the embankment at Vauxhall. The rain lashed against the stone railing. I ran my hand along the cold metal. There was no sign of a struggle, no blood, no broken glass. But as I looked down at the black water, I noticed something in the mud on the walkway. A distinct impression. Not a boot, but a shoe. A soft-soled shoe, the kind a gentleman might wear for a casual stroll. And beside it, a trail of mud that led not toward the river, but away from it, up the path toward the gardens.

“He went into the park,” I said. “He did not go to the water. He was fleeing something on the embankment.”

We hurried into the gardens. The trees were dark, dripping canopies. I saw a figure moving in the distance. It was a man, small and hunched, moving with a quick, darting pace. He was wearing a grey coat and a cap pulled low.

“Stop!” I shouted.

The man did not stop. He ran, his steps light and erratic. I gave chase, Miss Penhaligon and Mrs. Penhaligon following at a distance. The chase wound through the wet hedges and across the open lawns. The man was fast, but he was not a young man. I could hear the gasp of his breathing. He turned a corner by the boating lake and vanished into the shadows of the boathouse.

I rounded the corner and found him. He was backed against the wall, a revolver in his hand, his face pale and contorted with terror. But it was not his face that stopped my breath. It was the face of the man who had been chasing him.

Standing behind the small man was Arthur Penhaligon.

He was not running. He was standing perfectly still, his own revolver raised, his face a mask of terrible calm. The rain plastered his hair to his forehead. He looked at me, and for the first time, I saw not the merchant, but the man from the goldfields.

“Put down your weapon, Arthur,” I said, my voice steady. “It is over.”

“It is not over,” Arthur said. His voice was flat, devoid of emotion. “He stole it. He stole the gold, and he killed Corbett. I paid for it. I paid him to stay silent. But he is not silent. He sent me the canvas. He sent me the nugget. He is here, Mr. Hale. He is here to finish the job.”

I looked at the small man he was pointing at. The man lowered his weapon, his face crumbling in despair.

“Arthur,” the small man said, his voice cracking. “I didn’t mean for it to come to this. I just wanted my share. Corbett was a liar. He had the map to the real vein. We were all cheated.”

“You were cheated,” Arthur agreed. “But I was not. I had the gold. And now I have the truth. It was you, wasn’t it? You and Corbett. You planned to kill me and take the rest.”

The small man, whose name I later learned was Silas, dropped to his knees in the mud. “I didn’t know he would send the canvas to you. I thought he had kept it. I came here to warn you, Arthur. To tell you to run.”

Arthur looked down at him, then at me. He lowered his revolver. The tension in the air seemed to snap, replaced by a heavy, exhausted silence.

“Take him away,” Arthur said. “And take my revolver. I have no use for it anymore.”

As the police took Silas into custody, Arthur Penhaligon stood alone in the rain. He looked at the nugget in my hand, then at the dark water of the lake.

“The past is a heavy thing, Mr. Hale,” he said. “It is easier to carry a revolver than a memory. But I find the memory is heavier. I think I shall go back to the study. I have a great deal of thinking to do.”

He turned and walked back toward the house, his shoulders slumped, the merchant’s dignity gone, leaving only a tired old man who had finally put down the weight he had carried for ten years. I watched him go, the rain washing the mud from my boots, and I thought of the world’s obvious things, and how often the most dangerous ones are hidden in plain sight, waiting for a single, jagged piece of canvas to reveal them.
