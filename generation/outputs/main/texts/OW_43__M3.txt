Lord Arthur Pomeroy had achieved the impossible: he had made himself universally respected, and universally detested, by the remarkably simple expedient of giving away half of his vast fortune. It was a wholly unprecedented act, and London society, which prides itself on knowing the exact market value of every human soul, was entirely at a loss to know how to classify him. 

He had distributed his millions among the hospitals, the art galleries, and the various societies for the suppression of virtues that nobody seriously practised. When the last cheque had been signed, he was left with merely a paltry half-million a year and the entire undivided weight of the public’s approval. The poor, of course, blessed him. The rich, who had not thought of the scheme first, pronounced him a dangerously eccentric socialist. 

Lady Pomeroy, his wife, found his philanthropy entirely tiresome. She was a woman of exquisite fragility, who wore her melancholy as gracefully as she wore her sables, and who firmly believed that the only true purpose of wealth was to provide a sufficiently beautiful backdrop for one’s own ennui. She thought the poor were very much better left in the shadows, where the picturesque squalor of their lives might provide a pleasing contrast to the glittering drawing-rooms of Mayfair, without ever actually intruding upon them. To her, Arthur’s mania for endowing slums and building model lodgings was a distinct breach of good taste.

“You have quite ruined the East End, Arthur,” she had remarked to him only the previous evening, as she adjusted a rope of black pearls in a looking-glass. “You have taken away its charming despair, and replaced it with sanitation. Nothing is so dreadfully unromantic as a drain. It is the sort of thing that should be kept strictly out of sight, like the kitchen, or one’s relatives.”

Arthur had merely smiled, a placid, infuriating smile that always made her feel she had been reading a poorly translated French novel. 

The catastrophe occurred on a Tuesday morning. The *Morning Meteor*, a journal that combined the highest principles of Toryism with the lowest forms of gossip, published a thunderous editorial. It declared that Lord Pomeroy’s much-lauded generosity was nothing more than a monstrous speculation. He had not given his money away; he had merely purchased a peerless reputation. The newspaper demanded to know what a man who had bought his sainthood so cheaply had to do with the common, honest virtue of the English gentleman, who traditionally does nothing for nobody and expects the same in return.

Lady Pomeroy was reclining on a damask sofa in her boudoir when she read the article. The paper trembled slightly in her delicate, ringless hands. She was not angry because her husband had been slandered. She was furious because the slander was so profoundly vulgar. To accuse a Pomeroy of buying a reputation was as dreadfully middle-class as asking the price of a picture, or paying one’s taxes. 

She swept into the amber-hued library, where Lord Pomeroy sat peacefully arranging a vast array of rare orchids in a vase of Venetian glass. He was a man of forty, with eyes of a curious, faded grey, and an expression of permanent, well-bred indifference. He looked like a man who had just declined a particularly tedious invitation to dinner.

“Have you seen this abominable thing?” Lady Pomeroy cried, tossing the newspaper onto a table of lacquered ebony. “It is infamous! It is coarse! They say you have bought your reputation. They say your philanthropy is merely a commercial transaction.”

Lord Pomeroy adjusted the petal of a purple Cattleya with a slender pair of silver tweezers. “I should have thought,” he murmured gently, “that a commercial transaction was the one perfectly respectable thing left in modern England. But I confess I have not read it. Is the typography very alarming?”

“It is a scandal!” she repeated, pacing the Persian carpet, her silk skirts hissing like a nest of fashionable adders. “You must demand a retraction. You must sue them for libel. You must do something terribly English and aggressively litigious.”

“My dear Agatha,” said Arthur, pausing to examine a stain of pollen on his fingertip, “to sue a newspaper is to admit that one reads it. Besides, libel suits are the amusements of the middle classes. They have to do something to fill the intervals between their meals. I prefer to leave them to the bankrupt peers and the disappointed actresses.”

“You are too utterly indifferent,” she cried. “It is your name they are dragging through the journalistic gutters. You, who have given away millions! You, who are the most generous man in London!”

“I am afraid,” said Lord Pomeroy, plucking a dead bloom from the stem, “that I must contradict you. I am not generous at all. I am excessively selfish. I gave the money away because I found it such a terribly heavy thing to carry about. Money is like a very dull companion; it insists on talking about itself all the time. I simply wanted a little peace and quiet. I assure you, I did it entirely for my own comfort. The hospitals and the galleries were merely an accident. The newspaper is quite right. I did buy something. I bought my own solitude.”

Lady Pomeroy stared at him. For a moment, she was struck utterly dumb, a condition so rare in her that it almost constituted a medical miracle. 

“You mean,” she whispered, a new, strange light breaking in her beautiful, weary eyes, “that you never cared for the poor at all?”

“My dear Agatha, nobody cares for the poor. To care for the poor would be to admit that they exist, which is the one solecism that society will never forgive. I merely disliked my money. It was becoming a positive nuisance. It was beginning to dictate my tailor, my horses, and my politics. So I threw half of it overboard to lighten the ship.”

A soft, silvery laugh broke from Lady Pomeroy’s lips. It was the first genuine laugh she had uttered in years, and it had a curious, enchanting sound, like the falling of a single drop of water into a crystal basin. 

“How perfectly exquisite!” she murmured. “How perfectly, beautifully wicked! And to think I have lived in terror of your virtue all these years. I thought you were merely a saint, and I found saints so dreadfully uninteresting. But you are an epicurean! You are a pagan! You gave away a million pounds merely because it bored you!”

“I found it a great deal more tedious than a bad play,” said Arthur, returning to his orchids. “And a great deal more expensive.”

Lady Pomeroy came forward and stood beside him, resting her hand lightly upon his shoulder. The morning sunlight, filtering through the stained glass of the long windows, threw a cloak of purple and gold across the floor. She looked down at his placid, handsome face, and a sudden, overwhelming tenderness swept over her. She had always thought him tiresome because she believed him good. Now that she knew he was merely selfish, he seemed to her the most fascinating man in Europe.

“Then you will not defend yourself?” she asked, a note of triumph in her voice. “You will let them go on thinking you a hypocrite, when all the while you are merely an artist in apathy?”

“One should never explain,” he answered, arranging a spray of green foliage. “Explanation is the suicide of mystery. Let them print their little paragraphs. The public will believe anything, provided it is sufficiently dull. If I were to tell them the truth, they would say I was lying to cover up my virtues. The only thing that society cannot forgive is the crime of being original. I prefer to be conventional, even in my sins.”

Lady Pomeroy sank gracefully into a chair opposite him. She took up a fan of dyed ostrich feathers and waved it slowly to and fro, her eyes fixed on her husband with a new and delightful curiosity. 

“You are quite right, Arthur,” she said softly. “It is infinitely better to be misunderstood. To be understood is the privilege of the middle classes. But I must say, the newspaper is terribly wrong about one thing.”

“And what is that, my dear?”

“They said you bought your reputation. It is quite untrue. You did not buy it. You merely threw your money away, and the reputation came tumbling after, like a vulgar beggar demanding alms. It is a terribly heavy thing to carry, Arthur. I suppose I must help you bear the burden of being called a saint, when we both know you are merely a very clever man.”

“You are always charming, Agatha,” he replied, stepping back to admire the arrangement of his flowers. 

“And you are absolutely impossible,” she answered, smiling at him across the scent of the orchids. “But I forgive you. After all, one must forgive one’s husband something. It is the only thing that makes marriage at all endurable.”
