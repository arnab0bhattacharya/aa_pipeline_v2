It is a common enough error, and one into which the most careful reasoners may fall, to imagine that the great choices of our lives arrive with a clear accompaniment of fanfare, like the turning-point in a stage-play where the music swells and the audience knows at once that the crisis is upon us. For the most part, the momentous decisions of a man’s destiny are prepared by a long and quiet accumulation of lesser ones, and the final act of will is less a leap than a settling of the scales, which have been trembling for a long time. Such was the case with Mr. Arthur Penrose, a young clergyman of earnest disposition and considerable learning, whose mind had become the very theatre of such a contest.

His was a nature of that ardent and somewhat fragile order which, having been nurtured on the great moral philosophies and the stirring narratives of social reform, feels the present state of the world not merely as a fact to be endured, but as a text to be corrected. He had been educated at a northern college, where the very air seemed to smell of coal-smoke and the honest sweat of labour, and where the lectures on theology were delivered with a fervour that suggested the kingdom of God might be established before the next market-day. In this atmosphere, Arthur had conceived his vocation not as the gentle stewardship of comfortable souls, but as a kind of spiritual surgery, to cut away the cancers of ignorance and want. He had pictured his future parish as a place of grim necessity and grand purpose, where his sermons would be not lullabies but clarion calls, and where the faces of his congregation would bear the noble stamp of suffering and endurance.

It was at this juncture that the two offers arrived, and in their stark opposition presented him with a choice that was, in essence, a choice between two versions of himself.

The first came from Sir Greville Thorne, a baronet of ancient lineage and modern wealth, who was a power in London society and, more to the point, the patron of the living of St. Jude’s, Belgravia. Sir Greville was not, in his own estimation, an ungodly man. He attended church with punctilious regularity, he subscribed to the proper charities, and he believed, with the serene confidence of his class, that the existing order of things was a direct and benevolent dispensation of Providence. He had met Mr. Penrose at a dinner party, and had been impressed by the young man’s fine features, his classical quotations, and the earnest light in his eyes. Here, Sir Greville thought, was a man who could elevate the tone of St. Jude’s, which had grown a little slack under its last incumbent. He required a preacher who could lend an intellectual lustre to the service without unsettling the digestion of his flock; a man who could speak of the higher life while affirming the propriety of this one.

His offer, delivered with a bland and magnificent assurance, was a model of patronage. The living was worth a handsome sum, the rectory a handsome house, and the society of London was, he observed, a fine finishing-school for a man of parts. “What we need at St. Jude’s, Penrose,” he said, tapping his snuff-box with a plump finger, “is a little polish. A little… illumination. We do not want the congregation troubled with the grubbier aspects of existence. Charity, yes, of course—a subscription list for the deserving poor. But the pulpit should be a source of consolation, of pleasant reflection. A man comes to St. Jude’s on a Sunday morning for spiritual refreshment, not to be told that the foundations of his own house are built upon sand. You take my meaning?”

Arthur had taken his meaning perfectly. He had felt a cold weight settle in his stomach as he listened to this vision of a religion stripped of its thorns, its prophet reduced to a purveyor of agreeable platitudes. The very phrase ‘spiritual refreshment’ seemed to him a profanation, a turning of the wine of the gospel into a soothing tonic for the dyspeptic rich.

The second offer was contained in a single sheet of coarse paper, covered in the laboured hand of a certain Mr. Josiah Clegg, the head of the deacons at the Bethel Chapel in Sedgefield, a mining village in the North Riding. Mr. Clegg’s letter was a masterpiece of its kind, utterly devoid of ornament and full of a blunt, almost painful sincerity. He wrote of a parish of some three hundred souls, nearly all of them connected to the pits; of a chapel that was damp in winter and suffocating in summer; of a stipend that was, he admitted frankly, “nowt to mak a man rich, but enough to keep body and soul together if he’s careful.” He wrote of the ignorance that was as thick as the coal-dust, and of the rough, unlettered goodness that could be found in the men who spent their lives in the dark. “We want a man as can speak plain,” Mr. Clegg had written, “and as isn’t feart to come into a cottage where the floor is none too clean. We want a man as can find the Gospel in a pint pot and a miner’s lung. We cannot offer you London, sir, nor the society of your betters. But we can offer you work that needs doing.”

As Arthur sat in his London lodgings, the two documents lay before him on the table, representing the two poles of his existence. The prospect of St. Jude’s was a temptation, not of gross avarice, but of a subtler kind. It was the temptation of the intellect, of the aesthetic sense. He could not deny the appeal of the fine library he would have, the hours for study, the company of educated men. He could write the great work on Christian ethics he had long contemplated, a work that would be read and discussed in the drawing-rooms of the nation. Was it not possible, he argued with himself, to do more good from such a position of influence? Could he not, from his elegant pulpit in Belgravia, preach a sermon that would move a wealthy man to a great act of philanthropy, thereby relieving more suffering than he could ever hope to touch in a lifetime in Sedgefield? It was a seductive line of reasoning, and one that his own vanity eagerly supported.

But then he would look at Mr. Clegg’s letter, and the coarse paper seemed to him a more authentic text than any vellum-bound treatise. The vision of the damp chapel and the miners’ cottages rose before him. He saw the faces, not of the polished patrons of St. Jude’s, but of men with coal-dust ground into the lines around their eyes, of women with hands made rough by labour, of children with a precocious gravity born of hardship. This was the reality he had been trained to confront. This was the Christianity of the Gospels, a thing of sweat and sorrow and redemption, not of comfortable pews and consoling words. To go to Sedgefield would be to honour his deepest convictions, to become the man he had always intended to be. But it would also be to bury himself alive. He would be a voice crying in a wilderness of coal, unheard and unknown. The great work on ethics would never be written; his life would be consumed by the petty struggles of a single, poor parish.

The choice, then, was between a broad, well-lit path of influence and a narrow, arduous track of duty. It was a choice, he saw with a painful clarity, between the desire to be a good man and the desire to be a great one. For a day and a night he was paralysed, the two possibilities warring within him. He thought of the praise he might win in London, and felt a flush of pleasure. Then he thought of the quiet satisfaction of a soul saved in Sedgefield, and felt a deeper, calmer warmth. It was in the small hours of the morning, when the city was still and the first grey light was beginning to touch the chimney-pots, that the resolution came to him. It was not a dramatic moment of revelation, but a quiet settling, as if the dust of his conflicting thoughts had finally fallen. He saw that Sir Greville’s offer, for all its worldly advantages, was a gilded cage, designed to tame the very spirit that gave his vocation its meaning. To accept it would be to sell his soul for a comfortable living, and to preach a gospel of his own invention. He picked up Sir Greville’s letter, its elegant script a symbol of all he was about to refuse, and laid it carefully aside. He then took up Mr. Clegg’s letter, and read it again. The plain words, the honest admission of hardship, the simple call to work—these things felt solid and real. He was not choosing poverty for its own sake, but choosing the only path that would allow him to remain whole. He would go north, to the coal and the damp and the rough, unlettered souls. It would be a harder life, and a smaller one, but it would be his own. And in that conviction, he felt a peace that no amount of London polish could ever have afforded him.
