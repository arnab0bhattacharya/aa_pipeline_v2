The tempest which had raged with such unmitigated fury through the watches of the night, began, as the faint and pallid light of a Sicilian dawn crept over the waters, to abate its savage roar; yet the agitation of the deep remained terrific. The waves, swelling into mountains of dark and yeasty green, dashed themselves with a sullen and awful majesty against the rugged cliffs of Capo di Morte, as if the imprisoned spirits of the abyss were lamenting the violence they had endured. Upon that desolate and rock-bound shore, where the cormorant alone built her solitary nest, I, Alessandro, then but a humble fisherman of the neighbouring village of San Lucido, stood gazing upon the tumult, my heart filled with that strange and solemn melancholy which the sight of nature in her wrath never fails to awaken in a soul capable of deep feeling. 

Suddenly, as I strained my eyes through the driving spray, I perceived a shattered bark, bereft of its mast, tossed like a feather upon the crests of the overwhelming billows, and driving swiftly towards the fatal reef. I called together the few hardy youths of the village; we launched our stoutest boat, and at the imminent peril of our own lives—for the sea roared around us as a ravenous beast deprived of its prey—we drew from the closing jaws of death a single survivor. He was a youth of singular beauty, though now pale as the sea-foam, and wrapped in the insensibility of approaching dissolution. By indefatigable exertions, we recalled the fleeting spark; and when he opened his eyes, he disclosed to me, in the feeble accents of one yet hovering between two worlds, that he was Donato, the only son of the Duke of Montalto. 

The Duke, a nobleman of immense wealth and overbearing pride, no sooner learned that his heir had been preserved from the fury of the elements by the valour of a peasant, than he hastened to San Lucido. He came surrounded by his retinue, his countenance bearing the heavy stamp of a grief too recently converted into gratitude. He poured forth his acknowledgments with an eloquence that partook of the passionate warmth of our clime; and he bore me away to his palace, a splendid and solitary pile reared upon a promontory that looked out upon the very waters which had well-nigh swallowed his child. "You have given me my son," he exclaimed, his voice trembling with the weight of emotion; "you shall be to me a second son; my house, my fortunes, my favour, shall be your heritage." 

I was removed from my humble dwelling, from the companions of my youth, and the simple, sunburnt toils of my early life, to be installed within those proud and antique halls. The suddenness of my elevation was as a dream; yet I endeavoured to discharge the duties of my new station with a modesty and zeal that might commend me to my benefactor. Donato and I became inseparable. His nature was gentle, contemplative, and deeply sensitive to the beauties of the sublime scenery by which we were surrounded; and in him I found a friend, a brother, whose affection repaid me a thousandfold for the sacrifice of my former liberty. The Duke, observing our growing attachment, heaped upon me fresh marks of his regard; I was admitted to his councils, and entrusted with affairs of no small moment. 

But there was one who viewed my rising favour with an eye of unmitigated gall. Bernardo, the Duke’s steward, had served the house of Montalto for thirty years. He had grown grey in its service; his heart was intertwined with its interests, and he regarded himself as the sole legitimate depository of its honour and its secrets. To see a peasant, a fisherman of San Lucido, lifted at a bound to a level with himself, and even preferred before him in the Duke’s confidence, awoke within his bosom a fury which it is given to few but the deeply wronged, or the deeply wicked, to feel. It was not merely envy; it was a dark, settled, and remorseless hate, that fed upon his vitals, and resolved to accomplish my ruin. 

He commenced his operations with a subtlety that might have imposed upon a more suspicious mind than that of the Duke. He dropped poisoned whispers into the ear of his master; he clothed his malicious insinuations in the garb of profound concern for the honour of the house. "This youth," he would say, "is of low and turbulent origin; the blood of the vulgar cannot be refined by the mere accident of elevation; he will betray the trust reposed in him, and bring disgrace upon a name that has been spotless for centuries." The Duke, at first, repelled these insinuations with a warmth that did honour to his heart; but the constant dropping of slander, like water upon a stone, at length wore away his firmness. I perceived, with agonizing apprehension, that his glance became colder, that his manner was more reserved; and a thousand little circumstances, trivial in themselves but terrible in their aggregate, convinced me that I was losing my hold upon his affections. 

The crisis of my fate arrived upon a night of tempest, not unlike that which had first thrown me into the arms of the Duke. The elements were abroad in all their majesty; the lightning flashed from cloud to cloud, and the thunder rolled in tremendous peals among the mountains. I was alone in my chamber, a prey to the most distressing reflections. I had been summoned to appear before the Duke on the morrow, to answer to charges which Bernardo had artfully framed, and which, though false, were yet supported by such a tissue of circumstantial evidence that I trembled for the result. I thought of Donato, of his gentle nature, and of the anguish he would endure when he should learn the extent of my disgrace. I thought of my humble cottage by the sea-shore, and of the peaceful days when I followed my nets, undisturbed by the storms of human passion. 

As I gazed from my window upon the raging sea, a figure emerged from the shadows of the garden. It was Bernardo. He looked up, and the lightning revealed a countenance distorted by a demoniacal triumph. In that moment, a terrible conviction seized my soul. He had resolved upon my destruction; and the morrow would behold me driven forth, a ruined and dishonoured man, from the house where I had hoped to spend my days in the enjoyment of peace and honour. 

I rushed from the palace, and wandered, I knew not whither, through the storm. The wind howled around me, and the rain beat upon my head, but I heeded them not. I was possessed by a frenzy that partook of the madness of the elements. O, why, I exclaimed, why was I snatched from the obscurity of my humble station, to be plunged into this abyss of misery? Why was I raised to a height from which I must now fall, never to rise again? I had been happy in my poverty; I had been content with the lot which Heaven had assigned me; and now, I was a wretch, torn by the fangs of a relentless enemy, and abandoned by the very friends who had sworn to protect me. 

Yet, even in the depth of my despair, a ray of hope broke in upon my soul. I thought of Donato; of his truth, his constancy, his unalterable affection. I would not believe that he would desert me. I would appear before the Duke; I would confront my accuser; I would lay bare the iniquity of his designs. And if I failed—if the heavy hand of power should crush me to the earth—I would at least have the consolation of knowing that I had maintained my innocence, and that I had fallen a victim to the malice of a foe, rather than to the weakness of my own heart. With this resolution, I returned to the palace, and awaited, with a calmness which despair itself had given, the dawn of the eventful day.
