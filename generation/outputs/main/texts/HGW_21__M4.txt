I had been at Boma Creek for the better part of six years, and in that time I had come to regard the place with the peculiar affection a man reserves for a wound that has healed badly. The station itself was nothing to boast of—a dozen corrugated huts, a wharf that groaned under the weight of a lighter, and a store of trade goods that smelled perpetually of palm oil and damp. Behind it the forest rose like a green wall, and the river went past in a brown, deliberate way, as though it had all the time in the world and no particular destination.

I was the only medical man within forty miles, which meant that I was also the only man within forty miles who could be blamed for the weather, the mosquitoes, and the general perversity of the human constitution. My practice was chiefly fevers, yaws, and the sort of lacerations that come from working a machete in the dark. I had a microscope of which I was inordinately proud, and a set of surgical instruments that I kept in better order than my own conscience.

It was in the third week of the dry season that Mr. Horace Vane arrived. He came up the creek in a launch that had seen better days, and he stepped onto my wharf with the air of a man who has already decided that the entire continent is a disappointment. He was tall, thin, and dressed in a suit of grey flannel that must have been intolerable. He carried a leather case that clinked when he set it down, and he looked at my station as a naturalist might look at a specimen that had been improperly preserved.

"You are Dr. Merton?" he said.

"I am."

"I am Vane. I have come from the Royal Society. I have been sent to investigate the—" he paused, and consulted a small notebook "—the peculiar immunity to pain reported among the Kpelle people of this district."

I offered him a chair, and he sat down with the stiffness of a man who had not been comfortable since leaving England. I poured him a whiskey, which he declined, and then accepted, and then drank rather quickly.

"You have heard of the phenomenon?" he asked.

"I have seen it," I said. "I have cut for the stone in a man who sang throughout the operation. I have seen a woman walk six miles on a compound fracture of the tibia. They do not feel pain as we do. Or rather, they feel it, but it does not signify."

Vane's eyes lit up. They were pale eyes, and they had a way of fixing on a thing that made it feel like a specimen under a lens. "And you have done nothing about it?"

"What would you have me do?"

"Study it, man! Isolate it! Determine its mechanism!" He set down his glass. "Do you know what this could mean? Anesthesia without drugs. Surgery without chloroform. The entire practice of medicine—"

"I am aware of the implications," I said. "I am also aware that I am a doctor, not a vivisectionist. The Kpelle are my patients. They are not my material."

Vane smiled. It was not a pleasant smile. It was the smile of a man who has heard an objection and has already dismissed it. "My dear doctor," he said, "I have not come here to vivisect anyone. I have come to observe. To measure. To understand. The pursuit of knowledge is not cruelty. It is the highest form of compassion."

I should have listened to my instincts. I should have put him back on his launch and sent him down the creek. But I was bored, and I was curious, and I was, if I am honest, a little flattered that the Royal Society had thought to send someone to my station. So I gave him a hut, and I introduced him to the elders, and I told myself that no harm would come of it.

For the first week, he was as good as his word. He observed. He measured. He took notes in a cramped, precise hand. He sat with the Kpelle and asked them questions through my interpreter, a Sierra Leonean named Coker who had a gift for languages and a profound suspicion of white men with notebooks. Vane asked about their diet, their customs, their gods. He asked whether they had always been immune to pain, or whether it was a recent development. He asked whether the immunity extended to all forms of pain, or only to certain kinds. He asked whether it could be induced in others.

The elders answered him politely, and then they came to me and asked what the white man was really doing.

"He is a scientist," I said. "He wants to understand your gift."

"It is not a gift," said the eldest, a man named Kumba who had seen three generations come and go. "It is a curse. We feel pain. We simply do not show it. It is not permitted. A man who cries out is a man who has lost his honor. So we teach our children to be silent. We teach them to smile when they are hurt. We teach them to walk when they should crawl. The pain is still there. It is always there. We have simply learned to carry it."

I relayed this to Vane. He wrote it down. He nodded. He said, "Interesting." And then he went back to his measurements.

It was in the second week that I began to notice the needles. Vane had brought a case of them—fine steel needles, of the sort used for hypodermic injections. He had also brought a supply of a clear liquid that he did not name. He began to visit the villages at night, when I was asleep or occupied with my own patients. He began to pay the young men to submit to his experiments. He began to record their responses with a stopwatch and a notebook.

I confronted him on the tenth day. I had found a boy—a lad of perhaps fourteen, named Tembo—who had been paid a shilling to hold his hand in a flame for as long as he could bear it. He had held it for nearly a minute. His palm was a ruin of blisters.

"What in God's name are you doing?" I demanded.

Vane looked up from his notebook with an expression of mild surprise. "I am establishing a baseline," he said. "The boy feels pain. He simply does not react to it. I am attempting to determine the threshold at which the reaction becomes involuntary."

"You are torturing children."

"I am paying them for their time. They are free to refuse."

"They are not free to refuse. They are poor, and you are rich, and you have a shilling."

Vane set down his notebook. He looked at me with those pale, patient eyes. "Dr. Merton," he said, "do you know what the life expectancy is in this district? Do you know how many children die before their fifth year? Do you know how many die of tetanus, of malaria, of simple infected wounds? I am trying to save lives. If I can isolate the mechanism of this immunity, I can develop a treatment that will benefit millions. The suffering of one boy is a small price to pay for that."

"The boy is not yours to price," I said.

He smiled again. "Neither is he yours," he said. "You are a doctor. You are not a god. You do not get to decide what knowledge is permissible and what is not. I have a mandate from the Royal Society. I have a duty to science. And I will not be deterred by the sentimental scruples of a colonial physician who has been in the bush too long."

I should have thrown him out. I should have written to the Society. I should have done a dozen things. Instead, I did nothing. I told myself that he was right, that knowledge was knowledge, that the ends justified the means. I told myself that I was not responsible for what he did. I told myself that I was a doctor, not a policeman.

It was in the third week that Tembo died. The infection had set in, as I had known it would. Vane had not thought to sterilize his needles. He had not thought to clean the wounds. He had been too busy measuring, too busy recording, too busy establishing his baseline. The boy died in my surgery, with his hand wrapped in a bandage that was already turning black, and his mother keening in the doorway.

Vane came to see me that evening. He was, I think, genuinely sorry. He said that it was a tragedy, that it was an unfortunate accident, that it would not happen again. He said that he had learned from his mistake. He said that he would be more careful in the future.

I looked at him for a long moment. I thought of the boy, and the flame, and the shilling. I thought of the elders, and their silence, and their honor. I thought of the pain that they carried, and the pain that he had added to it.

"You will leave tomorrow," I said. "You will leave on the morning tide, and you will not come back. If you do, I will have you arrested. If there is no law here to arrest you, I will shoot you myself. I am not a violent man, Mr. Vane, but I am a doctor, and I have sworn an oath. I have sworn to do no harm. You have made me complicit in harm. I will not be complicit any longer."

Vane looked at me with those pale eyes. He did not argue. He did not smile. He simply nodded, and went to pack his case.

He left the next morning. I watched his launch go down the creek until it was lost in the haze. Then I went back to my surgery, and I washed my instruments, and I sat in the dark for a long time, thinking about the things that men do in the name of knowledge, and the things that they do in the name of compassion, and the vast, unbridgeable distance between the two.
