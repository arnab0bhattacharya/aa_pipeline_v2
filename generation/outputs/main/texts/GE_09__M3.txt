In the county town of Treddleston, where the slow, brown waters of the Stour reflected the gable-ends of malting-houses and the weather-worn sign of the Barley Mow, the political agitation of eighteen thirty-two had penetrated even to the cool, yeasty recesses of Mr Jeremiah Bowcher’s brewery. It was a fact deeply significant of the times, and perhaps not entirely favourable to the stability of domestic government, that a question concerning the representation of the people’s interests should thrust itself into a family whose highest previous exercise of public opinion had been the unchallenged consumption of their own ale at the parish vestry. Jeremiah, a man of substance whose breadth of figure was only matched by the immovable nature of his prejudices, was a staunch supporter of Sir Cuthbert Vane. Sir Cuthbert was a man who, in the intervals of parliamentary attendance, consumed Treddleston ale with a regularity that constituted a bond of political loyalty, and who had no manner of objection to the existing state of things, provided the malt-tax remained tolerable and his tenants kept their places.

But Stephen Bowcher, Jeremiah’s only son, a young man of two-and-twenty, whose mind had been slightly unsettled by reading the London newspapers in the back parlour, had conceived a violent attachment to Mr Arthur Leighton, a reformer who spoke of franchise extensions and the rights of unrepresented tradesmen with an eloquence that made the brewer’s blood run cold.

The quarrel, which had been fermenting for some weeks, came to its head on a Tuesday afternoon in the brewery yard. The great vats stood silent, and the men were at their dinner in the upper room, leaving the yard empty save for the master and his son, and an ancient dray-horse that munched hay in a corner with profound political indifference.

“And I tell you, Stephen,” said Jeremiah, his voice vibrating with the effort of restraining a passion that was all the more formidable because he considered it a righteous defence of the constitution, “I tell you that a man who would give a vote to every ragged-fellow that can hold a pot in the tap-room is no friend to order, and no friend to property, and no friend to the trade that has fed him and his before him. Sir Cuthbert is a gentleman, and he drinks my beer, and I’ll not see him turned out by a rabble that knows nothing but the inside of a public-house.”

“But, father,” said Stephen, who had inherited the elder Bowcher’s stubbornness without acquiring his prudence, and who was further exasperated by a consciousness of his own intellectual superiority, “you speak of the men as if they were cattle. They are your own workers, and they have no voice in choosing the men who make the laws that bind them. Mr Leighton says that the franchise should be extended to all who contribute to the wealth of the country, and I cannot see that a brewer’s men are less worthy than a brewer.”

This was a blow of a kind Jeremiah was not prepared for. The comparison between his men and himself struck him as a sort of sacrilege, not because he was consciously cruel to his workers, but because the distinction between master and man was woven into the very fabric of his moral universe. To question it was like questioning the difference between right and wrong, or between malt and hops.

“You are a fool, Stephen,” said Jeremiah, with a heaviness of tone that had more of sorrow than anger in it, though anger was not absent. “You have read books till your head is turned. You think because you have been to a boarding-school you know better than your father, who has built up this business from a single copper and a mash-tub. But I will tell you this: while the breath is in my body, no son of mine shall electioneer against the man who has been the friend of this house, and this town, for twenty year.”

Stephen’s face flushed, and he shifted his weight from one foot to the other, a movement that was habitual with him when his pride was wounded. He was fond of his father, in the way that a young man is fond of a parent whose authority he has begun to find irksome, and the consciousness of this fondness made his rebellion all the more uncomfortable. He wished to be magnanimous, but he also wished to be right, and the two desires are not always easily reconciled.

“I do not electioneer against Sir Cuthbert,” said Stephen, in a voice that he tried to make calm but that was tremulous with the sense of injury, “I electioneer for Mr Leighton. And if the men who work in this yard were to have a vote, I believe they would vote for the man who promises them a voice in their own government, and not for the man who promises them nothing but a glass of beer on election day.”

“Damn Mr Leighton!” said Jeremiah, with an emphasis that made the dray-horse prick up its ears.

“Nay, nay, maister, there’s no call to swear at a body as isn’t here to answer for hisself,” said a voice from the shadow of the mash-house door, where Jabez Rudd, the head cooper, had been standing unobserved. Jabez was a man of sixty, with a face like a weather-beaten apple, and hands that were cracked and stained with the labour of forty years. He had the slow, deliberate speech of a man who thinks while he talks, and who is accustomed to be listened to, at least in the matter of barrel-hoops, if not of constitutional reform.

“Jabez,” said Jeremiah, turning sharply, “what are you doing there? This is no concern of yours.”

“Nay, maister, I weren’t meaning to make it my concern,” said Jabez, touching his forelock with a gesture that was humble but not servile, for Jabez had a shrewd sense of his own value in the brewery economy. “I were only coming to fetch a hoop-twig as I’d left behind, and I couldn’t help hearing what was said. But if you’ll pardon me, Mr Stephen has a right to his opinion, same as you have to yours, and I reckon there’s many a man in this yard as would be glad of a vote, if so be the likes of us was thought worthy to have one.”

This intervention, though mildly expressed, was singularly unfortunate in its effect upon Jeremiah’s temper. The notion that his own cooper should presume to support his son’s reforming notions was a double affront, coming as it did from a man whose bread depended on the brewery’s prosperity. It seemed to Jeremiah that the very foundations of his authority were being undermined in his own yard, and by the very people whom he had fed and employed, as he believed, with a paternal benevolence that ought to have secured their political silence, if not their gratitude.

“You hold your tongue, Jabez,” said Jeremiah, in a voice that had the ring of authority in it, but also a tremor of something not unlike fear, the fear that a man feels when the world he has known begins to shift beneath his feet. “I’ll have no politics in my yard. What I pay you for is to make barrels, not to make speeches.”

Jabez retreated, with a slow nod and a look that was meant to be conciliatory but that Jeremiah interpreted as sullen. The cooper’s face wore an expression that was common among the Treddleston working-men in those days, an expression of dawning consciousness, as if a light had been carried into a dark corner of the mind, and the furniture there, though familiar, looked different in the new illumination.

When Jabez had gone, Jeremiah turned to Stephen, and his face had in it that mingling of anger and grief which is perhaps the most painful combination of human feeling, because it wounds the heart while it inflames the tongue.

“You see,” he said, “what your reforming notions have done. You have set my own men against me. Jabez Rudd, who has eaten my bread for thirty year, dares to tell me that he would be glad of a vote. And whose example has encouraged him? Yours, Stephen. Yours.”

Stephen felt the weight of this accusation, and it pressed upon him the more heavily because he was not entirely sure that his father was wrong in attributing the effect to his cause. It was true that he had spoken to the men, not in the way of direct political solicitation, for he had some scruples about that, but in the way of general conversation, which is often more persuasive because it is less defensible. He had let fall opinions about the rights of working-men, about the injustice of a system that excluded them from representation, and these opinions had fallen upon minds that were prepared to receive them, not because they were sophisticated, but because they were simple and seemed to correspond with certain dim dissatisfactions that the men had felt without being able to articulate them.

But Stephen was young, and youth is impatient of the charges that age brings against it, especially when the charges contain a measure of truth. He said, with a heat that was more eloquent than his arguments, that he was not ashamed of his opinions, and that if the men desired the vote, it was because the vote was their right, and not because he had put the notion into their heads.

Jeremiah made no reply, but turned on his heel and walked into the counting-house, closing the door behind him with a sound that was not quite a slam but that conveyed a finality more absolute than any words could have done. Stephen remained in the yard, looking after his father with a face in which anger, compunction, and a vague sense of the tragic irony of human affairs were curiously mingled. He was conscious that he had been right in principle and wrong in method, a discovery that comes to most reformers sooner or later, and that is perhaps the chief reason why reform is so slow in coming.

The dray-horse, having finished its hay, breathed a loud sigh, as if it found the political situation of Treddleston altogether beneath its notice, and turned its broad hindquarters toward Stephen with an indifference that was, under the circumstances, not without a certain philosophical grandeur.
