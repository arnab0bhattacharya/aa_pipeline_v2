The letter lay upon the tea-tray, beside the toast which I had made ready, and my brother’s hand had superscribed it in that bold, unhesitating character which he carried into every corner of his life, from the pulpit to the kitchen. I knew the writing of the woman to whom it was addressed, for I had seen it once before—a small, neat, sloping hand, the letters pressed so close together that they seemed to whisper secrets to one another. Miss Ellinor Hartley, of the Grange. The name ran through me like a wire drawn tight.

I did not open it. I am not a woman given to the reading of other people’s letters, though I confess that on that grey October morning I stood a long while with the tray in my hands, looking down at the folded paper as though it were a living thing which might speak if I had the courage to listen. The fire had sunk low in the grate; the rain beat upon the window with a dull, persistent enmity, and the clock upon the mantel ticked out the seconds with that measured indifference which time always shows to the small agonies of mortals. I set the tray down at last and went about my duties. Duties! As though the polishing of a candlestick could silence the question which had begun to knock at my heart.

My brother came in from the vestry at noon, shaking the wet from his coat, and I saw at once, by a certain brightness in his eye and a certain carefulness in his manner, that he had something to say to me. He was a good man, my brother James—a better man than most, I believe, and a duller one than many; but there was no malice in him, and no meanness, and if he had a fault it was that he never could conceive how a thing might look from any eyes but his own. He sat down to his dinner and talked of the parish, of the new schoolroom, of the fever at Wetherby’s farm; and all the while I watched him, and knew that he was circling nearer and nearer to the thing he had to tell, as a bird circles before it settles.

“Margaret,” said he at last, laying down his knife and fork, “I have been thinking—that is, I have had it in my mind to speak to you—I am to be married.”

The words fell into the quiet room like a stone into still water. I felt the ripples go out from them, and I sat very still, and heard my own voice answer, as though it belonged to someone else: “To Miss Hartley.”

He coloured. “You have guessed it. Yes. To Ellinor. We are to be married at Christmas.”

“At Christmas,” I repeated. “That is soon.”

“It is not soon enough for me,” said he, with a smile which I had never seen upon his face before, and which, God forgive me, I could have struck from it. “But there is the house to be put in order, and Ellinor wishes—”

He stopped. I knew why he stopped. Ellinor wished—what? That the sister should be gone before the bride arrived. He had not the courage to say it, and I had not the cowardice to help him.

“You will want the house to yourselves,” said I, and my voice was as hard and dry as the crust of the bread beside me. “It is natural. I am thirty years old, James, and I am not a fool. I have kept your house these seven years, since our mother died, and I have done it, I think, without complaint. But I am not so blind as to suppose that a wife will want her husband’s sister sitting at the head of her table.”

“Margaret!” he cried, and there was trouble in his face, and something that was almost anger. “You do me injustice. Ellinor has nothing but kindness for you. She hopes—she desires—”

“She desires what every woman desires,” I interrupted, “the mastery of her own hearth. And she is right. Do not think I blame her. I blame no one but myself, that I have lived these seven years in a house that was never mine, and have warmed myself at a fire that another woman will soon be sitting by.”

He rose from the table, pushing back his chair with a noise that grated on my nerves, and stood looking down at me with that perplexed frown which he wore when the theology of the parish council was too much for him. “You are unjust,” he said again. “You are bitter. I had not expected this from you.”

“What did you expect?” I asked him. “That I should smile, and wish you joy, and go quietly into some corner to starve? I have no money, James. I have no training. I have kept your house, and in keeping it I have learned nothing that the world will pay me for. What am I to do? Go out as a governess at my age, with no accomplishments but plain cooking and plain sewing? Take a situation as a housekeeper, and be ordered about by some woman younger than myself? Is that what you have planned for me, in your kindness?”

He stood silent, and I saw the flush fade from his face, and a grey look come over it, and I knew that I had hurt him, and I was glad. Yes, I was glad. There is a fierce joy in the infliction of a wound when one is wounded oneself, and I felt it then, and I feel no shame in the confession. He was a good man, but he was a man, and he had never in his life been obliged to consider what it was to be a woman with no home but the one that another woman might at any moment take from her.

“I did not think,” he said slowly, “that you would take it so.”

“No,” said I. “You did not think. You never do think. You are a curate, and you have the cure of souls, and you preach every Sunday about charity and forbearance, and you never once looked at your own sister and asked yourself what would become of her when you married. You have taken my youth, James—seven years of it—and you have taken it without thanks, because it was your due. And now you will take my home, and you will take it with a clear conscience, because you are to be married at Christmas, and Ellinor wishes the house to be put in order.”

The rain beat upon the window, and the fire sank lower, and I saw my brother’s face grow old and troubled, and I saw him stretch out his hand to me across the table, and I saw my own hand lie still and not take it. There was a great silence in the room, and in the silence I heard the clock ticking, ticking, as though it counted out the last hours of my life in that house.

“I am sorry,” he said at last, and his voice was low and unsteady. “I am sorry, Margaret. I did not mean—I did not know—”

“You know now,” said I, and I rose from my chair, and stood before him, and I felt the tears rise in my eyes, and I forced them back with a violence that hurt me. “You know now. And what will you do?”

He was silent. He had no answer. He stood there in his black coat, with his hands clasped behind him, and his eyes fixed upon the fire, and I saw that he was helpless, and that he would do nothing—that he would marry Ellinor at Christmas, and that I should be expected to smile and wish him joy, and to find some corner for myself as best I might. The world was made for men, I thought; and women must shift as they can.

“I will write to my cousin at Ripon,” said I. “She is a widow, and she has a small house, and she may have room for me. If not, I will go to the school at Bradford, and see if they will take me as a teacher of the youngest children. I have kept your house, James, and I have kept it well, and I will not be a burden upon you or upon Ellinor. But I will not pretend to be glad. I will not pretend that I am not angry, and hurt, and afraid. I am all three. And I think—I think you might have spoken to me sooner.”

He turned to me then, and there was something in his face that I had never seen there before—a kind of shame, and a kind of sorrow, and a kind of fear. “Margaret,” he said, “what shall I do?”

“Do?” said I. “Do your duty. Marry Ellinor, and be a good husband to her, and a good parson to your people. And let me go. There is nothing else to be done. But do not ask me to be glad, James. Do not ask me to be glad.”

I went out of the room, and up the narrow stairs to my own chamber, and I shut the door, and I stood at the window, and looked out at the rain falling upon the graveyard, and at the grey stones leaning this way and that among the wet grass. And I thought of all the years I had spent in that house, and of the mornings I had risen early to light the fires, and of the evenings I had sat alone while my brother was about his parish work, and of the little hopes I had cherished, and the little plans I had made, and how they were all come to nothing. And I thought of the words I had spoken, and of the anger in my heart, and I knew that I had been bitter, and that I had been unjust, and that I had spoken the truth.

The rain fell, and the wind rose, and the night came on, and I sat in the dark and made my plans. There was no help for it. I must go. I must find some place for myself in the world, and I must do it alone, and I must do it without money, and without training, and without encouragement. And I thought of the words of the Scripture: “The foxes have holes, and the birds of the air have nests; but the Son of man hath not where to lay his head.” And I thought that there were many women in the world who had less than the foxes and the birds, and who yet found strength to go on from day to day, and to keep their sorrow to themselves, and to smile when their hearts were breaking. And I resolved that I would be one of them.

At Christmas my brother was married, and I stood beside him at the altar, and wished him joy with a steady voice, and kissed Ellinor upon the cheek, and she was kind to me, and I believe she meant it. And in the new year I went to Ripon, to my cousin’s house, and I found a place there, and I kept it for many years. And I never forgot the parsonage, nor the grey stones in the graveyard, nor the rain upon the window, nor the words that I had spoken to my brother in the bitterness of my heart. And I never forgave him—not wholly—though I prayed for him every Sunday, and I believe that God forgave us both.
