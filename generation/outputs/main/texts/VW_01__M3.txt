The light fell in long slabs across the Close, cutting the worn flagstones into a chequerboard of amber and slate, and there, where the shadow of the cathedral spire lay heaviest, the ravens were pulling at a shred of wet moss. It was Tuesday; it was, Eleanor thought, irretrievably Tuesday; and the letter from the Archdeacon lay unanswered beneath a paper-knife shaped like a miniature sword, its blade tarnished with age. She looked at it; she looked at the thick yellow envelope, and at the fine, exhausted hand which had traced her father’s name; and it seemed to her that all the dust of the cathedral, all the murmured Latin of centuries, had settled upon that one sheet of paper, weighting it down, so that she must lean, must press her whole life against it, to keep it from sinking through the polished wood of the writing-table and being lost for ever in the dark.

Her father was asleep in the next room. She could hear his breathing, shallow and regular as the ticking of the tall clock in the hall, and it seemed to her that the whole house hung suspended upon that sound, as if the breath of the Bishop of St. Cuthbert’s were the single thread from which the chandelier, the faded Axminster, the portraits of dead bishops, and she herself, Eleanor, were all dangling, suspended in a crystal globe of silence which at any moment might crack and let in the roaring tide of the world. She was twenty-six; she was unmarried; she kept his house; she answered his correspondence; she poured his tea with a hand that did not tremble; and yet here, in this silence, in this light that came through the leaded panes in long coloured slabs, something was rising in her, something stubborn and dark, like the green shoots that pushed through the gravel of the Close each spring and were always, always trodden down.

Mr. Heywood was coming at four. He came every Tuesday and Thursday now, to read to her father, because the Bishop’s eyes had failed by degrees, as a lamp fails, the oil sinking so slowly that one does not remark it until the wick gutters and the light narrows to a blue point and goes out. So the Bishop’s sight had gone; and Mr. Heywood—young, raw, with that extraordinary reddish hair that seemed to have been cut by a blunt knife—sat in the leather chair by the bookcase and read the Fathers in a voice that was too loud, and stumbled over the Greek, and yet had in it, sometimes, when he forgot himself, a quality that made Eleanor, standing at the door with the tea-tray, pause and hold her breath, as one pauses on a staircase when music has started in a room below.

He had contradicted her. That was the thing. He had contradicted her, not once but twice, and the second time he had looked at her with those grey eyes—was it defiance, or was it the raw honesty of a young man who has not yet learned that the daughters of bishops are not contradicted?—and had said, “But surely, Miss Aldridge, that is to confuse endurance with submission.” And she had felt the colour rise in her face; not the faint polite flush that came when old Archdeacon Phipps told his endless stories at dinner, but a hot, dark wave that seemed to rise from the very soles of her feet, and she had answered—how had she answered? She could not now remember the words, only the sensation, as if a door had been flung open inside her and a wind had rushed through rooms that had been shut for years, lifting the covers from the furniture, stirring the curtains, so that everything in her that had been draped and darkened and protected from the light was suddenly exposed and trembling.

It was not that he was clever; or not clever in the way that Father was clever, with the massive, worn cleverness of a man who has read everything and found most of it wanting. Mr. Heywood’s cleverness was ragged at the edges; it had the quality of something newly made, still warm from the making; and when he argued, his hands moved as if he were shaping the air, pressing and moulding invisible substance, and she found herself watching the hands and forgetting the argument, and then remembering it with a start, and flushing again, and turning away to the window, where the sparrows were squabbling over a crust on the leads.

Now the clock struck three-quarters past three, and the sound boomed and faded, leaving the silence deeper than before. She moved to the window. The Close lay in its usual Wednesday aspect—no, Tuesday; it was Tuesday; and there was Mrs. Fenwick, the Canon’s wife, in her navy coat, walking her ridiculous dog along the path by the yews, and nodding, as she always nodded, to unseen acquaintances, so that one might think her perpetually in conversation with the dead bishops whose monuments lined the cloister. Eleanor watched her; she watched the neat navy figure receding; and she thought how extraordinary it was that Mrs. Fenwick should be walking there, alive, in the afternoon light, wearing that coat, holding that lead, when inside this house, inside this skin, there was this commotion, this upheaval, as if the ground beneath the Close had shifted and no one had noticed, no one would ever notice, because the daughters of bishops do not have earthquakes; they pour tea; they answer letters; they smooth the creases from the altar cloth.

And yet. And yet there had been that moment—when was it? Thursday, last Thursday, after he had finished reading the passage from Gregory and her father had fallen into his doze, and they had been left alone in the library with the fire dying and the smell of old leather and coal smoke heavy in the air—and he had said, closing the book with a careful, almost reverent hand, “Do you never think, Miss Aldridge, that a life spent in service to another’s vision is—” and he had stopped, and she had waited, and the silence between them had been of a quality entirely different from the Bishop’s silence, or the Close’s silence, or the cathedral’s silence; it had been a silence that was full, not empty; a silence in which something was being offered, or risked, and she had not known whether to take it or to let it fall, and so she had done what she always did; she had said, “More tea, Mr. Heywood?” and the moment had closed, like a hand closing over a moth, and the wings had crumpled, and the dust had settled, and she had poured the tea and he had taken it and their fingers had not touched, because the cups were large and her hand was steady.

But the argument; the argument was the thing. For he had said—what had he said?—that endurance was not submission; and she had felt, with a clarity that startled her, that he was right, and that she had been wrong, and that being wrong was not a catastrophe but a door opening onto a corridor she had never explored, lit by a strange, uneven light, and at the end of it there was—what? She did not know. She could not see. There was only the sense of space, of air, of a window being opened in a room that had been shut since she was eighteen, since her mother died, since she had come down from the schoolroom and taken the keys and the ledger and the responsibility, and had become, without anyone remarking it, the shadow of the Bishop, the extension of his will, the hand that wrote his letters and smoothed his lawn and kept the house in its crystal globe of silence, uncracked, unbreathed upon.

He was coming at four. She could hear his step now—no, it was the postman; it was always the postman, heavy-footed on the gravel, bringing circulars and parish magazines and the endless, endless correspondence of a bishop’s household. But soon he would come; and she would be at the door; and she would say, “My father is resting, Mr. Heywood”; and they would stand for a moment in the hall with the light falling in long slabs through the leaded glass, and she would say—what would she say? She did not know. She felt that something was about to be said, something that mattered, something that would crack the globe and let in the air; and she was afraid, and she was ready; and the light shifted on the Close, and the ravens cried, and the clock ticked, and her father breathed, and Eleanor stood at the window with her hand on the curtain, waiting.
