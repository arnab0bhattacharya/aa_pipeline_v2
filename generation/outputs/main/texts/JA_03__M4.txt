The proposal, when it came, was conveyed in a manner so characteristic of Lady Fenwick that Miss Arabella Thorpe could not but admire the economy of it. Her ladyship had driven over from the Hall expressly, she said, to consult the judgment of the one young person of her acquaintance whose understanding she could trust without reservation; and having delivered herself of this preamble with the complacency of a woman who has never yet found her confidence misplaced, she proceeded to lay before her god-daughter the singular difficulty in which she stood.

The difficulty concerned Miss Lucy Fenwick, her ladyship's niece, a young woman of amiable disposition and tolerable fortune, who had reached the age of three-and-twenty without having received a single offer that her family could consider serious. "She is not," Lady Fenwick observed, with the candour of one speaking of a favourite, "what the world calls a beauty, nor yet what the world calls a wit; but she has a good heart, Arabella, and a very pretty competence, and I am resolved she shall not throw herself away upon the first man who asks her merely because nobody has taken the trouble to show her what better things there are to be had."

Miss Thorpe, who had spent the better part of every summer of her life at Fenwick Hall, and who considered herself, with some justice, to possess a clearer view of the neighbourhood's eligible gentlemen than any person in it, received this commission with the gravity it deserved. She was not insensible to the compliment implied; indeed, she had long been of the opinion that Lady Fenwick's judgment, though excellent in matters of upholstery and poultry, wanted that final polish which only a younger and more disinterested eye could supply. To be asked, therefore, to direct the affections of her cousin was to be asked to do what she had been doing silently for years.

There were, as her ladyship had said, three gentlemen worthy of consideration. The first was Mr. Henry Ashcombe, a clergyman of nine-and-twenty, whose living was comfortable and whose sermons were universally allowed to be the shortest in the county. He was a man of sober manners and regular habits, and though he had never been heard to say anything remarkable, he had never been heard to say anything foolish either; a distinction which Miss Thorpe, who had known many clever men, had learned to value. The second was Captain Edward Raleigh, a naval officer lately returned from the Mediterranean, whose conversation was lively to the point of restlessness, and whose prospects, though vague, were brilliant enough to make him the subject of much hopeful speculation among the mothers of Kent. The third was Mr. Thomas Pemberton, a gentleman of independent means, who had inherited a handsome estate from an uncle and had done nothing with it since but ride about it, and who was generally understood to be of a retiring and somewhat melancholy turn.

Miss Thorpe applied herself to the task with the diligence of a woman who has found at last a proper use for her talents. She visited each gentleman in turn—not, she was careful to assure herself, with any design that could be called forward, but with that easy, natural intercourse which the neighbourhood permitted and which her position as Lady Fenwick's god-daughter made perfectly unexceptionable. She observed Mr. Ashcombe at his own table, and marked how he pressed his guests to a second helping without ever pressing them to a second opinion. She walked with Captain Raleigh along the sea-wall at Deal, and noted that he spoke of his ship as a man speaks of a mistress, and of his prospects as a man speaks of the weather. She sat with Mr. Pemberton in his library, which contained more books than he had read and more dust than either, and heard him say, with a sigh, that the autumn had been very fine, and that he supposed the winter would be very wet.

From these observations she drew her conclusions with a confidence that was almost mathematical. Captain Raleigh, she decided, was charming, but charming men are seldom constant, and a woman of Lucy's modest expectations would be worn to nothing in the attempt to keep pace with him. Mr. Pemberton was steady, but his steadiness was of a kind that would soon become mere inertia; a woman married to him would spend her life waiting for something to happen, and would wait in vain. Mr. Ashcombe alone united the qualities that a prudent woman ought to desire: he was sensible without being dull, settled without being stagnant, and his regard, once given, might be relied upon to endure. She pictured Lucy installed in the rectory at Ashcombe Parva, presiding over parish dinners and christenings with a quiet competence, and found the picture entirely satisfactory.

She lost no time in laying her recommendation before Lady Fenwick, who received it with the solemnity of a queen accepting a treaty. "Mr. Ashcombe!" her ladyship repeated. "Well, Arabella, I confess I had not thought of him. But you are very right; you are very right indeed. He is not a man to set the Thames on fire, but he is exactly the sort of man to keep a house warm. I shall speak to Lucy directly."

It was arranged that Miss Thorpe should herself be the bearer of the good news, for Lady Fenwick, who dreaded anything that resembled a scene, preferred that the first mention of the matter should come from a younger and less authoritative tongue. And so it was that on a Tuesday afternoon in the middle of October, Miss Thorpe found herself seated in the little parlour at Fenwick Hall, with her cousin opposite her, and the words of her carefully prepared address ready upon her lips.

"Lucy," she began, "I have been thinking a great deal lately about your happiness, and I believe I have found the very thing to secure it. You must not laugh at me; I am quite serious. I have observed the neighbourhood closely—more closely, I dare say, than you have yourself—and I am persuaded that there is one gentleman among us whose character and situation would suit you to perfection. You will have guessed him, perhaps, already. Mr. Ashcombe."

Lucy Fenwick looked up from her work with an expression that Miss Thorpe could not immediately interpret. "Mr. Ashcombe?" she said.

"Mr. Ashcombe," Miss Thorpe repeated, with the satisfaction of a woman who has chosen well and knows it. "He is not, I grant you, the most dazzling of men, but he is the most dependable, and dependability, my dear Lucy, is what a woman of your disposition requires. You would be mistress of a comfortable house, the wife of a respected clergyman, and—"

"Arabella," said Lucy, laying down her work, "I am sure you mean it kindly, but I must stop you. I am engaged to be married."

Miss Thorpe sat back in her chair as though she had been struck. "Engaged? To whom?"

"To Mr. Pemberton."

"Mr. Pemberton!" The name escaped her before she could prevent it. "But Mr. Pemberton is—" She checked herself, but not before the damage was done.

"Mr. Pemberton is what?" said Lucy, with a calmness that Miss Thorpe found almost offensive.

Miss Thorpe rallied. "I only mean that I had not supposed—that is to say, I had not observed—"

"No," said Lucy, "I do not suppose you had. Nobody had. Mr. Pemberton is not a man who shows his feelings in public, and I am not a woman who invites observation. We have known each other for three years, Arabella. He has been in the habit of walking with me in the shrubbery after church, and of sending me books I had mentioned in passing, and of saying nothing at all in a manner that I found, from the first, perfectly intelligible. He spoke to my uncle last week."

Miss Thorpe was silent for a moment. She was aware of a sensation she did not altogether like, and which she was obliged to recognise, after a struggle, as the mortification of having been wrong. "I hope you will be very happy," she said at last, and meant it, though the words cost her more than she would have wished.

"I hope so too," said Lucy, and took up her work again with the air of a woman who has said all that is necessary. "And Arabella—I am very grateful for your trouble. But you must not think me ungrateful if I say that I am glad I did not wait to be chosen for me."

Miss Thorpe walked home that evening along the lanes she knew so well, and found them, for the first time in her recollection, rather less instructive than she had supposed. She had chosen with care, presented with confidence, and been answered by a fact. It was a lesson she did not care to examine too closely; but she was a woman of sense, and she knew, even as she acknowledged it, that she would not forget it.
