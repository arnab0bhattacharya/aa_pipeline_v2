The bell above the door of Fenwick’s Pawnbrokers gave a thin, tinny jingle, a sound that was more a complaint than a greeting. Silas Croft looked up from his ledger, his spectacles perched on the end of his nose. The rain that had been threatening all afternoon had finally broken, and the man who stepped inside brought the smell of the wet, grimy street with him. He was a man Croft knew, not by name, but by the quiet authority he carried into the cramped, dusty shop. A man from Scotland Yard.

“Mr. Croft,” the investigator said, his voice a low rumble that seemed to absorb the shop’s silence.

“Inspector Blackwood.” Croft closed the ledger with a soft thud. He did not offer a seat; the only other chair in the room was piled high with a bundle of moth-eaten furs. “A poor day for trade, and a worse one for weather. What brings you to my door?”

Blackwood placed a small, cloth-wrapped object on the counter between them. His fingers were long and steady. “This,” he said. He unwrapped it, revealing a silver hunter-case watch. It was a fine thing, or had been. The silver was tarnished with neglect, and the crystal over the face was spiderwebbed with a crack. But it was the back that mattered. Blackwood flipped it over with his thumb. An engraving, deep and precise, was etched into the metal: a crest of a coiled serpent swallowing its own tail, beneath a crown of oak leaves.

Croft’s breath caught in his throat. He had seen that crest before. Everyone in London with an interest in the society pages had. “The Ainsworths,” he whispered.

“Indeed,” Blackwood said. “The personal effect of Lord Ainsworth’s only son and heir. The one who took a spill from a cliff path in Cornwall two years ago. The one they never found.”

A chill, unrelated to the damp of the shop, crept up Croft’s spine. “It came in yesterday,” he admitted, his voice lower now. “A boy. A street urchin, no older than twelve, though it’s hard to tell with their sort. He wanted ten shillings for it. I gave him two, and he snatched the coins and was out the door before I could even ask his name.”

“Tell me about the boy,” Blackwood said, his grey eyes fixed on the watch as if it were a corpse on a slab.

“Ragged as a scarecrow,” Croft began, picturing the child. “Thinner than a whip. But his eyes… they weren’t dull. He was frightened, but not of me. He kept glancing out the window, as if expecting someone to follow him. He was desperate for the money, but he wouldn’t part with this scrap of paper.”

Croft reached beneath the counter and produced a small, folded piece of cheap, pulpy paper. He slid it across to the inspector. Blackwood unfolded it carefully. On it was a single address, written in a spidery, elegant hand: *The Old Hound, 14 Penny Lane, Southwark*.

“The boy insisted a gentleman gave it to him,” Croft explained. “Said he was paid a shilling to carry the watch to this address and leave it with the landlord. But when he got there, the place was boarded up. A dead end. Frightened and with nowhere else to go, he came here to try and turn the watch into coin. He claimed he couldn’t read the note himself, just that the gentleman had shown it to him and told him where to go.”

“A gentleman,” Blackwood mused, turning the watch over in his palm. “Did he describe him?”

“Tall, he said. Well-dressed in a dark coat and hat. Had a cane with a silver handle, something like a snake’s head. The boy said the man’s face was in shadow, but his voice was soft, like a gentleman’s. He found the boy in an alley off Cable Street, offered him the work. Simple enough. A message to be delivered, the watch as proof of good faith. The boy never questioned it. Hunger doesn’t encourage curiosity.”

Blackwood was silent for a long moment, his gaze distant. The only sounds were the drumming of rain against the windowpanes and the faint hiss of the gas lamp above the counter. “The Ainsworth heir was named Julian,” he said finally. “A bright boy, by all accounts, but reckless. He was fond of the sea, of sailing. His body was never recovered. The coroner ruled it a tragic accident. The family mourned and moved on. Or so they tried to.”

“And now this watch turns up,” Croft finished, a knot of unease tightening in his stomach. “After two years.”

“Precisely.” Blackwood wrapped the watch back in its cloth. “It suggests the accident was not the end of the story. It suggests someone has been holding onto this. And now, for reasons of their own, they wish to be rid of it. Or, perhaps, to send a message.”

“A message to whom?”

“That,” Blackwood said, “is what I intend to find out. This boy. You said he was desperate. Where do boys like that go when they have a pocketful of coin?”

Croft sighed, running a hand over his bald head. “The Ragged School, if they’re lucky and still have some hope. The soup kitchen on Dorset Street, if they have the sense to eat before they drink. Or the Seven Dials, if they’ve a mind to lose it all at dice and cards. But he looked like a runner, that one. Quick and light on his feet. He’ll be back in the alleys, keeping his head down. He’ll have spent the two shillings by now and be looking for his next meal.”

“Find him,” Blackwood commanded, his tone leaving no room for argument. “Not for the watch. I want to know everything he saw. The cut of the gentleman’s coat, the way he held his cane, the tone of his voice. The smallest detail could be the thread that unravels this whole affair. He is not a thief. He is a witness.”

“And what if this ‘gentleman’ is looking for him, too?” Croft asked, the thought sending another shiver through him. “If he’s a witness to something, he’s also a loose end.”

Blackwood’s face was grim. “Then we must find him first. Start with the places you know. Talk to the other pawnbrokers, the costermongers, the beggars on the corner. Someone will have seen him. A boy like that, with two whole shillings in his pocket, is a king for a day. He will have been noticed.”

He placed a few coins on the counter, not as payment, but as a practical necessity. “I’ll be at the station. Send word the moment you have him.”

With that, Inspector Blackwood turned and was gone, the bell jingling his departure. Silas Croft stood alone in the sudden silence of his shop. He looked at the space on the counter where the watch had been, then at the scrap of paper with its dead address. The air in the room felt thick, heavy with the ghosts of things unsaid and deeds long buried. The rain continued its steady assault on the city, washing the grime from the cobblestones, but Croft knew it could not wash away the secrets that festered in the dark corners of Whitechapel. He picked up his heavy coat from the hook by the door. He had a boy to find.
