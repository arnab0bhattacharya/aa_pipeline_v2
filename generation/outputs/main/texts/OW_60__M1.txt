Circé was, by universal admission, the most beautiful creature that had ever graced the placid, mirror-like waters of the Royal Lake of Aurelia. Her neck was a question mark of purest alabaster, her plumage the colour of a pearl worn by a duchess, and her beak was a sliver of polished gold. She moved with a slow, disdainful grace, as if the water itself were a liquid floor laid down for her benefit, and the other birds were but a poorly dressed audience permitted to watch her performance. Her tragedy, as is often the case with tragedies, was that she was entirely aware of her own perfection.

It was the human element, of course, that brought the matter to a head. Every afternoon, a parade of governesses and indulgent parents would appear at the lake’s edge, their pockets heavy with the day’s communion. And there, bobbing and quacking with a vulgarity that was almost a form of art, were the ducks. They were a tribe of common, brown-feathered creatures, all noise and appetite, their heads perpetually submerged in the hunt for crumbs. Circé regarded them not with anger, but with a profound and sorrowful pity. To share their bread was to acknowledge a commonality of existence she found philosophically repugnant.

‘My dear Circé,’ remarked a portly goose named Barnaby, who had positioned himself near her in hopes of catching some reflected glory, ‘the children seem to be offering a particularly fine crust today. A brioche, I believe.’

Circé did not turn her head. ‘Barnaby,’ she said, her voice a low, melodic hum, ‘one does not survive on bread. One survives on style. These… ducks… are not eating. They are merely filling a void, a void created by their own lack of aesthetic purpose.’

‘They are filling their stomachs,’ Barnaby noted practically.

‘A far less noble enterprise,’ Circé sighed, trailing a wingtip through the water, creating a perfect, fleeting V-shape. She would glide past the hordes of quacking supplicants, a silent, self-contained ark of beauty, and accept the occasional, reverently tossed morsel from a child who was struck dumb by her elegance. She ate alone, daintily, and considered the frantic scrabbling of the ducks to be a kind of moral failing made manifest.

The ducks, for their part, were not troubled by her philosophy. They were troubled by her refusal to move. She would often park herself directly in the middle of the most lucrative bread-dropping zone, a living, breathing velvet rope. ‘She thinks the sun shines from her tail feathers,’ grumbled a drake named Paddler, shaking his head and sending a spray of water over his neighbours. ‘She’s a blockage in the river of commerce, is what she is.’

‘Let her be,’ said a wise, old duck named Marta. ‘The world has a way of providing its own lessons, and they are rarely gentle.’

As autumn bled into winter, a change came over the Royal Lake of Aurelia. The air grew sharp, the light turned from gold to pewter, and a filigree of ice began to creep out from the reedy shallows. The visitors grew fewer, the bread grew scarcer, and the ducks grew plumper, having stored their summer’s frantic earnings as fat against the coming cold. Circé, meanwhile, grew more magnificent. The frost on the bare branches of the willows seemed to be an imitation of her, and the first snowflakes, as they settled upon her back, looked like diamonds on white satin.

Then, one morning, the world was silent. The lake had frozen. It was not a mere dusting of ice, but a solid, crystalline sheet that stretched from shore to shore, a vast pane of frosted glass under a leaden sky. The Royal Lake was, for all practical purposes, gone.

At first, Circé found the situation rather becoming. She stood upon the ice, a perfect white statue, and admired her reflection, which was now clearer and more defined than ever before. The ice was a superior sort of water, she decided. It was still, it was silent, and it did not allow for the distasteful bobbing of ducks. The ducks, however, were in a state of quiet panic. Their world had been sealed shut.

‘The lake is dead!’ quacked Paddler, waddling nervously on the slippery surface.

‘Nonsense,’ said Barnaby the goose, who was having a difficult time maintaining his dignity. ‘It is merely… resting.’

But Marta, the old duck, knew better. ‘The water is not gone,’ she said calmly, her breath pluming in the frigid air. ‘It has merely hidden itself. But we know where it breathes.’ She led them, a huddled, shivering procession of brown and grey, not towards the centre of the lake where the ice was thickest, but towards the far northern shore, where a cluster of ancient, gnarled oaks grew. Their roots, she explained, held the earth in such a way that the current, weak but persistent, could never be entirely vanquished. There, in a small, dark circle no bigger than a drawing-room table, the water remained open, steaming gently in the cold.

Circé, from her central position of icy splendour, watched them go. She saw them disappear behind the trees and felt a pang of something she could not name. It was not hunger, not yet. It was a disquieting sense of being excluded from a narrative she had not written. The silence of the frozen lake, which had seemed so elegant at first, now felt oppressive, absolute.

Days passed. The cold deepened. The sky remained the colour of a bruise. The last of the children’s offerings had been consumed long ago. Circé, whose diet had been one of aesthetic choice rather than necessity, now felt the gnawing reality of the void Barnaby had spoken of. Her magnificent plumage, once a source of pride, now felt thin and useless against the biting wind. She watched the distant circle of open water and saw the ducks dipping and rising, their movements no longer vulgar, but vital. They were a community engaged in the simple, sacred act of survival.

Her pride was a fortress, but hunger was a patient siege engine. One evening, as the sun bled a weak, violet light across the horizon, Circé made a decision. She would not go to them as a supplicant. She would go as one noble to another, to inquire about the… the hydrological peculiarities of the northern shore. She would glide over, with all her inherent grace, and they would, of course, make room for her.

But gliding was no longer possible. Walking on the ice was a graceless, shuffling affair. Each step was a humiliation. When she finally reached the cluster of oaks and peered into the small, dark pool of open water, she saw the ducks. They were all there, huddled together for warmth, their dark heads bobbing in the steam. They looked up at her. There was no mockery in their eyes, no triumph. There was only a weary, guarded watchfulness.

Paddler ruffled his feathers. Marta simply watched.

Circé stood at the edge of the ice, the proud swan, the paragon of Aurelia, and found that she had nothing to say. Her epigrams about style and purpose were frozen on her tongue. Her poise was a fragile shell around a core of cold and emptiness. She had been right about so many things—about beauty, about elegance, about the foolishness of the crowd. But she had been catastrophically wrong about the one thing that mattered: the location of the water.

She lowered her long, golden beak towards the dark, steaming circle. It was the most difficult movement she had ever made. It was not a bow of grace, but a gesture of surrender.

Marta paddled slowly towards her. The old duck did not speak. She simply moved aside, making a small space in the crowded, life-giving pool. It was not a large space. It was not a place of honour. But it was open water.

And for the first time in her life, Circé, who had always believed herself to be the sole ornament of the lake, understood that she was, in the end, merely a part of it. She slipped from the ice into the water, and the cold shock of it was a kind of baptism. She was no longer the most beautiful creature on the lake; she was simply a bird, alive, in winter. And in the shared steam of their common breath, that was enough.
