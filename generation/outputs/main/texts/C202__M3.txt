# The Inoculant

The candle had burned down to a stub, and still Dr. Alistair Faraday hunched over his microscope, the back of his neck aching with the effort of keeping his one good eye pressed to the lens. The study behind the lecture hall of the Culloden Medical Academy smelled of camphor and vinegar and something else — something sweet and rotten that he had learned to stop noticing. His fingers trembled as he lifted the glass slide. The sample from his own blood showed it again: those strange, branching filaments he had first observed in Elspeth's tissue three days after she died.

He leaned back and pressed his palm against his sternum, where a dull heat had taken up residence. Twenty-one days since he had pressed the infected matter into the cut on his forearm. Eighteen since the fever had begun its slow climb through his body.

A knock at the door. He covered the slides with a cloth and swept the vials into the drawer before he answered.

"Dr. Faraday?" The night porter, McDougal, held out a folded letter. "A boy brought this from the house on Candlemaker Row. The young woman said it was urgent."

Alistair took the letter without opening it. He knew the handwriting, the way the tail of the M in Margaret curled like a fern frond. He thanked McDougal and waited until the footsteps retreated before unfolding the page.

*You were not at dinner again. I sent word to Dr. Kincaid and he said you have not attended lectures in a fortnight. I am coming to you tomorrow whether you wish it or not. I will bring broth and I will sit with you and you will tell me what you are doing. You owe me that much. — M.*

He folded the letter and placed it beneath the microscope. Tomorrow was too soon. He needed three more days at least to track the progression of the filaments through successive blood samples. Three days to prove that the agent multiplied in the blood, that it was not merely a symptom of the disease but its cause. Three days to confirm that the tincture of quinine and white willow he had begun taking yesterday arrested the growth of the filaments in vitro, which might mean it could arrest them in the body.

Three days was a long time. His hands shook. His vision blurred at the edges. He had lost nearly two stone since the experiment began, and his collarbones stood out like the ridgeline of a roof.

But Elspeth had lost more. Elspeth, who had been twenty years old and singing as she hung the washing in the yard, who had laughed at him for his solemnity when he'd returned from his first year at the academy. She had sickened in the autumn and died before the first snow, and Dr. Kincaid himself had stood at the foot of her bed and said it was a putrid fever, an imbalance of the humours, and there was nothing to be done.

There was always something to be done. That was what Alistair believed, what he had staked his career and now his life upon.

He unlocked the lower drawer of his desk and took out the leather journal. He recorded the evening's observations in his cramped, deteriorating hand: the density of the filaments, their apparent rate of division, the color of the serum. Then he uncorked the small bottle of tincture and measured out ten drops into a glass of water. It tasted bitter and metallic, like chewing on a penny.

He slept on the cot in the corner of the study, and his dreams were of Elspeth standing in a field of white flowers, her mouth open but no sound coming out.

Margaret arrived at half past nine, as she had promised. She carried a basket covered with a cloth, and her face had the particular arrangement of calm that Alistair had come to understand meant she was terrified. She set the basket on his desk and looked at him, and he watched her take in the hollows of his cheeks, the way his coat hung loose on his frame, the sweat that beaded on his forehead though the room was cold.

"You are ill," she said.

"I am conducting an experiment."

"You are dying." Her voice did not waver. She pulled the cloth from the basket — broth, bread, a small pot of honey — and then she pulled the chair from his desk and sat down. "Tell me what you have done."

So he told her. He told her about the filaments he had found in the tissue samples he had taken from Elspeth before burial, how he had preserved them and studied them for two years, how he had traced the same structures in the blood of patients at the Royal Charity Ward who presented with the same wasting fever. He told her how he had cultivated the agent in a medium of beef tea and sugar, and how he had introduced it into his own blood to observe its progression from the inside, to track its course in a living body rather than a dead one.

Margaret listened. She did not interrupt. When he finished, she said, "And what have you learned?"

"That the agent is not a symptom. It is the cause. It enters the blood and multiplies. It consumes the red corpuscles and replaces them with itself. The fever is the body's attempt to burn it out, but the body cannot burn hot enough."

"And the treatment?"

He hesitated. "The tincture. I believe it may arrest the growth."

"You believe."

"I have not yet proven it."

Margaret stood. She came to him and took his face in her hands, and her palms were cool against his skin. "You are the proof," she said. "If the tincture does not work, you will die, and you will have proven only that you were wrong."

"Thousands die of this fever every year, Margaret. In the slums, in the villages. Children. Mothers. If I can prove the cause and find a treatment —"

"You are not the only physician in Edinburgh. You are not the only man who could find the answer."

"I am the only one who has seen it. The only one who has looked."

She released his face and stepped back. Her eyes were bright, and he could see the effort it cost her to speak evenly. "Elspeth would not have wanted this."

"You did not know what she would have wanted. None of us knew her mind at the end. She was burning, Margaret. She was burning and she could not tell us what she felt."

The room was quiet. Somewhere in the building a door closed, and footsteps echoed in the lecture hall below.

Margaret said, "Will you let me help you?"

"How?"

"I will record your observations when your hand fails. I will measure the tincture and bring your meals and watch while you sleep. And when the experiment is finished — if you survive — you will let me read your journal, and I will tell you if you have written the truth or only what desperation has shown you."

He wanted to refuse. The work was his alone, the debt to Elspeth his to pay. But his hand was shaking, and the words on the page of his journal had become a scrawl he could barely decipher, and Margaret's face held an steadiness that he recognized as the same quality he had seen in the best surgeons: the ability to look at suffering and not turn away.

"Sit down," he said. "I will show you the slides."

She pulled her chair to the microscope, and he showed her the filaments — branching, pale, unmistakable. She looked for a long time. Then she said, "It looks like frost on a windowpane."

He had never thought of it that way. But she was right. The agent that had killed his sister, that was even now multiplying in his own blood, was beautiful in the way that frost is beautiful: intricate, geometric, and cold.

He sat down at the desk and began to dictate, and Margaret wrote in her clear, round hand, and the morning light came through the window and fell across the pages of the journal where the truth of the fever was taking shape, word by careful word, in the quiet room at the top of the stairs in the old medical school on the hill.
