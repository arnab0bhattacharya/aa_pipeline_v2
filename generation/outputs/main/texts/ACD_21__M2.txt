The rain lashed against the windows of my consulting rooms on Baker Street with a persistence that suggested the heavens themselves were in a state of profound dissatisfaction. I was occupied with a tedious case of a disputed will, the ink in my blotter still wet from my last entry, when the door was pushed open with a force that admitted a gust of cold, wet air and a man who looked as though he had just emerged from a shipwreck.

He was a tall, gaunt figure, dressed in a coat of once-respectable brown that now hung loosely upon his frame, its elbows worn through to the lining. His face was a study in anxiety, the skin drawn tight over high cheekbones, his eyes wide and darting about the room as if expecting the walls to close in upon him. He clutched a damp umbrella in one hand and a small, leather-bound notebook in the other, his fingers white-knuckled.

“Mr. Holmes?” he gasped, stopping in the center of the room. “You are the man? They said you were the one to see when the police have given up and the law has failed.”

“I am,” I said, rising from my chair. “And you are, I presume, Mr. Silas Thorne, the bookseller of Charing Cross Road? Sit down, sir. The fire is low, but the water in the kettle is hot. Shall I pour you a cup of tea?”

He did not sit. He paced to the window, then back to the fireplace, his steps quick and irregular. “Tea? No, no. I have no time for tea. I have no time for anything but this. I am a ruined man, Mr. Holmes. My reputation, my trade, my very sanity, are all at stake, and I do not know why.”

“Pray, take your seat, Mr. Thorne. You are shivering. Fear is a poor companion, and it clouds the mind. Tell me exactly what has happened. Begin at the beginning.”

He sank into the armchair, trembling visibly. He placed the notebook on the table between us. It was a small volume, its cover scuffed, its pages filled with a cramped, hurried script.

“I have been in the book trade for twenty years,” he began, his voice rising in pitch. “I have served every scholar, every collector, and every eccentric in London. I know every rare volume in my stock. But last month, a new customer came in. A man of no particular distinction. He wore a grey suit, kept his hat low, and asked for specific books. Not novels, not histories, but atlases. Old ones. Mercator, Blaeu, Ortelius. He bought them with cash, no questions asked. I thought him a student, perhaps a geography teacher. But the way he handled the books… it was not with the reverence of a scholar, but with the precision of a surgeon.”

Holmes leaned forward, his eyes narrowing. “Go on.”

“He returned the books one by one over the next three weeks. He would bring a volume back, pay the deposit, and leave. I checked the books, as is my custom, for damage. They were perfect. Until I noticed the gaps. One page missing from each. Cut out with a razor. Cleanly. No tear, no ragged edge. Just a square hole where a page had been. I assumed it was a mistake, a prank by a student. But when the fifth book came back, and then the sixth, I began to count. Each missing page contained a map. Not a map of a known country, but a fragment. A coastline here, a river there. Nothing obvious connected them. I tried to put them together, but they were like pieces of a puzzle from different boxes.”

“Did you confront him?” Holmes asked, his voice calm but sharp.

“I tried to watch him. I saw him take a cab to the East End. I followed him, but he lost me in the fog. The next day, he did not return. The books were gone. He had not paid for the last three. And now, Mr. Holmes, I am afraid. Not of the loss of the books, but of what he is doing. Those maps… I looked at them again last night. I pinned them to my study wall. And I saw it. They are not random. They are a route. A route that leads to a place that should not exist. Or perhaps a place that was hidden for a reason. And now that he is gone, I am left with nothing but holes in my books and a terror that I cannot name.”

Holmes stood up and walked to the window. The rain had lessened, turning the street outside into a blur of grey and black. “You say the maps contain fragments. You say they form a route. Do you have the pages, Mr. Thorne?”

“Yes. I kept them. I could not throw them away. They are here, in this notebook. I copied them out, as best I could, before I realized the significance.”

Holmes took the notebook and examined the drawings. They were meticulous, drawn in ink, showing snippets of topography. One showed a jagged coastline with a single anchor symbol. Another showed a winding river with a bridge marked by a cross. A third showed a cluster of trees with a single stone marker.

“Interesting,” Holmes murmured. “You are a bookseller, Mr. Thorne, not a cartographer. How do you know these are not merely decorative illustrations?”

“Because of the date,” Thorne said, his voice dropping to a whisper. “These maps are from the 1600s. They are accurate to the inch. And the final fragment… the one from the last book… it shows a house. A red house, with a white door, and a tree on the left. It is in Kent. I know Kent. I have been there. That house is in a village called Huxley’s Hollow. It has been empty for a hundred years. Or so the villagers say.”

Holmes turned to me. “Watson, bring me the map of Kent. And fetch my magnifying glass.”

I retrieved the items, and Holmes spread the large map across the table, aligning the fragments of the notebook against the paper. He worked in silence, his brow furrowed, his lips moving slightly as he calculated distances and bearings. The room was still, save for the ticking of the clock on the mantelpiece.

“Here,” he said at last, pointing to a small red dot on the map. “Huxley’s Hollow. And here, the river. And here, the coastline. It is a journey. From the coast, up the river, to the village. But why? What is in that red house?”

“I don’t know,” Thorne said. “But I know the man who took them. I saw his face clearly for a second when he left the shop yesterday. It was the face of a man who had seen something terrible. And who was afraid of it. I believe he is not looking for treasure, Mr. Holmes. I believe he is looking for a way to escape it. Or perhaps, to put it to rest.”

Holmes looked at Thorne with a sudden, piercing intensity. “Mr. Thorne, you say the man was afraid. Fear is a powerful motivator. But it is also a fragile one. If he is afraid of what is in that house, why did he go to the trouble of stealing the maps? Why not simply stay away?”

Thorne hesitated. “Perhaps because the house is calling him. Perhaps because he believes that if he can find the key—the final piece of the map—he can unlock the secret and free himself. But he is gone now. And I am left with the fear that if he finds it, something will happen. Something bad.”

Holmes stood up, buttoning his coat. “I think we shall pay this red house a visit, Mr. Thorne. I suspect that the mystery of the missing pages is only the beginning. And I suspect that the man who took them is either dead, or in great danger. Or perhaps, he is already inside that house.”

I looked at Thorne, who was pale and trembling. “You will go?” he asked.

“I shall,” Holmes said. “And I shall take you with me, Mr. Thorne. For you are the only one who knows the full extent of the theft. And in my experience, a thief who steals maps is often looking for a way out of a labyrinth he has built for himself. We shall see if he has found his way out, or if he is still lost within it.”

As we prepared to leave, the rain began to fall again, heavier than before, washing the grime from the streets of London and, perhaps, from the minds of those who walked them. I felt a chill that had nothing to do with the weather. The case of the missing pages was not just a matter of stolen property. It was a matter of memory, of history, and of a secret that had waited three centuries to be found. And now, it had found us.
