I had taken the shop in West Street, Brighton, in the November of 1893, a narrow-fronted place wedged between a slop-tailor and a tea-dealer. I was recovering from a bout of influenza and had but forty pounds in the bank. The previous tenant had been a photographer of sorts, and he had left behind a good deal of stuff—chemicals, battered tripods, and a dark-room that smelt strongly of stale coffee and silver nitrate. I determined to set up as a portrait photographer. It was a practical trade, demanding no great genius, only a steady hand, a civil tongue, and a working knowledge of exposure times.

The lens, however, was my own. I had bought it from a bankrupt optical firm in Clerkenwell. It was a double-anastigmat of a new and peculiar pattern, designed, I believe, for some astronomical instrument. It possessed a depth of field and a sensitivity to certain ultra-violet rays that I had never encountered in any commercial glass. I fitted it to my old bellows camera, found it remarkably sharp, and thought no more about it.

It was Mrs. Gloop, the greengrocer’s wife, who sat for the first portrait. She was a large, irascible woman, come to commemorate her fortieth birthday. I posed her in the Windsor chair, adjusted the magnesium ribbon, and made the exposure. When I developed the plate in the red light of the dark-room, I was annoyed to find, immediately behind her left shoulder, a faint, greyish smudge. I put it down to a flaw in the emulsion, cleaned the plate meticulously, and thought no more of it.

But the smudge persisted. It appeared in the portrait of the bank clerk, a weedy young man with a high collar; it was there behind the three plump daughters of the retired colonel. It was always behind the left shoulder. It was not a blur, exactly. It had a sort of structure. Under my magnifying glass, it seemed to possess a vertical, vaguely humanoid outline. 

I said nothing to my sitters. They, in any case, were delighted. The faint, misty shape, which I could not bring myself to call a shadow, gave my portraits a peculiar distinction. It was an age of spiritualism, and the public was eager for marvels. The bank clerk declared it was the spirit of his dead grandfather; the colonel’s daughters thought it "sweetly eerie." My business trebled. I found myself taking photographs, day in, day out, of fools who came to sit before my lens and pay me two-and-six for the privilege of being haunted. 

But I am a practical man. I do not hold with spirits. I had bought a lens of exceptional power, and it was recording something that ordinary glasses missed. I began a series of experiments. I photographed the empty studio. Nothing. I photographed the street outside. Nothing. I photographed myself, sitting grimly in the Windsor chair, holding the bulb. The plate, when developed, showed the smudge behind my own shoulder.

I was alone in the room. I had locked the door. There was no mirror, no trick of the light. Yet the plate showed a shape. It was faint, but it was denser than before. It was taller. 

I began to feel a distinct unease. The shape was not static. In the early photographs, it had seemed a distant, formless thing. But as the weeks passed and the plates accumulated, the shape grew denser. It was coming closer. 

I stopped taking portraits for a day. I dismantled the camera, cleaned the lens, and rebuilt the apparatus. I took a photograph of a plaster bust. The shape was there, hovering just behind the plaster shoulder. It was unmistakably a head and shoulders now. There were no features, only a grey, granulated mass, but it had the proportions of a human form.

I was angry. I am a photographer, not a medium. I told myself it was some flaw in the glass, some trick of the ultra-violet light. I would expose the nonsense. I invited my friend Dr. Hopper, a medical man with a turn for science, to see the phenomenon. I posed him in the chair. I made the exposure. I developed the plate while he watched.

The shape was there, behind his shoulder. But it was not behind his shoulder. It was beside his neck. It was closer. 

Hopper was delighted. "A psychic force, my dear fellow!" he cried. "You have a gift. You must charge more."

I did not charge more. I closed the studio. I sat in the dark-room, staring at the row of drying prints. The shape was growing. It was a man, or the idea of a man. It was a thing of grey, swirling particles, like smoke caught in a draught. And it was coming closer.

I began to understand. The lens was not seeing spirits. It was seeing something else. Something that exists alongside us, something that is normally invisible, something that is drawn to the heat of a living body. The lens, with its strange sensitivity, was recording its approach.

I took a photograph of the empty chair. The shape was there. It was sitting in the chair.

I took another, an hour later. The shape was leaning forward. It was reaching out.

I have a practical mind. I do not believe in ghosts. But I believe in what I see, and I see a shape that is coming closer. I have locked the studio and come to my lodgings. I have brought the camera. I have set it up in my bedroom. I have taken a photograph of my bed. 

The shape is on the bed.

It is not a ghost. It is something else. It is something that has always been there, perhaps, waiting for someone to see it. And now it has been seen. And it is coming closer.

I am writing this in the gaslight, my back to the wall. I have the revolver on the table. I do not know if a revolver will stop a thing of grey, swirling particles. I do not know if it is hostile. I only know that it is coming closer. 

I hear a sound. It is a faint sound, like the rustle of dry leaves. It is behind me.

I will turn around.
