The letter arrived on a Tuesday, the day the carrier from Bury came through Ashwell Thorpe with his cart, and Mrs. Priscilla Gannet knew before she broke the seal that it was from her son. She had waited for it the way a field waits for rain—not doubting, only enduring. She set down her sewing, smoothed her apron twice, and carried the letter to the window, where the light was best.

*My dear Mama,* it began, in the sloping, confident hand she had paid Mr. Devereux the schoolmaster eleven pounds to cultivate, *London is everything you promised and more. I have been received at the best houses. Colonel Haskett's lady has pronounced me a natural gentleman, and I dined only last week at the Bellamy, at the very table where the Duke of Cumberland once sat, and the waiter knew my name.*

She read it three times, and each time her heart swelled like a loaf in the oven. She had known it. She had always known it, from the hour he was born squalling in the room above the chandler's shop she and her late husband had kept, and every hour since, when she had sold the shop and moved to the cottage at the end of Mill Lane so that the boy might have a garden and clean air and no smell of tallow about his clothes. She had hired the dancing master from Stowmarket. She had bought him the grey gelding, and the second-best boots from Mr. Pell's, and when he had said, at nineteen, that a man of parts must go to London or wither, she had opened the tin box beneath her bed and counted out the notes without a word of complaint.

He wrote again in the spring, asking for a little more. The tailor's bill, he explained, was a matter of keeping up appearances among people who noticed such things. She sent it. In the summer he wrote of a horse he might buy at a bargain, a bay mare with a white foot, which would let him ride in the Park and be seen. She sent it. In the autumn the letters grew shorter and the sums larger, and she sold the grey gelding's empty stall, and then the silver tea service her husband had given her on their tenth anniversary, and then, though she told no one, the little gold locket with his hair in it.

By the following winter there was nothing left but the cottage and the pension from her husband's old regiment, which was small, and the letters had stopped coming altogether.

---

Mr. Algernon Gannet had, in fact, discovered London to be a more exacting creditor than he had imagined. He had discovered it in the second month, when the first tailor's bill came due and he had no notion of how a gentleman paid such things except by ordering more. He had discovered it more sharply in the fifth, when the bay mare went lame and a man at Tattersall's explained, with a patience he found humiliating, precisely how much he had been cheated. And he had discovered it finally, in the eleventh month, when his purse held three shillings and sixpence and the landlord of his lodgings in Half Moon Street asked, with a bow that was somehow worse than a blow, whether the young gentleman might find it convenient to settle.

He thought of writing to his mother. He wrote the letter four times and burned it four times, for each draft said, in one way or another, *I have spent everything you had, and there is nothing to show for it but a coat I cannot pay for.* He could not send that to the woman who had sold her locket. He could not go home and stand in the doorway of the cottage and be looked at.

So he sold the coat. He sold the boots and the hat and the silver-topped cane, and he took a room in a mean street off the Strand, and he answered an advertisement in the newspaper, and on a grey morning in February he presented himself at the service door of the Bellamy Hotel and was engaged as a waiter, at fourteen shillings a week and his board.

---

The Bellamy was a great stone pile on the corner of a square, with a portico of six columns and a porter in scarlet who stood at the door like a guardsman. Algernon had dined there twice in his season of glory—once at the table by the window in the coffee room, where he had ordered a mutton chop and a bottle of claret and tipped the waiter a sovereign for the pleasure of being called *sir*; and once, memorably, in the great dining room itself, at the best table, the one beneath the chandelier, where he had eaten turtle soup and pressed a half-crown into the hand of the man who took his hat, and had gone home to his lodgings feeling that London had, at last, admitted him.

Now he stood in the passage behind the coffee room at half past six in the morning, in a plain black coat and a white apron, learning from an old waiter named Cobb how to fold a napkin into the shape of a water lily.

"Not like that," Cobb said. "You'll have it looking like a dead crow. Watch." Cobb's hands moved like a conjuror's. "Where'd you learn your airs, then? You talk like a parson's son."

"Nowhere," Algernon said. "I mean—the country."

"Ah," said Cobb, and looked at him a moment too long, and said nothing more.

The first week was a lesson in the geography of shame. He carried trays through the coffee room and heard, at the table by the window, two young men in fine coats discussing a horse they meant to buy, and one of them said, "I'd give a hundred guineas and be glad of it," and Algernon, setting down their toast, felt his ears burn. On the fourth day he saw Colonel Haskett's lady in the hall, in a plum-coloured pelisse, waiting for her carriage, and he turned his face to the wall and polished a brass rail until she had gone. On the sixth day a gentleman called him "my good fellow" and flicked a shilling at him, and he caught it out of the air before he remembered to be ashamed.

But it was the best table that undid him. On a Saturday evening in March, a party of eight came in for a birthday dinner—a merchant and his family, from Leeds, by the look of them, the women in good silk and the men a little uneasy in their cravats. Cobb was to take the head of the table and Algernon the side. The chandelier blazed. The turtle soup went round. And at the head of the table the merchant's eldest son, a young man of perhaps twenty, called out to Algernon for more wine, and then, when Algernon bent to pour it, said to the company at large, "You see how they train them here? You'd think he was a lord himself. I daresay he's been to a better school than I have."

The table laughed. Algernon poured the wine without spilling a drop, and said, "Will there be anything else, sir?" and the young man said, "No, no, that's all, my good fellow," and turned away.

He went out into the passage afterwards and stood with his back against the wall, and Cobb, coming through with a tray of glasses, stopped and looked at him.

"Best table's the worst," Cobb said. "Always is. They can smell it on you, the ones that's come up quick. They don't mean nothing by it. They're only frightened themselves."

"I don't know what you mean," Algernon said.

"No," said Cobb. "You wouldn't." And he went on with his tray.

---

The letter came in April, in a hand he did not know, from a neighbour in Ashwell Thorpe. Mrs. Gannet had taken a chill in February and had not risen from her bed since, and the neighbour thought the son should be told, though the son had not been home in a year and a half and no one knew where to find him.

He read it in the passage behind the coffee room, in the grey light of the morning before service, and then he folded it and put it in his pocket and went in and folded the napkins into water lilies, and at noon he asked Cobb to speak to the manager for him, and at two o'clock he was on the coach from the Bellamy yard, with his wages in his pocket and his apron folded on the seat beside him.

He did not know what he would say to her. He had spent a year and a half learning the words that gentlemen used, and none of them would serve. But he had fourteen shillings a week now, and his board, and a trade in his hands, and he thought—as the coach rattled out through the fields, past the milestones, into the flat green country of his childhood—that he might begin by telling her the truth, and that perhaps the truth, spoken plainly, was the only thing he had ever owned that was worth the having.
