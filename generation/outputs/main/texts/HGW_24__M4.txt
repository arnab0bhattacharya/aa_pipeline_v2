The Imperial College of Science and Technology stood in those days a little aloof from the general traffic of South Kensington, a great brick and terracotta pile whose windows stared with a kind of patient inquiry down upon the museum-going crowds. It was a place of corridors that smelled faintly of coal-gas and formaldehyde, of lecture-rooms where the blackboards were never quite clean, and of a general atmosphere of earnest, unglamorous endeavour that I found, on first acquaintance, both stimulating and a little oppressive. I had come there, I should explain, by way of a scholarship—a circumstance that I mention not from any wish to advertise my cleverness, but because it is the key to everything that followed. My father was a stationer in Camberwell, a man of limited means and unlimited respectability, and it was his firm conviction, expressed often and with a certain mournful pride, that I was to be the first of our family to rise above the counter and the till.

For the first two terms I was, I think, a model of industrious obscurity. I attended my lectures, I made my notes, I dissected my dogfish with a conscientiousness that was perhaps a little excessive, and I kept very much to myself. The college was a strange new world to me. There were men there who had come from schools I had only read about in the newspapers—Winchester men, Rugby men, men who spoke with a careless ease and a curious flatness of vowel that I found both fascinating and repellent. There were others, too, from the board schools and the grammar schools, men like myself, who had won their places by dint of sheer grinding application, and who wore their learning as though it were a slightly uncomfortable suit of clothes. Between these two groups there was a gulf that no amount of common interest in the binomial system could quite bridge.

It was in the second year that I fell in with a set that met, most afternoons, in the refreshment-room of the Natural History Museum—a lofty, echoing place where one could get a cup of tea and a bun for twopence, and where the talk ran, with a freedom that I found intoxicating, upon every subject under the sun. There was Merivale, a tall, languid young man from somewhere in the West Country, who had a private income and a passion for the more obscure coleoptera; there was Miss Ashby, a small, dark, intense young woman who was reading for a degree in chemistry and who spoke of the suffrage question with a fervour that made me feel profoundly uneasy; and there was Fotheringay, a stout, red-faced, argumentative person who had come up from a grammar school in Leeds and who was for ever quoting Huxley and Spencer and Darwin in a broad Yorkshire accent that seemed to me the very essence of intellectual daring.

I was flattered, I confess, by their notice. I had never before been in the way of hearing such talk, and I found it easy to persuade myself that I had found my true level at last. I began to adopt, in a small way, the manners and phrases of my new companions. I affected a certain carelessness in my dress, a certain scepticism in my opinions. I learned to say "quite" and "rather" and "I shouldn't wonder" with the best of them. I even, I am ashamed to record, began to drop the aspirates that had been my father's pride and my mother's care. It was not a conscious deception—or if it was, I did not admit it to myself—but a kind of protective colouring, an instinctive imitation of the creatures about me.

And then, one grey November afternoon, my father came to see the college.

He had written to me a week before, a letter in his careful, copperplate hand, saying that he had business in town and would like to look me up. I had read the letter with a sinking heart. I had pictured him, with a vividness that was almost painful, coming up the steps of the college in his Sunday best—his black coat, a little shiny at the seams, his stiff white collar, his bowler hat, which he would remove with a flourish as he entered the portal—and I had pictured the looks of my companions, the raised eyebrows, the faint, ironic smiles. I had written back, with a clumsiness that I now find hard to forgive, suggesting that we might meet instead at a tea-shop in the Brompton Road. But my father, who was a man of simple and direct habits, had replied that he would prefer to see the place where his son was studying, and that he would present himself at three o'clock on Thursday afternoon.

He came, punctual to the minute. I saw him from the window of the laboratory, a small, neat figure in his black coat and bowler, standing at the foot of the steps and looking up at the great façade with an expression of mingled awe and satisfaction. I hurried down to meet him, and as I did so I was aware of Fotheringay at my elbow, and of Miss Ashby in the doorway behind me.

"Your father?" said Fotheringay, in his loud, cheerful voice. "Let's have a look at him."

I muttered something about having to go and meet someone, and slipped out into the corridor. My father greeted me with a warmth that was, I think, a little surprised. He shook my hand, and then, in an access of feeling, put his arm through mine.

"Well, my boy," he said, "and this is the place, is it? It's a fine building, a very fine building. I daresay you've got some grand company in here."

I said that the company was much like any other, and tried to steer him towards the side entrance. But he would not be hurried. He stood in the middle of the corridor, looking about him with the frank curiosity of a man who has never been inside such a place in his life, and he asked me questions—about the lectures, about the professors, about the specimens in the museum—in a voice that seemed to me to carry to the furthest corner of the building. I answered him in monosyllables, and kept glancing over my shoulder at the door of the refreshment-room, from which I could hear the sound of laughter and the clatter of teacups.

It was at that moment that Fotheringay came out. He saw us, and his face lit up with that terrible, genial curiosity of his.

"Hallo, there!" he cried. "Is this the governor? How do you do, sir? Your son's been telling us nothing about you at all."

My father, who was the soul of courtesy, shook hands with him warmly. "I'm sure I'm very glad to meet any friend of my boy's," he said. "He's not much of a hand at letter-writing, I'm afraid, but I daresay he's got other things to think about."

Fotheringay laughed, and said something about the claims of science, and then, to my horror, he turned to me and said, "You never told us your father was in the stationery line. That's a very useful trade, that is. I wish I'd had a father who could have kept me in notebooks."

I felt the blood rush to my face. I saw my father's look of mild surprise, and I saw the dawning comprehension in Fotheringay's eyes. And in that moment I had a vision of myself as I must have appeared to them all—the silent, shabby young man who had been so careful to say "rather" and "quite," who had dropped his aspirates and affected a scepticism he did not feel, and who had been all the while the son of a Camberwell stationer, a man who sold penny exercise-books and twopenny pens over a counter, and who was now standing in the corridor of the Imperial College, with his bowler hat in his hand, looking at his son with an expression of puzzled and patient love.

I do not know what I said. I think I made some excuse and hurried my father away. I remember walking with him down the Brompton Road in a kind of fever, talking loudly about nothing, and I remember his silence, which was more eloquent than any reproach. He left me at the corner of the museum, and as he shook my hand he said, in a voice that was very quiet and very steady, "I daresay I shouldn't have come. I'm sorry if I've put you out."

And then he was gone, a small, neat figure in a black coat, walking away from me through the November drizzle, and I was left standing on the pavement with the taste of my own shame in my mouth.

I did not go back to the college that afternoon. I walked for a long time, and I thought of many things—of my father's shop in Camberwell, with its smell of gum and paper and its rows of penny notebooks; of my mother, who had died when I was a boy, and who had always said that I was to be a great man; of the careful economies that had made my scholarship possible; and of the easy, careless laughter of the refreshment-room, which I had been so anxious to share. And I saw, with a clarity that was almost painful, that I had been trying to be something I was not, and that in the process I had been something very much worse than I need have been.

The next day I sought out Fotheringay and told him, in as few words as I could manage, that my father was a stationer in Camberwell, and that I was proud of him. Fotheringay looked at me for a moment with a curious expression, and then he said, "Well, and what of it? My father's a draper in Leeds. I thought you knew." And he laughed, and clapped me on the shoulder, and that was the end of the matter.

But it was not quite the end for me. I have never been able to think of that afternoon without a certain pang. My father died two years later, before I had taken my degree, and I have often wished that I had had the courage to acknowledge him in the corridor of the Imperial College, before Fotheringay and Miss Ashby and all the rest of them, and to have said, "This is my father, who has worked all his life behind a counter so that I might stand here and dissect dogfish and talk about the binomial system." It would have been, I think, a small thing, and a very easy thing, and yet it was the one thing I could not bring myself to do. And that, I suppose, is the way with most of us: we are ashamed of our origins, and then we are ashamed of our shame, and the shame of the shame is the worst of all.
