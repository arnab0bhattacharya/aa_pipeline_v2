The rain in Ghent had a spiteful, northern quality; it did not fall so much as it scoured the slate roofs and the cobblestones, a grey and ceaseless assault that made the gas-lamps in the Rue de la Vigne burn with a feverish, unnatural light. From the window of my room in the Pensionnat St. Claire, I could see the spire of St. Nicholas’s, a black and pointed finger accusing the heavens. It was a fitting view for my thoughts, which were likewise dark and accusatory.

For three weeks, the school had been a crucible of whispers and sidelong glances. Madame de Vries, our directress, was retiring to a convent in Bruges, and the post of Headmistress was to be filled from within. The choice, it was universally understood, lay between Miss Priscilla Thorne, the senior mistress of English literature, and Monsieur Léon Valois, who taught the natural sciences and geography.

I, Miss Anne Holloway, occupied an intermediate station. I was neither senior nor popular. I taught English composition and grammar to the younger girls with a quiet diligence that brought me no credit and spared me, for the most part, the school’s more violent political storms. I was a sparrow on the roof, observing the eagles as they wheeled and clashed.

And clash they did, with a fury that belied their placid, academic exteriors. Miss Thorne was a woman of sharp angles and sharper opinions, her piety as severe as her black bombazine dresses. She saw the school as a fortress of virtue under siege by the laxity of the age. Monsieur Valois was her perfect antithesis: a man of soft words and softer morals, who spoke of progress and the Enlightenment with a fervour that, to my mind, smacked of vanity. He believed the girls should be taught to reason, to question; Miss Thorne believed they should be taught to obey.

Each, in turn, sought me out. Miss Thorne would corner me in the chilly cloister, her breath pluming in the cold air. “Miss Holloway,” she would begin, her voice a low, urgent rasp, “you are a woman of principle. You see the rot in this place. Valois would have these girls reading Voltaire and doubting their Maker. He would turn our school into a den of godless philosophers. I know I can count upon your voice in the council.”

I would murmur my assent, my eyes fixed on the worn flagstones, hating the deceit. I did not believe in her cause. Her “principles” felt to me like a cage of iron, and her God a merciless jailer.

Then Monsieur Valois would find me in the library, his handsome face alight with a conspiratorial smile. “Ah, Miss Holloway,” he would say, his voice a low caress, “you, with your love of the poets, you understand that a mind must be free to soar. Thorne would clip our wings and bind us to a dreary psalter. She would make automatons of these bright young creatures. A vote for me is a vote for the future—for light, for liberty!” He would press my hand, his own warm and dry, and I would feel a cold revulsion.

I did not believe in his cause either. His “liberty” seemed a shallow, self-serving thing, a cloak for his own ambition and his desire to be adored.

I disliked them both with a passion that frightened me. And so I told each what they wished to hear, offering them the lukewarm currency of my agreement, and in doing so, I forged a chain of lies that I was destined to wear.

The crisis came on a Tuesday evening. A special meeting of the teaching staff was called in Madame de Vries’s parlour. The air was thick with the scent of beeswax and barely suppressed animosity. The old directress, a frail bird of a woman, presided from her wingback chair. The subject of the dismissal of the junior mathematics master, a young man named Dubois, for alleged incompetence, had been raised.

Miss Thorne saw her chance. “Madame,” she said, her voice ringing with righteous certainty, “Monsieur Dubois’s failure is but a symptom of the disease. It is the lax, modern methods encouraged by Monsieur Valois that have permitted such incompetence to fester. He speaks of reason, but where is the discipline? Where is the rigour? I have seen his own students’ work—it is a scandal of disorder!”

A murmur went through the room. All eyes turned to Monsieur Valois, then, and then, most horribly, to me. Miss Thorne had cited my “excellent and orderly” classroom as the model of proper teaching. Monsieur Valois, his face pale with fury, leapt to his feet.

“This is slander!” he cried. “A base and personal attack! Miss Thorne knows nothing of science, of progress! She clings to the dead forms of the past. As for my methods, I appeal to any here who have a mind unclouded by prejudice. Miss Holloway,” he said, turning to me, his dark eyes burning into mine, “you are a person of intellect and fairness. You have seen my work. You have spoken to me of the beauty of a free mind. Will you not speak now?”

It was a trap. The air crackled. Miss Thorne’s gaze was a spear. Monsieur Valois’s was a plea. Madame de Vries watched me, her old eyes sad and knowing. My heart hammered against my ribs, a frantic, trapped bird. To speak for one was to condemn the other, and to condemn myself to the victor’s camp. To speak for neither was to be seen as a coward. To speak the truth—that I thought them both ambitious hypocrites—was unthinkable.

I opened my mouth, but no sound came out. My silence stretched, a vast and damning chasm. I looked at my hands, clasped so tightly in my lap that the knuckles were white. I thought of my small room, my quiet books, my fragile peace. I chose my peace. I chose silence.

In that void, Miss Thorne’s voice rose again, sharp and triumphant. “You see, Madame? Even Miss Holloway, who values order above all, cannot in good conscience defend him.”

Monsieur Valois stared at me, his expression one of utter disbelief, of betrayal. In that moment, he was not a rival for a post, but a man whose appeal for justice had been met with a coward’s refusal. He sank back into his chair, the fight gone out of him.

The vote was taken. Monsieur Valois was dismissed.

The weeks that followed were a purgatory of my own making. Miss Thorne was confirmed as Headmistress. A new, grim order descended upon the Pensionnat St. Claire. The library was purged of its more “questionable” volumes. The girls’ chatter was silenced by a new regime of discipline. Miss Thorne walked the corridors like a victorious general, and when she passed me, she would give a small, tight smile of acknowledgement, the smile of a conspirator to her silent accomplice. Her gratitude was a weight heavier than any condemnation.

Monsieur Valois’s departure was swift and without ceremony. I saw him one last time from my window, a tall figure in a dark coat, carrying a single trunk to a waiting fiacre. He did not look up at the school. The rain had ceased, and the wet streets reflected the bruised purple of the twilight sky. The fiacre rattled away, and he was gone.

The silence he left behind was not peaceful. It was the silence of a grave. I walk the same corridors now, but I feel like a ghost. The injustice I witnessed, and to which my silence gave its assent, has become a part of the very air I breathe. I had sought to preserve my quiet life, but I see now that it was built on a foundation of sand, washed away by the storm of my own cowardice. The peace I purchased with my silence is a hollow thing, and in the long, quiet evenings, I hear nothing but the echo of a man’s ruined career and the sound of my own unforgivable quiet.
