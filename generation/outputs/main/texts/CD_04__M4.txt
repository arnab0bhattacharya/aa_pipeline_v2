Mr. Alderman Tupplebury Vane, of the firm of Vane, Vane, and Vapour, corn-chandlers and general merchants, and Mayor of the good town of Stodgeborough for the second time in his life (which he was wont to observe, with a jocularity that never failed to bring down the table, was the second time in anybody’s life who had the good fortune to be Mr. Alderman Tupplebury Vane), awoke upon the morning of the great day with the sun shining full upon his counterpane, and with a song in his heart.

The great day! The day of days! The day upon which the new Town Hall of Stodgeborough—that edifice of the Corinthian order, with a portico that was the pride of the county and a clock-tower that was the envy of the neighbouring boroughs—was to be opened with a public banquet, a procession, a brass band, and a speech from Mr. Alderman Vane that he had been composing in his bath for the space of three months, and which he confidently anticipated would be reported in the *Stodgeborough Sentinel* at full length, with the adjective “eloquent” inserted no fewer than four times by the editor, a gentleman of independent spirit but a just appreciation of the advertisements.

“Mrs. Vane,” said the Alderman, rising and drawing the curtains with a hand that trembled with pleasurable anticipation, “Mrs. Vane, the sun shines. The sun shines, ma’am, upon the greatest day in the annals of Stodgeborough.”

“It shines into the cistern,” replied Mrs. Vane, who was a practical woman, and who had never in twenty years been able to see a silver lining without immediately remarking upon the cost of the umbrella. “The ball-cock is out of order again, and the water is running down the stairs.”

“Pooh, pooh!” said the Alderman. “A trifle, ma’am, a trifle. What is a ball-cock—what, I ask you, is a cistern—when the eyes of the county are upon us? When the Lord Lieutenant himself has promised to attend? When the band of the Stodgeborough Rifle Volunteers will discourse ‘The Roast Beef of Old England’ beneath the very portico? When——”

He was interrupted by a knock at the door, and by the entrance of his confidential clerk, Mr. Pilbeam, a small, pale, agitated man, who had the appearance of one who had been running, and who carried in his hand a letter which he held at arm’s length, as though it were a reptile.

“Sir,” said Mr. Pilbeam, “a letter. By the morning post. From Mr.—— from the contractor.”

“From Mr. Boodle?” cried the Alderman, joyfully. “From Boodle? He is to be here at nine with the final account, the keys, and the last of the brass-work. Read it, Pilbeam, read it! I am no hand at reading aloud before breakfast.”

Mr. Pilbeam read. He read in a voice that shook like a leaf in autumn, and the letter ran thus:—

“Honoured Sir,—I regret to inform you that, owing to circumstances over which I have no control, and the extreme tightness of the money-market, I have been compelled to—in short, sir, I have absconded. The roof is not finished. The funds are not forthcoming. The men have not been paid. I am, sir, your obedient, humble servant, T. Boodle.”

There was a silence. The Alderman sat down upon the edge of the bed. He looked at Mr. Pilbeam. Mr. Pilbeam looked at the Alderman. The clock upon the stairs—the clock that had been presented to the Alderman by the subscribers to his first mayoralty—struck eight, and seemed to say, in its jocund way, “Ha! ha! ha! Eight o’clock, and no roof!”

“Pilbeam,” said the Alderman, at length, in a hollow voice, “is this a jest?”

“It is a letter, sir,” said Mr. Pilbeam, “and the postmark is London.”

“The roof,” said the Alderman, “is not finished.”

“It is open to the sky, sir,” said Mr. Pilbeam, “in several places. The lead is down in the courtyard. The workmen have gone off in a body. And there is a rumour in the town, sir—I blush to repeat it—that the foundation-stone was never properly laid, and that the whole concern is—is——”

“Is what, Pilbeam?”

“Is what the vulgar call a sham, sir,” said Mr. Pilbeam, and burst into tears.

Now, the reader is to understand that Mr. Alderman Vane was a man of a sanguine and majestic turn of mind, who had never in his life admitted a difficulty without immediately discovering in it a subject for a speech. He had been heard to observe, upon the occasion of a fire in the High Street, that it was a magnificent opportunity for the display of the Stodgeborough Fire Brigade; and when the river had overflowed its banks and flooded the lower town, he had publicly congratulated the inhabitants upon the excellence of the drainage, which had carried off the water so rapidly that nobody could possibly have been drowned. To such a man, the discovery that his Town Hall had no roof was not so much a calamity as a—a contingency. It required, as he himself expressed it, “looking in the face.”

He looked it in the face for the space of five minutes, and then he rose, and his countenance was radiant.

“Pilbeam,” said he, “we will dine.”

“Dine, sir?”

“Dine, Pilbeam. The banquet is at three. The Lord Lieutenant comes at two. The band plays at half-past two. The rain—for I perceive, Pilbeam, that the sun has gone in, and that there is every prospect of a heavy shower—the rain, I say, will be regarded as a dispensation of Providence. We will dine under the roof—under the *idea* of a roof, Pilbeam—and if any gentleman remarks upon the absence of the ceiling, we will refer him to the stars.”

“But there are no stars, sir,” said Mr. Pilbeam, “at three o’clock in the afternoon.”

“Then we will refer him to the clouds,” said the Alderman, with dignity. “The clouds, Pilbeam, are a noble canopy. The ancients dined in the open air. The Greeks—the Romans—the—— the whole of antiquity, sir, was in the habit of dining without a roof. It is we moderns who have become effeminate. We will dine, Pilbeam, as the Romans dined.”

“With umbrellas, sir?” suggested Mr. Pilbeam, faintly.

“With umbrellas,” said the Alderman, “if necessary. And I will make a speech, Pilbeam, that shall be remembered when the roof is forgotten.”

The banquet was held. The Lord Lieutenant came, and was received with cheers. The band played. The corporation walked in procession, the Alderman in his robes, the mace-bearer before him, and the rain—for it rained, as Mr. Pilbeam had predicted, in torrents—descended upon the assembled company with a impartiality that was almost sublime. The tables were laid in the great hall, which was open to the sky in three several places; and the guests, with napkins on their heads and umbrellas over their shoulders, ate their turtle-soup in a silence that was broken only by the drumming of the rain upon the silk, and by the occasional observation of a gentleman in the rear that he “hadn’t come there to be drowned.”

The Alderman rose to speak. He stood beneath the central aperture, and the water ran down his nose, and he did not wipe it away. He spoke for an hour and twenty minutes. He spoke of the genius of the architect, of the munificence of the subscribers, of the commercial greatness of Stodgeborough, of the glorious constitution of old England, and of the peculiar satisfaction it afforded him to see so many gentlemen assembled beneath—he paused, and looked up at the dripping rafters—“beneath,” said he, “the broad and ample canopy of heaven.”

There was a cheer. It was not a loud cheer, but it was a cheer.

“The roof,” continued the Alderman, “is—is in a state of transition. It is—it is undergoing that process of completion which is the inevitable lot of all great undertakings. The contractor—the contractor, gentlemen, has—has been called away. He is—he is engaged upon other works. But I say to you, gentlemen, that the Town Hall of Stodgeborough, roofed or unroofed, is—is a monument of which any town might be proud. And if,” said he, raising his glass and his voice together, “if the rain descends, let it descend! It is a baptism, gentlemen—a baptism of the edifice! It is the blessing of Providence upon our enterprise! I give you—the Town Hall of Stodgeborough!”

The company drank, with chattering teeth. The band struck up “See the Conquering Hero Comes.” And Mr. Alderman Vane, with the water trickling from his chin, and his heart swelling within him, sat down, and was heard to observe to the Lord Lieutenant that it was the proudest moment of his life.

Whether it was the proudest moment of his life is not for me to say; but it was certainly the wettest. And when, a fortnight later, Mr. Boodle was apprehended at Liverpool, and the missing funds were found to have been invested in a speculative building at Birkenhead, the Alderman was the first to propose that the corporation should present him with a testimonial, “in recognition,” as he phrased it, “of the spirited manner in which he had endeavoured to carry out a great public work under circumstances of unusual difficulty.” The testimonial was a silver inkstand, and the inscription was composed by the Alderman himself. It was very long, and it was very wet, and it was presented at a dinner which was held, by a happy inspiration, in the open air.
