Mr. Theophilus Gubbins, of "Inglemere," Chalk Lane, Tooting Bec, was a man of independent means and of an inventive turn of mind, and it was his habit of an afternoon to sit in his garden shed—which he called his laboratory—and to brood upon the imperfections of the created universe. His particular grievance at this time was the matter of animal speech. It seemed to him a scandalous oversight in nature that a dog, which could smell a rabbit at fifty yards and hear the rustle of a mouse beneath the frost, should be unable to communicate the results of these perceptions to the man who fed it. And so, with a coil of platinum wire, a battery of his own devising, and a great many discarded drawings upon squared paper, he had set himself to bridge the gap.

The machine, when it was at last assembled, presented nothing very remarkable to the eye. It was a cabinet of polished mahogany, somewhat larger than a hat-box, furnished with a brass dial and a pair of vulcanite receivers upon a flexible cord, very much like those of a telephone. It was Mr. Gubbins's theory that the vibrations of the larynx in the lower animals, though inaudible or meaningless to the human ear, were yet regular, and capable of being translated by a sufficiently delicate apparatus into the terms of human speech. He had tried it upon his own cat, a cynical beast named Solomon, but had obtained nothing but a prolonged and rather contemptuous silence, which he was inclined to attribute to the cat's temperament.

His neighbour, Mr. Adolphus Pinhorn, was a retired coal-merchant of a somewhat aggressive respectability, who kept a fox-terrier called Binks. Binks was a dog of spirit, and it had long been Mr. Gubbins's private conviction that Binks was a much misunderstood animal. And so, upon a fine Thursday in September, when the Pinhorns were gone to Margate for the day, Mr. Gubbins invited Binks into his laboratory with a piece of cold mutton, and adjusted the receivers to that worthy animal's throat.

There was a whirring, a clicking, and then a voice—a small, clear, querulous voice, such as one might expect from a very old and somewhat dyspeptic gentleman—issued from the brass trumpet of the machine.

"I say," said the voice. "I say, that's a bit of all right, that is. Mutton. Good old mutton. I could do with a bit more of that, I don't mind telling you."

Mr. Gubbins was so startled that he dropped his notebook. He recovered himself, however, with the true scientific spirit, and addressed himself to the machine.

"Good afternoon, Binks," he said, rather foolishly.

There was a pause. The dog looked up at him with an expression of profound suspicion.

"Who's that?" said the voice. "Who's that talking? Is that the old buffer with the shed? 'Ere, I don't like this. I don't like this at all. It's not natural, that's what it isn't."

"It is I, Mr. Gubbins," said the inventor soothingly. "I have merely—"

"Merely what?" interrupted the voice, with a sudden access of alarm. "Merely what? 'Ere, you put me down. I know your sort. You're one of them vivisectionists, you are. I've 'eard about you. Cats and dogs and 'orses and that. Oh, lor'."

"Pray calm yourself," said Mr. Gubbins. "I assure you my intentions are purely scientific."

"Scientific!" said Binks, and there was a world of scorn in the little voice. "Scientific! That's what they all say. Wot about my tail, eh? Wot about my tail? You just keep your 'ands off my tail, that's all."

"I have not the slightest desire to touch your tail," said Mr. Gubbins, with some irritation. "I wish merely to converse with you. To learn your views upon—upon the world in general."

There was a long silence. The dog sat down and scratched himself thoughtfully.

"Well," said Binks at last, "you may as well make yourself useful. 'Ave you got any more of that mutton?"

Mr. Gubbins, who was a bachelor and kept a cold joint in the larder, produced a second slice. Binks wolfed it down, and then, with a grunt of satisfaction, settled himself upon the mat.

"Now then," said the voice, more affably. "Wot d'you want to know?"

"I am curious," said Mr. Gubbins, "as to your opinion of your master."

Binks was silent for a moment. Then he gave a short, unpleasant laugh.

"Old Pinhorn?" said he. "Old Pinhorn? You want my opinion of old Pinhorn?"

"If you would be so good."

"Right you are," said Binks. "Right you are, guv'nor. I don't mind if I do. Well, to begin with, 'e's a liar."

"Good heavens!" said Mr. Gubbins.

"A liar," repeated Binks firmly. "A liar and a 'ypocrite. 'E goes on something shocking. You wouldn't believe the things that man says. 'E says 'e's fond of me. 'E says it to the missus. 'E says it to the butcher. 'E says it to the vicar. 'Fond of me,' 'e says. And then what does 'e do? 'E goes and 'as me took to the vet to be—well, you know. And 'e calls it 'seeing to the little dog.' Seeing to the little dog! I ask you."

"Dear, dear," said Mr. Gubbins.

"And 'e's stingy," Binks went on, warming to his theme. "Stingy ain't the word. 'E gives me the leavings of the 'ouse, and 'e calls it dog-biscuit. Dog-biscuit! I've 'ad better out of a dustbin. And when 'e takes me for a walk, it's always the same walk, round the same square, at the same time, and 'e never lets me stop to smell nothing. 'Urry up, 'e says. 'Urry up, Binks. Wot's the good of 'urrying? I ask you. It's not as if 'e 'ad any business to attend to. 'E's retired. 'E's got nothing to do but 'urry."

Mr. Gubbins was now thoroughly interested. He drew his chair closer and adjusted the receivers.

"And Mrs. Pinhorn?" he asked. "What of her?"

"Ah," said Binks, and there was a distinct change in his tone. "The missus. She's different, she is. She's got a 'eart, the missus 'as. She slips me a bit of something nice when 'e's not looking. She talks to me. She says, 'Poor old Binks,' she says. 'Poor old Binks.' She understands. But she's afraid of 'im, that's what she is. Afraid of 'im. 'E bullies 'er. 'E bullies 'er something cruel. You should 'ear 'im go on about the 'ousekeeping money. It's a disgrace, that's what it is. A fair disgrace."

"Indeed," said Mr. Gubbins, whose own dealings with Mr. Pinhorn had been of a somewhat strained character, owing to a dispute about a boundary fence.

"And the things 'e says about you," said Binks suddenly.

"About me?" said Mr. Gubbins, startled.

"About you," said Binks, with evident relish. "Oh, 'e's got it in for you proper. 'E says you're a 'umbug. 'E says you're a lazy, good-for-nothing, interfering old—well, I won't repeat it, it ain't fit for 'uman ears. 'E says you ought to be locked up. 'E says you're a menace to the neighbourhood. 'E says—"

"Enough," said Mr. Gubbins, who had turned rather pale. "I think—I think I have heard sufficient."

But Binks was not to be stopped. He continued for some minutes in a vein of the most scurrilous personal abuse, attributing to Mr. Gubbins a variety of crimes and vices, some of which the inventor had never even heard of. And then, quite suddenly, the door of the laboratory opened, and there stood Mr. Adolphus Pinhorn himself, returned unexpectedly from Margate, and in no very good humour.

"What's this?" said Mr. Pinhorn, looking from the dog to the machine and back again. "What's this? What are you doing with my dog?"

Mr. Gubbins rose in some confusion. "I was merely—merely making a few experiments—"

"Experiments!" said Mr. Pinhorn. "Experiments! On my dog! I'll have the law on you, that's what I'll do. I'll have the law on you."

And he did. The case of Pinhorn v. Gubbins came on at the Southwark County Court in the following November, and excited a good deal of interest in the neighbourhood. Mr. Pinhorn's counsel, a young man from the Temple, made great play with the words "vivisection" and "outrage," and spoke of the sanctity of the Englishman's home, and the domestic hearth, and the faithful hound that guards it. Mr. Gubbins, who conducted his own defence, attempted to explain the nature of his machine, but was repeatedly interrupted by the judge, who observed that he had no patience with scientific mumbo-jumbo, and that a dog was a dog, and there was an end of it.

The upshot was that Mr. Gubbins was fined five pounds and costs, and bound over to keep the peace. He paid the fine with a very ill grace, and retired to his laboratory in a state of profound dejection. Binks, he was given to understand, had been sent to the country to stay with Mrs. Pinhorn's sister, and was not expected to return.

But the thing that rankled most with Mr. Gubbins was not the fine, nor the humiliation, nor even the loss of his machine, which he dismantled and put away in a cupboard. It was the memory of that small, clear, querulous voice, and the things it had said. For he could not help reflecting that, if a dog could speak so freely of its master, what might not be said of him, in the kitchens and the areas of Chalk Lane, by those who had no machine to translate their thoughts into human speech. And he took to sitting in his garden of an afternoon, watching the cats upon the wall, and wondering what they were thinking.
