The light in the drawing room was the colour of weak tea, a thin, watery gold that drained the crimson out of the carpet and left the mahogany table looking grey and tired. Outside, the rain had ceased, but the wetness remained, clinging to the windowpanes in heavy, diamond-shaped drops that refused to fall. She watched them, Sylvia; watched how they trembled and held, and thought that she too was holding, trembling on the edge of something, though what that something was, she could not say. It was the hour of the afternoon when time seems to unravel, when the clock on the mantelpiece ticks not with the regular march of seconds, but with a slow, dragging reluctance, stretching the minutes like taffy until they snap.

He was speaking. Edward. Edward was always speaking, or thinking of speaking, his mind a great, grey engine of logic that turned over and over, grinding the grist of the law into fine, digestible flour. He sat opposite her, his hands resting on his knees, his posture as rigid and upright as the columns of the Inn where he spent his days. He was her father’s pupil, the most promising one, the one who had the gift of turning a phrase in Latin, who could find the flaw in a conveyance as a terrier finds a rat. He admired her. She knew this with a certainty that was heavy and dull, like a stone in the pocket. He admired her hair, which was the colour of chestnuts, and her eyes, which he said were like the sky before a storm (though today they were merely tired), and he admired her silence, which he mistook for depth.

“And so,” he was saying, his voice rising slightly at the end of the clause, “the settlement must be absolute. There can be no ambiguity. That is the beauty of the law, is it not, Sylvia? That it fixes things. That it makes them real.”

“Yes,” she said. The word floated out of her, weightless. “It fixes things.”

She looked at him, really looked at him, searching for the spark, the friction, the thing that would make her heart turn over or her breath catch. But there was only Edward. His collar was starched stiffly against his neck; his hair was parted with mathematical precision. He was good. He was kind. He was, as her father said, a man of substance. And because there was no reason not to—no other suitor, no scandal, no hidden passion for a poet or a soldier—she had accepted him. She had let him put the ring on her finger, a small, cold diamond that glittered with a hard, unyielding light. Now she was engaged, and she spent the days looking for the reason why. She felt like a detective at the scene of a crime, searching for the motive, finding only the body.

“Shall we walk?” she asked suddenly. The room was too close. The heavy curtains, the velvet upholstery, the smell of beeswax and old paper—it was all pressing in on her. She needed the air, the sharp, cold bite of the London evening to clear her head.

“The ground will be wet,” Edward observed, glancing at the window. “But if you wish it.”

They walked to Lincoln’s Inn. It was not far, but the transition was absolute. They left the domestic softness of Kensington and entered the grey, stone world of men and statutes. The gas lamps were beginning to flicker on, casting long, dancing shadows against the high brick walls. The air here smelled of damp earth and ancient dust, the smell of centuries of litigation and quiet study. It was a hallowed place, a place of silence and order.

Sylvia walked beside him, her gloved hand resting lightly on his arm. She felt the wool of his coat, the solidity of him. He was walking to his chambers to fetch a document, some deed or will that required his attention. To him, the Inn was a cathedral; to her, it was a labyrinth. She looked up at the windows of the barristers’ chambers. They were squares of yellow light, glowing like eyes in the twilight. Behind them, men were sitting, reading, arguing, their lives bound up in the interpretation of words. Did they ever look out? Did they ever wonder if the words meant anything at all, or if they were just a net to catch the slippery fish of reality?

“Here we are,” Edward said, stopping before a heavy oak door. “I shan’t be a moment.”

He disappeared into the gloom of the stairwell. Sylvia stood alone in the twilight of the courtyard. The silence of the Inn was profound, broken only by the distant rumble of a carriage on the Strand and the soft cooing of a pigeon roosting in the eaves. She looked up. The sky above the rooftops was a bruised purple, fading into a pale, watery green. It was beautiful, she thought, with a sudden, sharp pang. It was beautiful and indifferent.

She thought of her father, sitting in his library, his wig on the stand, his eyes tired behind his spectacles. He had arranged this. He had seen Edward’s brilliance, his steadiness, his future in the silk gown, and he had seen Sylvia’s need for stability. It was a transaction, sensible and sound. But standing here, in the shadow of the law, she felt a sudden, wild rebellion. She wanted to tear the ring from her finger and throw it into the fountain. She wanted to run, to disappear into the fog, to become a smudge on the pavement, a ghost in the machine.

But where would she go? There was nowhere. She was a young woman, and the world was a set of rooms, and she was expected to move from the drawing room to the dining room to the bedroom of the man who loved her. She felt a sudden, irrational desire to laugh. It was all so absurd, so terribly, terribly dull. The grand machinery of life, the laws of property and contract, all grinding away to produce this: a girl standing in a courtyard, waiting for a man to fetch a paper.

Edward emerged from the doorway, clutching a bundle of papers tied with red tape. He looked relieved, his duty done.

“Ready?” he asked.

“Yes,” she said.

They walked back towards the gate. The lamps were fully lit now, casting a golden pool of light on the wet cobblestones. As they passed under the archway, a gust of wind swept through, rattling the leaves of the plane tree and sending a shiver down Sylvia’s spine. She drew her shawl tighter around her shoulders. Edward offered her his arm again, and she took it.

“It is a fine evening,” he said, looking up at the sky. “A fine evening indeed.”

“Yes,” she said. And then, because she could not find the reason, and because the light was fading and the stone was cold and he was there, solid and real and hers, she leaned her head slightly against his shoulder, and walked on into the dark.
