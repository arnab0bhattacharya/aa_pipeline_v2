It was with a certain scientific detachment, I think, that I first observed the peculiar properties of the little glass jar that Professor Merrow kept upon his mantelpiece. He was a man of precise habits and an almost painful lucidity of mind, a lecturer in physics at the university, and his rooms in the Iffley Road were a model of academic order. The jar itself was a common enough object—a squat, wide-mouthed confectioner’s vessel with a ground-glass stopper—but it sat in a place of honour, and upon its contents the Professor’s spectacles would dwell with a curious, proprietary fondness. Within, there was nothing to be seen but a faint, shimmering distortion, like the air above a hot stove, a tiny vortex of apparent nothingness that twisted and turned upon itself in a silence that was somehow profound.

I had occasion to ask him about it one evening, as we sat over our pipes and a decanter of the College port. “That?” said he, following my gaze. “That, my dear Penderel, is a little piece of insolence. A small, private rebellion against the tyranny of natural law.” He leaned forward, his lean, ascetic face illuminated by the firelight. “I have reversed the polarity of space itself within that vessel. A local, contained inversion. In that jar, my friend, the laws of physics run backwards. Effect precedes cause; heat flows from cold to hot; a dropped object accelerates upwards.”

I confess I laughed. It seemed a piece of harmless, if ingenious, mystification. “And what happens if you put your watch inside?” I asked.

“It would, I fancy, begin to un-tick,” he replied, with a thin smile. “The mainspring would unwind itself into a state of tension, the hands would rotate in a counter-clockwise direction, and the entire mechanism would, in a sense, remember its future and forget its past. But I have not dared to try. The consequences for any complex mechanism are, shall we say, unpredictable.”

He was a man who delighted in such paradoxes, and I thought no more of it, dismissing the jar as a clever piece of apparatus designed to illustrate a lecture on the conservation of energy, or some such thing. The Professor was a bachelor, and his wants were ministered to by a Mrs. Hodges, a woman of immense practical competence and a profound distrust of anything that could not be scrubbed, boiled, or put to some sensible domestic use. She held the Professor’s scientific apparatus in a kind of awe, but the jar on the mantelpiece, with its empty, shimmering nothingness, she regarded with particular suspicion. It was, she confided to me once, “unchancy.”

The catastrophe, when it came, was of a singularly domestic character. I had called upon the Professor one Tuesday afternoon to return a book, and found him out. Mrs. Hodges, her sleeves rolled to the elbow and a duster in her hand, admitted me. “He’s gone to a meeting of the Society,” she said, with a sniff that suggested she considered such gatherings a frivolous waste of a good afternoon. “You can wait in the study if you like, Mr. Penderel. I’m just doing the dusting.”

I took a chair by the window and watched her work. She was a vigorous, energetic woman, and her dusting was less an act of delicate cleansing than a species of punitive expedition against dirt. She moved about the room with a brisk, clattering purpose, her duster flicking at the spines of books and the tops of picture frames. I saw her eye the jar on the mantelpiece with a familiar expression of disapproval. She reached for it with her duster, meaning, I suppose, to give it a good, sound whack, and in her zeal, her elbow caught the edge of the jar.

It was all over in an instant. The jar toppled, hung for a moment in a kind of dreadful equilibrium, and then fell. It struck the fender with a sharp, clean crack and shivered into a dozen pieces. And then—there was no explosion, no flash of light, no clap of thunder. There was simply a sound. It was a soft, sucking noise, like a vast indrawn breath, and in the same instant, the world went mad.

My first impression was one of profound vertigo. The room seemed to lurch, not physically, but in some more fundamental way. The fire in the grate, which had been burning cheerfully, suddenly flared up and then, with a strange, guttering sound, began to burn backwards. The flames retreated from the coals, the smoke curled downwards and into the firebox, and the logs, which had been glowing red, began to blacken and grow cold, as if a film were being run in reverse. The clock on the mantelpiece, a solid, respectable piece of Victorian engineering, gave a series of frantic, backward ticks, its hands spinning wildly in a counter-clockwise direction, and then stopped dead.

Mrs. Hodges screamed. It was a sound of pure, animal terror, and it was the only thing in the room that seemed to obey the normal laws of time. I saw her drop her duster. It did not fall to the floor. Instead, it rose, fluttering, towards the ceiling, where it hung, a ridiculous, limp shape against the plaster. The dust, which she had been so vigorously pursuing, began to gather itself from the surfaces of the furniture, from the air itself, and to coalesce into little grey clouds that drifted purposefully back towards the shelves and the books, as if returning to their rightful homes.

But the most extraordinary phenomenon was the Professor’s armchair. It was a large, well-worn affair of leather and horsehair, and it now began to exhibit the most alarming behaviour. The leather, which had been cracked and worn, grew suddenly smooth and supple; the horsehair stuffing, which had been compressed by years of use, plumped itself up with a soft, sighing sound. The chair was, quite unmistakably, growing newer. It was un-ageing before my eyes. And then, with a sound like the tearing of stiff paper, the fabric began to unravel. The threads of the covering separated, the horsehair stuffing erupted in a cloud of wiry fibre, and the wooden frame itself, with a series of sharp, splintering cracks, began to come apart, not as if it were being broken, but as if it were being un-made, returning to the component parts from which it had been assembled.

I stumbled to my feet, my mind reeling. The air was full of a fine, floating debris, and the light from the window seemed strange and uncertain. Outside, I could hear a confused noise from the street: the clatter of hooves and wheels, but played backwards, so that the sound of a departing cart preceded the sound of its approach. A man’s voice, shouting a greeting, was followed by the slam of a door, and then the door opened again with a soft click.

My eyes fell upon a vase of flowers on the sideboard. The blooms, which had been fresh that morning, were already faded. Now, I saw them lift their heads. The brown, withered petals flushed with colour, the drooping stems grew straight and green, and the flowers, in a silent, vegetable frenzy, closed themselves into tight buds. It was the most uncanny sight I have ever witnessed. It was life, not dying, but un-living.

I realised, with a sudden, cold clarity, that the pocket of reversed time was not static. It was expanding. It was spreading outwards from the shattered jar, a slow, invisible tide of cosmic anarchy. The very wallpaper was beginning to peel, not away from the wall, but back onto it, the pattern of roses and lilies re-asserting itself over the faded patches. I knew I had to act. My mind, trained in the exact sciences, grappled with the problem. The Professor had said he had reversed the polarity. To neutralise a field, one must introduce an opposing field. The jar was broken, the containment gone. But the principle remained.

I seized the poker from the fireplace. It was a heavy, iron instrument, and as I lifted it, I felt a strange, tingling sensation in my arm. I plunged it into the heart of the disturbance, into the shimmering, distorted air where the jar had fallen. For a moment, nothing happened. Then, there was a violent, wrenching sensation, as if the universe were being twisted back into shape. The poker grew hot in my hand, then cold, then hot again. The reversed fire in the grate gave a final, convulsive leap and then settled into a normal, cheerful blaze. The duster fell from the ceiling. The clock on the mantelpiece gave a single, hesitant tick, and then began to move forward once more.

The silence that followed was profound. The room was a wreck. The armchair was a heap of splintered wood and tangled horsehair. The vase of flowers was a withered, blackened ruin. The air was thick with dust. Mrs. Hodges was slumped against the wall, sobbing quietly. And I stood in the middle of it all, the poker still in my hand, feeling as though I had just wrestled with a ghost.

When Professor Merrow returned an hour later, he found me attempting to restore some order to his study. He listened to my account in silence, his face a mask of scientific fascination. He examined the splintered chair, the withered flowers, the twisted poker. He said nothing for a long time. Then he turned to me, and his eyes were shining with a light I had never seen before. “Good heavens, Penderel,” he said, his voice trembling with excitement. “You’ve done it. You’ve established the principle of the counter-field. The applications are… limitless.” He looked at the wreckage of his room, and a slow smile spread across his face. “I shall have to get a new chair, of course. But the data… the data is invaluable.”
