The rain that evening fell in cold, steady sheets, turning the gas lamps of Baker Street into blurred halos of yellow light. I sat by the fire, nursing a headache that had refused to yield to aspirin, when the door burst open without the customary knock. In stood Mrs. Evelyn Thorne, a woman whose composure seemed to be cracking like old porcelain under the weight of her distress. She was dressed for the street, her hat askew, her hands trembling so violently that she could not manage to remove her gloves.

"Mr. Holmes," she gasped, leaning against the doorframe. "You must help me. My husband, he is in danger. I am certain of it."

I rose and gestured to the chair opposite mine. "Pray sit down, Mrs. Thorne. You will hurt yourself if you stand there. I will not speak until you are seated. Thank you. Now, slowly, tell me what has happened."

She sank into the chair, wringing her hands together. "It is Mr. Thorne. He has just returned from his six years in China. He is home from the ship, settled in our lodgings in Kensington. But something is wrong, Mr. Holmes. It is a wrongness I cannot name, yet I feel it in my bones."

"Be precise," I said, leaning forward. "Facts, Mrs. Thorne. No guesses."

"It began this morning," she said, her voice thin but steady. "When his luggage was delivered from the packet boat. I was unpacking his trunks in the bedroom. I am a careful woman, Mr. Holmes. I note such things. The trunks were large, leather-bound, and locked. The man from the dock said they were delivered exactly as they arrived from the ship. But when I unlocked the first trunk, I knew at once that it had been opened."

I raised an eyebrow. "How so?"

"The lining," she whispered. "It is a silk lining, dark green. There was a tear in the seam near the bottom. But that is not all. The clothes had been rearranged. Mr. Thorne is meticulous. He folds his shirts in a specific way, and his books are stacked by size. In the trunk, they were a jumble. And there, in the lining of the pocket of his travel coat, I found a scrap of paper."

"Did you remove it?"

"I did. I held it to the light. It was a piece of blotting paper, with a single word written in ink: *Watch*. It was nothing more, and yet it terrified me."

I nodded slowly. "And the man?"

"There is a man, Mr. Holmes. A stranger. He has been in London, I think, for some time. He is tall, thin, with a grey beard and eyes that seem to miss nothing. He was at the dock when Mr. Thorne arrived. I saw him from the carriage window. He stood by the bollard, hands in his pockets, watching the crowd. He did not approach Mr. Thorne. He never does. He simply follows. Yesterday, I saw him in the park as we walked. Today, I saw him in the shop where I bought tea. He is always there, just out of reach. He observes, and then he vanishes."

Holmes tapped his fingers on the arm of his chair. "You say he never approaches. How far does he keep?"

"Never less than twenty yards. If we turn a corner, he is gone, but if we stop, he appears to be reading a newspaper or tying his shoe. It is a game of shadows, Mr. Holmes. And now, with the trunk disturbed and that note in the coat, I believe the game is ending."

"Your husband," I said. "Is he aware of this?"

"He suspects," Mrs. Thorne admitted. "He is a man of the East, Mr. Holmes. He has lived among people who value discretion above all else. He told me he felt eyes on him. He thinks it is a local curiosity, perhaps a journalist seeking a story about the 'Mysterious Missionary.' But I know better. This is personal. This is dangerous."

I stood up and walked to the window, looking out at the darkening street. "Mrs. Thorne, your husband’s mission in China... was it purely religious?"

She hesitated. "He was a missionary, yes. But he also collected antiquities. Porcelain, jade, scrolls. He was very successful. There were... competitors. Men who would do anything to possess what he found. Perhaps he offended someone powerful. Perhaps he stole something he should not have."

"Or perhaps," I said, turning back, "he found something he should have destroyed."

She looked at me sharply. "You speak in riddles, Mr. Holmes."

"I speak in possibilities. The man in the street is a watcher. The searched trunk is a message. The word *Watch* is a warning. Your husband is being tested. The antagonist wants to know if he is aware of the surveillance. If your husband ignores it, the man will escalate. If your husband reacts, he will know that his cover is blown."

"What must I do?" she cried. "I cannot leave him alone."

"You will do exactly as I tell you," I said, my voice dropping to that quiet, authoritative tone that brooked no argument. "You will not go to your husband tonight. You will not tell him what you have told me. You will go home, you will sit by the fire, and you will wait for him. But you will keep the window closed. You will not look out."

"And if he is attacked?"

"Then I shall be there. I am going to see this man. I am going to find out who he is and why he follows. But I must do it without your husband’s knowledge. If he knows I am involved, the dynamic changes. The watcher must believe he is still unobserved, or he will strike."

Mrs. Thorne stood up, her fear now tempered by a grim resolve. "You will protect him?"

"I will find the truth," I corrected. "Protection is a byproduct of the truth. Now, go. Do not let your face betray your fear. A woman who looks over her shoulder invites trouble. Walk with your head high, as if the street belongs to you."

I watched her exit, her shoulders straightening as she regained her composure. I rang the bell for Watson.

"Watson," I said. "Put on your coat. We have a case. A man in a grey beard, likely tall, likely wearing a dark overcoat. We are to follow him, not the other way around. And be careful. We are in the city of London, and tonight, it is a place of shadows."

I picked up my tobacco pouch and my pocket watch. The rain had stopped, but the air was thick with mist, obscuring the gaslights and the faces of the pedestrians. I smiled, for there is no satisfaction quite like the prospect of unraveling a mystery before it has fully tightened its grip. The missionary, the watcher, the torn silk, the word *Watch*. It was a puzzle of pieces that fit together with terrible precision. And I intended to solve it before the final piece was placed.

"Come, Watson," I said, opening the door to the night. "The game is afoot."
