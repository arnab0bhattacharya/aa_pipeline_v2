The affair of the *Alicia*—for so she was called, though her papers, I have since had occasion to believe, told a different and perhaps less romantic story—began, as such things will, in a manner so trifling that a landsman would have found it hard to credit the weight it came to bear. We were some three weeks out from the Sandwich Islands, bound for the guano coast, and the weather had settled into that heavy, breathless uniformity which is the true face of the Pacific in those latitudes—a vast, polished plain of water under a sky of hammered brass, where the sun by day and the stars by night seemed to mock the slow agony of our progress. The ship moved as if drugged, her sails hanging limp and yellow against the glare, and the creak of her timbers was the only sound that broke the silence, a sound so monotonous that it came at last to seem less a noise than the audible expression of our common weariness.

Captain Vane was a man of the kind that the sea, in its mysterious economy, will sometimes throw up to command other men—a hard, silent, close-fisted New Englander, with a face like a weathered figurehead and eyes that held no more warmth than a dead fish. He drove us as though the ship were a machine and we the fuel that fed it. There was no cruelty of the spectacular sort, no flogging at the gangway for the mere pleasure of it, but a grinding, day-by-day exaction of labour that wore a man down to the bone and left him with nothing but a dull, smouldering resentment. He had a way of standing at the break of the poop, his hands clasped behind him, and watching us work with a patience that was more terrible than any outburst of temper. It was the patience of a man who knew that time and the sea were on his side, and who could afford to wait.

I was second mate, and I shared the crew’s hatred of him with an intensity that sometimes frightened me. I had come to the ship with a certain romantic notion of the sea—a notion I had picked up, I am ashamed to say, from books—and Vane had taken a particular pleasure, it seemed to me, in grinding it out of me. He never addressed me without a sneer in his voice, never gave an order without implying that I was unfit to execute it. And yet, in the curious way of men, I could not bring myself to hate him wholly. There was something in his isolation—for he had no confidant aboard, not even the mate, a dull, heavy fellow named Carrick—that touched a chord in me I would rather not have acknowledged. He was a man alone, and I have always found it difficult to condemn a man who is alone, however much he may deserve it.

The trouble came to a head on a night of extraordinary stillness, when the ship lay becalmed and the crew, gathered in the forecastle, began to talk in low voices of the things they had endured. I was on deck, keeping the middle watch, and I heard the murmur of their voices rising and falling like the sound of distant surf. There was a man among them named Dunmore, a big, red-bearded able seaman from the Clyde, who had a gift for words and a temper that had been tried beyond bearing. It was he who spoke first, and what he said I did not hear, but the effect upon the others was immediate. They fell silent, and then one by one they began to nod, and I saw the glint of starlight on a knife blade that was passed from hand to hand.

I went below to the cabin I shared with Carrick, and I sat there for a long time in the darkness, listening to the water lapping against the hull and thinking of what I had seen. I knew what they intended. I knew also that if I went to Vane and told him, I would be saving his life—and losing my own, for the crew would not forgive a informer, and Vane would not protect me. And if I said nothing, I would be letting them murder a man in his bed, a man I despised, a man who had made my life a misery, but a man nonetheless, with a soul to be saved or damned. The moral question, I found, was not so simple as I had imagined. It was not a matter of choosing between good and evil, but between two evils, and I could not see that either choice was better than the other.

I went on deck again before dawn. The sky in the east was growing pale, and the sea was like a sheet of hammered silver, and the ship lay motionless, her sails hanging in the dead air. Dunmore was at the wheel, and he looked at me with a certain calculation in his eyes—not hostile, but appraising, as though he were weighing what I might do. I said nothing. I went to the rail and stood there, looking out at the emptiness, and I felt the weight of the decision pressing upon me like a physical thing.

It was then that Vane came on deck. He emerged from the companionway with his usual silent tread, and he stood for a moment at the break of the poop, looking at the sky. Then he turned and saw me, and for a moment our eyes met. I have thought since that there was something in his gaze that I had not seen before—a flicker of something that might have been recognition, or might have been fear. He knew, I think, what was in the air. He was not a fool, and he had commanded men long enough to read the signs. But he said nothing. He merely nodded, a curt, dismissive nod, and turned away.

And in that moment I made my decision. I do not know whether it was the right one. I have never known. I went forward to where Dunmore stood at the wheel, and I said to him, in a voice that I tried to keep steady, that I would have no part in what they intended. He looked at me for a long moment, and then he laughed—a short, ugly laugh that had no mirth in it. "You're a fool, Mr. Hayes," he said. "A bloody fool." And he turned away.

I do not know what happened after that. I was not present at the mutiny. I had gone below to my cabin, and I lay there in the darkness, listening to the sounds above—the thud of feet, the creak of the wheel, the sudden, sharp cry that was cut off almost before it began. And then silence, a silence that seemed to go on forever. When I came on deck again, the sun was up, and the ship was moving, a light breeze filling her sails, and Vane was standing at the rail, looking out at the sea. He did not turn to look at me. He did not speak. But I knew, from the set of his shoulders and the way he held his head, that something had changed. The crew went about their work in silence, and Dunmore was nowhere to be seen. I never learned what happened to him. I never asked.

We reached the guano coast three weeks later, and I left the ship. I have not seen Vane since. But I have thought of him often, in the years that have passed, and I have wondered whether I did right. The sea, I have found, is a great solvent of moral certainties. It leaves a man with nothing but his own conscience, and that, in the end, is a poor enough guide.
