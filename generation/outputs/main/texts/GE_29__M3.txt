It is a common enough tragedy in the annals of provincial life, yet none the less heavy for those who bear it, that the very structures meant to nurture the soul—motherly devotion, clerical expectation, and the quiet routines of a country parish—may become the grinding stones that wear it to dust. Arthur Pendrell had known no other ambition than the one imposed upon him by his circumstances. For twenty years, the living of Thistledale had been held in trust for his moral and intellectual maturation; the present incumbent, the Reverend Mr. Toft, was a man of declining constitution and unyielding tenure, whose eventual departure from the pulpit had been calculated with the cold arithmetic of Christian hope by Arthur’s mother, Mrs. Pendrell, since her husband’s death left her reliant on the generosity of distant relatives. 

To speak of Mrs. Pendrell’s piety as mere calculation, however, would be an injustice to a nature whose spiritual arithmetic, though rigorous, was entirely unselfish. She had endured the slow narrowing of her domestic sphere in the market-town of Lyminge with a fortitude that drew its breath entirely from the prospect of her son’s ordination. The faded damask of their drawing-room, the carefully turned hems of her own gowns, and the meagre cuts of mutton provided by the local poulterer were not suffered as deprivations but invested as capital in the spiritual bank of Thistledale. She had prayed, with that specific intensity which marshals all the practical energies of a woman toward a single end, that Arthur might be found worthy to dispense the sacraments.

Arthur, for his part, was a young man of studious habits and a conscience that, lacking the robust elasticity of his peers, was prone to the corrosive self-scrutiny of the scholar. He had gone up to Oxford with a mind as receptive as a virgin ledger, eager to be inscribed with the orthodoxies of the Church. But the very texts meant to fortify his faith had proved his undoing. The relentless historical inquiries of the German theologians, the unflinching geology that pressed the Mosaic record into the ancient dust, and above all, the stark divergence between the apostolic ideal and the comfortable, endowment-drunk establishment he was meant to serve, had eroded the foundations of his belief. He did not lose his faith in a sudden blaze of rebellion; it leaked away through the fine fissures of intellectual inquiry, leaving him with a profound, aching void where the certainty of God had once resided.

He returned to the grey-stone rectory at Lyminge on a damp October evening, carrying his doubt like a physical weight in his chest. The autumn leaves were sodden upon the gravel path, and the ivy that clung to the parsonage walls seemed less a symbol of scholastic permanence than a parasitic grip. He entered the drawing-room, where Mrs. Pendrell sat by the fire, her hands busy with a piece of fine needlework, her spectacles pushed up onto her forehead. 

“Ah, Arthur,” she said, her face softening into a luminous welcome that made his heart contract painfully. “You are early. I did not expect the coach till the half-hour. But I am thankful. Mrs. Hucks brought up the side of pork this morning, and I have made a pudding. You will be sharp-set after your journey, I’ll be bound.”

“Thank you, Mother,” said Arthur, taking the chair opposite her. He watched the firelight play on the familiar, delicate lines of her face, feeling the full, tragic weight of what he was about to do. He knew that his confession would not merely disappoint her; it would dismantle the architecture of her existence.

For a while they spoke of the journey, of the new curate at the neighbouring parish, and of the dampness in the rectory walls. Arthur let the familiar cadences wash over him, postponing the inevitable. But conscience, that demanding creditor, would not be indefinitely deferred. 

“Mother,” he said at last, leaning forward, his hands clasped tightly between his knees. “I must speak to you of Thistledale. Of my ordination.”

Mrs. Pendrell looked up, a slight flutter of alarm in her eyes. “Yes, Arthur? Has Mr. Toft’s condition worsened? I had word he was tolerable last week.”

“It is not Mr. Toft. It is... it is myself. I find I cannot take orders.” He spoke the words with a quiet directness, stripping them of preamble, knowing that any rhetorical softening would only prolong the agony.

The needlework slipped from her lap, unheeded. She stared at him, the colour slowly receding from her cheeks, leaving her face of a waxen pallor. “Cannot take orders?” she repeated, her voice barely a whisper. “What do you mean, Arthur? You are to be ordained at Michaelmas. The Bishop expects you. The patron has been informed.”

“I cannot be ordained, Mother. I have no faith.” 

The silence that followed was of a kind that seems to press against the eardrums. Mrs. Pendrell’s breath came in short, shallow gasps. She looked at her son not with anger, but with the bewildered terror of a mariner who sees the stars by which he has navigated suddenly blotted from the sky.

“No faith?” she said at last, her voice trembling. “You have had a religious bringing-up, Arthur. You have been a pious child. You have prayed—”

“I have prayed,” he interrupted gently, “and I have studied. I have studied the Scriptures, and the history of the Church, and the evidences of our faith, and I find that I cannot, in conscience, declare before God and the congregation that I believe what I do not believe. I cannot administer the sacraments as if they were mere social rituals, nor can I stand in the pulpit and preach a doctrine that my intellect rejects. To do so would be to commit a fraud upon the very souls I was meant to guide.”

Mrs. Pendrell rose from her chair, her hands clasped against her breast. “But Arthur—the living! Thistledale! It is your inheritance. It is our only sure provision. Do you understand what you are saying? We shall be destitute. We shall be dependent on the charity of your uncle, who has no love for us. You would throw away your future, my future, on a... a scruple?”

It was the practical argument, and Arthur had expected it. “I would rather throw away my future than my soul,” he said, with a sadness that was almost gentle. “I know what this means for us, Mother. I have thought of nothing else for months. I have a little money from my fellowship. I can take pupils. We shall not starve. But I cannot be a hypocrite.”

“A scruple!” she cried, the tears now beginning to flow. “You call the loss of God’s truth a scruple! Oh, Arthur, you have been reading those German books. I knew it. I knew they were poison. They are the devil’s work, to steal the faith of young men. You must pray, Arthur. You must pray for guidance. God will restore your faith if you but ask Him. It is a trial, a trial of your faith, and you are yielding to it!”

Arthur bowed his head, feeling the full, tragic irony of the situation. His mother’s faith, which he could no longer share, was the very thing that made her suffering so acute. He could not tell her that her God was a phantom of the human imagination, that her prayers were addressed to the empty void. To do so would be an act of spiritual vandalism more cruel than his refusal to take orders.

“I have prayed, Mother,” he said. “I have prayed for faith, and it has not come. I cannot manufacture a belief that is not there. I am sorry. I am more sorry than I can say.”

Mrs. Pendrell sank back into her chair, her face buried in her hands. The fire crackled in the grate, indifferent to the small human tragedy unfolding in its light. Arthur watched her, his heart aching with a pity that was all the more bitter because it was entirely impotent. He knew that he had destroyed the one thing that had made her life endurable—the belief that her son was chosen of God, a soldier in the army of the Lord.

After a long while, she looked up. Her face was streaked with tears, but her eyes had a hard, dry brightness. “You must speak to your uncle,” she said, in a flat voice. “He will know what to do. Perhaps he can secure you a clerkship in the city. You have education. You are not without resources. But Thistledale is lost. Your father’s hopes are lost.”

Arthur reached out and took her hand. She did not resist, but neither did she return the pressure. She sat, a figure of rigid, desolate endurance, while the rain began to beat against the windows, and the darkness of the autumn evening closed in around them. He had spoken his truth, and in doing so, he had set them both free from the tyranny of an illusion, though the freedom they had gained was as cold and comfortless as the rain-soaked night.
