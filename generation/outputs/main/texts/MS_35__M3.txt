To you, who shall inherit this quiet earth when the slow centuries have buried my bones and the memory of our sorrows has faded into the dust of forgotten chronicles, I address this melancholy record. I am Elinor Vance, the sole surviving historian of the University of Ashford, a place that, in the days before the Great Pestilence shattered the pride of our species, stood as a beacon of healing and scientific exaltation. It is late in the twenty-first century, yet I write to you by the flickering illumination of a tallow candle, for the engines of light and the networks of infinite knowledge that once bound our minds together were the first victims of that catastrophic malady. I have dedicated my solitary years to compiling a true and faithful history of the Pestilence, seeking in the calm retrospect of survival to distil from our collective anguish some profound lesson, some warning, that might serve to steer your distant and happier generation away from the precipice of our folly. I believed, in my earnest and perhaps arrogant devotion, that by dissecting the past I might erect a bulwark against the future, and that the ink I spilled upon these pages would serve as a solemn antiseptic to the wounds of humanity.

The evening is uncommonly tempestuous. Without my solitary chamber, the autumnal winds howl through the ruined spires of Ashford, tearing the dead leaves from the elms and hurling them against the shuttered glass with a violence that speaks to the turmoil within my own breast. The rain falls in torrents, beating upon the desolate quads and the unyielding stone of the university library, where I now sit surrounded by the crumbling relics of a vanished world. I had been engaged in recounting the terrible summer when the Pestilence first reached our shores, describing the panic that seized the populace as the invisible destroyer moved among them, sparing neither the innocent babe in its mother’s arms nor the venerable sage in his study. I wrote of the carts bearing the dead, of the lamentations that echoed through the cobbled streets, and of the profound, suffocating solitude that descended upon the town when the survivors locked themselves away in futile terror. My heart swelled with sorrow as I recalled the fate of my own beloved mentor, Dr. Julian Hartwell, a man of unparalleled intellect and boundless benevolence, who sacrificed his own life tending to the afflicted in the lower quarters of the town. I remembered his last words to me, spoken with a failing breath but an unquenched spirit, urging me to survive and bear witness to the tragedy, so that the world might learn humility before the inscrutable forces of nature.

As I dipped my pen to continue the narrative, a sudden and unaccountable tremor seized my frame. It was not the shaking of the storm without, but an internal chill, a premonition of evil that crept through my veins like ice and caused the candle flame to flicker as if in sympathy with my dread. I paused, lifting my eyes from the parchment, and listened to the tempest. But above the roar of the wind and the beating of the rain, I heard another sound—a sound so faint, so hesitating, that it might have been mistaken for the scraping of a branch against the wall, yet which my tortured senses recognized instantly as the dry, rasping cough that had heralded the onset of the Pestilence. It came from the corridor outside my chamber, from the darkness beyond the feeble light of my candle.

Great God! Can it be? Have I been deceived by my own fears, or has the spectre of death returned to claim this ruined world? I rose from my desk, my limbs trembling beneath me, and moved towards the door. The coughing ceased, and in the silence that followed I heard the sound of laboured breathing, shallow and uneven, the unmistakable sound of a lung besieged by the same malignant fluid that had drowned the lives of millions. I threw open the door, and there, slumped against the cold stone wall of the corridor, was a young woman, her face ghastly pale in the dim light, her eyes wide with a terror that mirrored my own. She was a stranger to me, one of the scattered survivors who dwelt in the outskirts of Ashford, and she had come seeking the aid of the university, believing that here, in the seat of ancient learning, some remedy might yet be found.

“Alas!” she cried, raising a trembling hand towards me. “The sickness has returned. I have seen the purple marks upon the skin of my brother, and he is already dead. I fled, seeking help, but there is no help. The Pestilence is among us once more.”

Her voice was feeble and broken, and as she spoke, she was seized by a violent fit of coughing that brought forth a dark and ominous stain upon her lips. I caught her as she fell, bearing her into my chamber and laying her upon the narrow couch that served as my bed. My mind was a chaos of conflicting emotions—horror at the return of the Pestilence, pity for the suffering creature before me, and a profound and overwhelming despair that all my labours, all my hopes of preserving a warning for posterity, had been rendered vain in a single instant. The Pestilence had returned, and I, who had survived the first onslaught, was now to be a witness to its second coming, perhaps to fall victim to its insatiable hunger.

I gazed upon the young woman as she lay there, her breath growing shorter and more laboured, and I felt the tears course down my cheeks. What comment can I make upon this cruel repetition of our misfortunes? What words can express the depth of my anguish as I contemplate the extinction of whatever hope remained for our wretched species? The history I have written is but a futile monument, a record of a catastrophe that, far from serving as a warning, has been repeated in our own time, as if humanity were condemned to an eternal cycle of suffering and oblivion. I had thought that by dissecting the past I might control the future, but I see now that the future is not ours to command. It is a dark and unknowable sea, upon which we are tossed by forces beyond our comprehension, and against which our learning and our science are but frail and worthless bulwarks.

The storm without continues to rage, and the wind shrieks through the ruins of Ashford like the lamentations of a doomed world. I sit by the couch of the dying woman, watching the faint rise and fall of her chest, and I know that within a few hours she will be gone, claimed by the same relentless malady that took my mentor and my friends. And I know, too, that I shall likely follow her, for the Pestilence is highly contagious, and I have not spared myself in my attendance upon her. But I do not fear death. I have lived too long in solitude and sorrow to cling to a life that has offered me nothing but the bitter memory of loss. What I fear is that my work will perish with me, that these pages, upon which I have poured out my heart and my soul, will be lost or destroyed, and that no trace of our suffering will remain to warn or instruct the generations that may arise from the ashes of our world.

I must finish this record. I must complete it, even as the Pestilence closes in around me, even as the shadows of death gather upon my own threshold. I owe it to the dead, to Julian Hartwell and the millions who perished in the first catastrophe, and I owe it to you, my unseen readers, who may one day inherit this quiet earth. Perhaps it is vanity to suppose that my words will survive, that they will be found and read and understood by those who come after. Perhaps the Pestilence will consume everything, and the world will be left a silent and empty ruin, devoid of life and memory. But I must believe, I must hope, that somewhere, in some distant time, a hand will reach out and take up this manuscript, and that the story of our fall will not be entirely lost.

The candle is burning low, and the darkness presses in upon me. The young woman has ceased to breathe, and I am alone once more, alone with the dead and with the approaching terror of the Pestilence. I feel a strange sensation in my own throat, a dryness and a tickling that may be nothing, or that may be the first whisper of the sickness that is about to claim me. I will not despond. I will continue to write, to the very last, if my strength permits, and I will entrust these pages to the keeping of the stone walls of Ashford, where they may endure against the ravages of time and decay. Farewell, my distant friends. May you find in our sorrow the wisdom to avoid our fate, and may the earth, which has been so cruel to us, be kinder to you.
