The shop of Mrs. Hesketh stood in a narrow by-lane off the market-place of Bracebridge, and its single window, crusted with the grime of years and the spittle of many a rainy wind, admitted a light so chastened and so brown that within doors it was always a species of late afternoon. Here I had sat these six months, bent over my seam, and here I should sit, I supposed, until my eyes failed me or my fingers stiffened; for when a girl has neither fortune nor connexion, and has been bred upon the cold charity of an institution, the world offers her a narrow stool and a needle, and calls it a provision.

Mrs. Hesketh herself was a widow of some five-and-fifty years, small and spare of figure, with a face that had been handsome and was now merely patient. She was kind after a quiet fashion, and she was poor after a fashion that was not quiet at all, but clamorous and ceaseless; for the debts of her late husband had eaten up the shop, and the shop in its turn could scarcely be said to feed its mistress. Yet she never complained. She would sit by the fire of an evening with her spectacles pushed up among her grey curls, and speak of her girlhood in the West Country as though it were a tale she had read in a book, and not a country she had lost.

I had come to her out of the institution at Candleford, where I had been bred upon dry bread and drier doctrine, and where I had learned, among other lessons, that gratitude is a coin that must be paid down promptly, and that humility is best worn as a garment sewn without a seam, lest the world spy the joining. I was nineteen years old, and I had a pride in me that I kept locked in a cupboard of my heart, and took out only at night when the house was still.

It was in the second week of November, when the fog came up from the river and hung in the lane like a wet sheet, that the lady first came to the shop. She came in a chaise, which was a thing almost unknown in our by-lane, and the sound of the wheels brought Mrs. Hesketh to the window in a flurry, and set me to pinning my seam with a trembling hand. The lady who descended was tall, and dressed in black that was rich without being showy, and she carried her head as one who had never in her life been obliged to ask leave of anybody. She entered, and the little shop seemed to shrink about her, as a cottage parlour shrinks when a fine lady steps in.

“You are Mrs. Hesketh?” said she, in a voice that was low and clear and had a certain edge to it, like a knife wrapped in velvet. “I am told you do plain work well and honestly. I am Mrs. Ashworth of Thornleigh. I require a person of some delicacy of hand to undertake certain articles of my wardrobe, and I will not have them made in the common way by common hands.”

Mrs. Hesketh curtsied, and hoped her ladyship would be seated, and her ladyship sat, and looked about her with eyes that missed nothing—the worn boards, the poor fire, the scant basket of coal, and me in my corner with my needle.

“You have an apprentice?” said she.

“Yes, madam—Ellen. She has been with me since the spring.”

“She is young. Is she clever?”

“She is clever enough, madam, and steady.”

Mrs. Ashworth turned her eyes upon me, and I felt them go over me as a hand goes over cloth, to judge the texture. I bent my head and went on with my stitching, but my cheeks burned, and I was angry with myself for the burning.

“She has a look of something better than her station,” said the lady, coolly, as though I were not present. “That is a pity, or it is a fortune, according to the use made of it. Child, come hither.”

I rose and went, and stood before her with my hands folded, and I looked her in the face, for I had learned at Candleford that a servant’s eyes are best kept down, but I had never learned to believe it.

“You are not happy here,” said she.

“I am contented, madam,” said I.

“That is not the same thing. Contentment is a virtue for cows and curates. You have a brow that thinks, and a mouth that will not say what it thinks. I could make something of you, if I chose.”

I said nothing. Mrs. Hesketh, behind her, made a little sound in her throat, as of one who would speak and dares not.

“I want a person about me,” continued Mrs. Ashworth, “not a common servant—I have those—but a young woman of parts, who can read to me when my eyes are tired, and write my letters, and sit with me of an evening when the house is dull. I would have her taught music, and French, and such accomplishments as become a gentlewoman. In return she would be mine—mine to direct, mine to form, mine to make or mar. I am not a kind woman, child; I am a just one, which is a different metal. I do not offer you kindness. I offer you a chance.”

The word fell into the little shop like a stone into a pool, and the rings of it went out and out. I stood and felt the fire on my face, and the fog at the window, and the six months of stitching, and the years of stitching that were to come, and I knew that this was one of the moments that decide a life.

“Madam,” said I, “you are very good, but I do not know you.”

“That is a fair answer,” said she, and her lip moved, and I think it was the ghost of a smile. “You have a spirit, then. I like that better than gratitude; gratitude is a beggar’s word. But you will not decide to-day. I will come again on Friday. Think of it. And you, Mrs. Hesketh, will do well to advise her honestly, for I shall ask her, and I shall know if she has been schooled.”

She rose, and gathered her mantle about her, and went out, and the chaise rolled away, and the lane was a lane again.

That night Mrs. Hesketh and I sat long by the fire, and the widow was sorely troubled.

“Ellen,” said she, “I have no right to keep you. It is a poor life here, and you are young, and you have it in you to be something. But I have heard of Mrs. Ashworth, and they say she is a hard mistress, and that she has broken one girl already, and turned her off without a character. She is a proud woman, and she loves to have a creature about her that depends on her for every breath. It is a chance, as she says; but a chance is a thing that cuts both ways.”

“And what would you have me do?” said I.

“I would have you do what is best for yourself,” said she, “and not what is easiest for me. For if you go, I lose the best hand I have, and I am old, and I shall not find another.”

I looked at her, and her eyes were wet, and I thought of the dry bread and the drier doctrine of Candleford, and I thought of the pride in my cupboard, and I said in my heart, I will not be a beggar, and I will not be a bought thing; but I said nothing aloud, for the fire was low, and the night was cold, and I did not know my own mind.

On the Friday the chaise came again, and Mrs. Ashworth sat in the little shop, and looked at me with her steady, judging eyes.

“Well, child,” said she, “have you decided? Will you be mine?”

And I stood before her, and my heart beat hard, and I said, “Madam, I thank you, but I will not come.”

She did not frown. She looked at me a long moment, and then she said, “Why?”

“Because,” said I, “you have offered me a chance, and not a kindness, and I have had enough of chances. I would rather be poor with a friend than rich with a mistress. And because I think you do not want me for what I am, but for what you would make me; and I have a fancy to be my own, even if it is only a seamstress’s own.”

She rose, and drew on her gloves, and her face was cold, but not angry.

“You are a fool,” said she, “and you will live to know it. But you have spoken the truth to me, and that is more than most do. Keep your stool and your needle, then, and your pride, and see how far they will carry you.”

And she went out, and the chaise rolled away, and Mrs. Hesketh came and put her arms about me and wept, and I wept too, though I could not have told whether it was for sorrow or for joy. And the fog hung in the lane, and the fire was low, and I sat down again to my seam, and I thought, I am Ellen still, and I am my own.
