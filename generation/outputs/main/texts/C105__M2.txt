The autumn air in Cheshire hung heavy with the scent of decaying leaves and damp earth, a perfume that seemed to seep into the very walls of Ashworth Hall. I stood by the window of the library, watching the skeletal branches of the oak trees claw at the grey sky, when the door creaked open. It was not the heavy, deliberate footfall of Lord Ashworth, nor the brisk, sharp tap of his wife’s boots, but the soft, hesitant shuffle of Eleanor. She was eighteen, a age that should have been defined by the frivolous lightness of youth, yet she moved with the grave weight of a woman who has already buried her own future.

Eleanor did not speak as she entered. She simply approached the window, her fingers trembling as she brushed a speck of dust from the glass. She was beautiful in a way that felt fragile, like a porcelain doll that had been dropped and glued back together with too much haste. Her hair, the color of chestnut wood, was pinned up loosely, escaping in dark tendrils that framed a face pale with exhaustion. I knew I should return to my book, to maintain the strict boundaries of my station. A governess is a ghost in the household, present only to instruct, to correct, and to disappear. But the silence between us was a physical thing, pressing against my chest, and I could not turn away.

"He is coming this week," she whispered, her voice barely rising above the rustle of her skirts. "My father has received the letter."

I looked up, meeting her eyes. They were the same hazel as her mother’s, but where her mother’s eyes held a steady, calculating calm, Eleanor’s were wide with a terror that I had not seen since the cholera took her younger brother two winters prior.

"Mr. Thorne?" I asked, though I already knew the answer. Sir Julian Thorne was not a man; he was a force of nature, a wealthy industrialist from the Midlands whose age was a rumor usually estimated at somewhere past fifty. He was a man whose hands were stained with the soot of his factories and whose heart was rumored to be as hard as the iron he produced.

Eleanor nodded, her chin dipping. "He dined with us last week. Father spoke of nothing but the estate’s debts and the new wing for the stables. I felt his eyes on me the entire time. They were not the eyes of a suitor, Miss Halloway. They were the eyes of a buyer inspecting a horse."

The analogy was cruel, yet it rang with a terrible truth. I felt a surge of anger, hot and sharp, but I crushed it down. I was no one. I was a hired hand, a stranger in their lives. If I spoke, if I even suggested that I understood the horror of her plight, I would be overstepping. I would be inviting scrutiny upon myself, and perhaps, worse, upon her.

"What does your mother say?" I asked, keeping my tone neutral, flat.

"She says it is a blessing," Eleanor replied, a bitter smile twisting her lips. "She says that security is all a woman can hope for. That I am fortunate to be considered by a man of such standing. She told me to practice my walt. She said Mr. Thorne is a devoted dancer, though he moves slowly."

The cruelty of it was so mundane, so domesticated, that it made me sick. It was not a violent abduction, not a kidnapping by a shadowy figure in the night. It was a walt. It was a dinner party. It was the slow, suffocating embrace of propriety.

"I cannot dance," Eleanor said, her voice breaking slightly. "My feet are... I cannot move as she wishes. And even if I could, what would it matter? The contract is already written. Father spoke of the dowry in the hall yesterday, right after breakfast. He mentioned the interest rates. I was standing in the shadows, holding his wine, and he did not even look up."

I reached out, instinctively, to take her hand. I stopped myself just short, my fingers hovering in the air. The social contract of the household was a cage, and I was locked inside it with her. I had no power. I had no voice. I was a tutor of French and history, a provider of moral examples, not a protector. To intervene would be to destroy the delicate fabric of the household, and in doing so, I would only make things worse for Eleanor. The Ashworths were proud, and they were desperate. Any sign of rebellion from the daughter, or interference from the governess, would be met with a severity that would leave no room for escape.

"You are not a horse," I said softly. It was a small thing, a drop of water in the desert of her despair, but it was all I had.

Eleanor looked at me, her eyes shining with unshed tears. "But I am being treated as one. And I am too tired to fight. My mother looks at me as if I am a commodity that has finally found a market. My father looks at me as if I am a debt that has been settled. And Mr. Thorne... he looks at me as if I am already his. As if the wedding bells are just a formality, a noise to be endured."

The clock on the mantle ticked, a loud, rhythmic sound that marked the passage of time toward an inevitable ending. The sun had begun to set, casting long, purple shadows across the library floor. The room felt colder, the air thinner.

"What will you do?" I asked, the question slipping out before I could stop it. It was a breach of protocol, a dangerous inquiry.

Eleanor turned back to the window. Her reflection was a ghost in the glass, a pale shape against the darkening trees. "I will smile," she said. "I will wear the blue dress. I will learn the steps to the walt. I will be the perfect daughter, the perfect wife. And perhaps, if I am invisible enough, if I am small enough, the pain will not be so great. I will disappear, Miss Halloway. I will fade into the background, like the dust on this sill."

I wanted to scream. I wanted to grab her shoulders and shake her, to tell her that she was more than a dowry, more than a transaction, more than a name to be added to a ledger. I wanted to tell her that she was alive, that her mind was sharp, her spirit unbroken, and that there was a world beyond these stone walls where she could be free. But I knew the truth. The world beyond these walls was just as constrained, just as cold. There was no refuge for an eighteen-year-old girl in 1846. There was only the choice between a cage of gold and a cage of iron, and Eleanor had already been handed the keys.

"Goodnight, Eleanor," I said, my voice sounding foreign to my own ears.

She nodded, a single, jerky movement. "Goodnight, Miss Halloway."

She turned and left the room, the door clicking shut behind her with a finality that echoed in the silent library. I remained by the window, watching the darkness consume the garden. The oak trees swayed in the wind, their branches beating against the glass like hands seeking release. I realized then that my silence was not neutrality. It was complicity. I was part of the machinery that ground her down, a gear that turned without question, without mercy. I closed my book, the leather cover cold against my palm, and sat in the dark, listening to the house settle around us, waiting for the morning to bring the next step in the walt.
