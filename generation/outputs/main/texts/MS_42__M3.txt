It was in the autumn of 1833, amidst the melancholy decay of the provincial town of Blackridge, that I first became fully acquainted with the profound and tearing anguish which lay concealed beneath the placid exterior of Mrs. Eleanor Mowbray. I am a physician by profession, and my duties had long led me to the threshold of her chamber, where she, an aged woman of singular and unaffected piety, had for many weeks contended with the slow, relentless approaches of mortality. To the inhabitants of that midland district she was known as a being of almost saintly virtue; one who had passed through the trials of her long life with a serene and unblemished step, showering kindness upon the afflicted and gentle counsel upon the erring. Yet I, who watched the ebbing tide of her existence, had perceived in her fading eyes a depth of unutterable woe, a heavy and fixed shadow which the mere apprehension of death could not entirely account for; and I had often marvelled how a frame so fragile could sustain the invisible pressure of a grief so vast and so consuming.

Upon the evening of which I speak, the tempest without was loud and boisterous; the autumnal winds swept with a desolating fury through the leafless branches of the elms that surrounded her ancient dwelling, and the rain beat in heavy gusts against the casements, as if the tumultuous elements without were in dark sympathy with the agonizing throes of the departing spirit within. I had entered her chamber, expecting to find her sunk in that lethargic stupor which usually precedes dissolution; but instead, she sat upright in her bed, supported by pillows, her pale, emaciated hands clasped convulsively together, while her eyes, bright with the unnatural lustre of approaching death, were fixed upon me with an expression of wild, irrepressible appeal. The flickering light of the solitary candle cast strange, gigantic shadows upon the wainscot, imparting to the scene an air of solemn and supernatural awe. "Doctor Grayson," she murmured, in a voice tremulous yet fraught with a terrible earnestness, "draw near to me; for I feel that the hand of death is even now upon me, and I dare not present my polluted soul before the Eternal Judge with this black, unshriven guilt upon my conscience. You are a man of honour and of compassionate heart; to you alone can I confide the dreadful secret of my life, and from you alone must I claim a vow that you will execute the solemn behest I am about to impose."

I seated myself beside her, deeply moved by the impassioned energy of her manner, and I solemnly pledged my word. "Heaven bless you for those words," she continued, the tears gathering in her eyes and stealing down her furrowed cheeks. "You behold in me, Doctor Grayson, one whom the world has esteemed, one who has walked seemingly upright among her fellows; but I am a miserable, self-deceived hypocrite—a traitor to the holiest, tenderest affection that ever bound one human heart to another. For fifty years I have borne this crushing weight, masking my inward desolation beneath a calm and smiling exterior; but the agonies of this deathbed strip away the veil, and I must speak, or my spirit will be eternally damned."

She paused, as if to gather the scattered fragments of her courage, and then proceeded, her voice gradually rising into a strain of melancholy and impassioned eloquence. "In my youth, at the dawn of life, when the world was fresh and fair, and my heart overflowed with joyous sensibility, I had a friend—my sweet, my angelic Cecilia. We were nurtured together beneath the soft skies of our native valley, and our affection was of that deep and fervent nature which seems to elevate the soul above the common sordidness of earth. She was all that was noble, gentle, and exquisitely beautiful; her mind was a mirror of purity, and her deep, dark eyes beamed with a sensibility that spoke a heart alive to every fine emotion. We were inseparable; we walked together amid the primrose paths of our native woods, and poured forth our innocent confidences to the murmuring waters and the whispering winds. I loved her as my own soul; I would have shed my heart's blood to shield her from a moment's sorrow; yet I became the unconscious, yet guilty, instrument of her utter and most lamentable ruin."

Here her voice faltered, and she buried her face in her hands, while her whole frame was convulsed with suppressed sobs. At length she resumed: "There came to our town a stranger, one whose commanding stature and dazzling exterior might have won the admiration of the most wary. He was a man of the world, polished, eloquent, and possessed of a dark and fascinating power which seized upon the affections and subdued the judgment. His name was Valerius; and from the moment he beheld Cecilia, he appeared inflamed with a violent and ungovernable passion. I, in my blind and infatuated vanity, was flattered by his attentions, and mistook the artful blandishments with which he sought to secure my favour for a tender preference for myself. I was puffed up with a foolish pride, and in the delusive belief that I was the object of his attachment, I became the confidante of his pretended sighs, and led my too confiding Cecilia to believe that his visits were directed solely to me. But oh, Doctor Grayson! how shall I relate the black, the unpardonable treachery that ensued? Valerius, growing bolder in his iniquity, at length disclosed to me the true nature of his sentiments; he confessed that it was Cecilia whom he adored, and he implored me to be the bearer of his clandestine letters, to pave the way for their secret correspondence. I was stung to the quick by a venomous jealousy; a fiend seemed to enter into my breast, whispering that I might yet retain his admiration by thwarting their union. I yielded to the base temptation; I accepted the letters, but I never delivered them to Cecilia. Instead, I fabricated reports of Valerius's unworthiness; I breathed dark and malignant insinuations into her ear; I painted him as a treacherous seducer, and I intercepted and destroyed the impassioned appeals which she in her turn addressed to him. I severed their intercourse by a web of studied falsehoods, and I beheld my gentle friend droop and fade under the mysterious blight of unrequited and calumniated affection, without one generous impulse prompting me to reveal the truth."

She was now wildly agitated; her eyes flashed with a transient fire, and her voice assumed the thrilling tone of one who feels the full horrors of their own confession. "Cecilia, bereft of hope and poisoned by my slanders, at length fled from her native place, and sought an asylum in a distant and obscure village. There, secluded from the world, her health rapidly declined; the bloom forsook her cheek; the lustre faded from her eyes; and she sank into an untimely grave, the victim of my treachery and my fiendish jealousy. When too late, when the dreadful tidings of her death reached me, the scales fell from my eyes; I saw the full magnitude of my crime; I beheld myself a murderess—a being whose selfish vanity had immolated the purest, the most angelic of friends upon the unhallowed altar of my own unworthy passion. I have lived long since, it is true; I have been esteemed virtuous; I have clothed myself in the outward semblance of religion; but my life has been one long, sleepless purgatory. The memory of Cecilia has haunted me by night and by day; her pale, reproachful spectre has ever stood before me, pointing to the wounds my treachery had inflicted; and the tears I have shed, the alms I have given, the prayers I have offered, can never, never wash out the deep stain of that infernal guilt!"

She sunk back upon her pillow, exhausted by the effort of her narration, and her breath came in short and painful gasps. "And now, my friend," she continued, faintly, "I have one request to make—one solemn duty to impose upon you. Cecilia left behind her, I have been told, a child, an orphan, to inherit the bitter legacy of her mother's wrongs. Seek out that child, or the descendants of that child, wherever they may be scattered upon the face of the earth; find them in their lowliest abode; and tell them that the wretched woman who now addresses you was the author of their mother's ruin. Tell them that on her deathbed, with the terrors of eternity darkening around her, she implores their forgiveness; that she beseeches them, by the mercy of Heaven, to pardon the monstrous wrong she inflicted upon their ancestry. Say that Eleanor Mowbray, the seeming saint, was in truth a miserable, self-convicted sinner; and let my memory be held in abhorrence, if only the descendants of my injured friend may know that my latest breath was a sigh of repentance and an agonized prayer for their absolution."

The effort of speaking had exhausted her remaining strength; a mortal paleness overspread her features, and her eyes, growing dim and glassy, were turned upon me with a look of unutterable entreaty. I solemnly renewed my promise to execute her mandate; and as I did so, a faint, a scarcely perceptible smile played upon her pallid lips. She murmured something which sounded like "Cecilia, I come!" and then, with a gentle sigh, her head sunk upon her bosom, and the spirit of Eleanor Mowbray passed away. The storm without had now subsided; the winds were hushed, and the moon, breaking through the rifted clouds, shed a melancholy, unclouded light upon the calm, cold features of the dead, while I, overwhelmed with the contemplation of this tremendous revelation, remained kneeling beside the bed, a solitary witness to the mysteries of guilt, repentance, and death.
