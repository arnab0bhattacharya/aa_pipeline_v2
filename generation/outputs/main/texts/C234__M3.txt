The path to the Gasthof Silberfuchs climbed through a corridor of pines so dense that the late afternoon sun was reduced to scattered coins on the melting snow. Konrad walked ahead, his boots finding the familiar rhythm of the incline, his breath pluming in the thin, freezing air. Elias followed, watching the broad line of Konrad’s shoulders, the way his wool coat stretched across his back, and the careless, confident swing of his walking stick. They had been walking for three days, tracing the spine of the Austrian Alps from valley to valley, but Elias felt the distance between them acutely, a quiet, unspoken chasm that had existed since their university days in Vienna. 

Elias was deeply, secretly, and agonizingly in love with Konrad. It was a malady he had learned to manage, a dull ache he carried like a loose tooth he could not stop probing with his tongue. He had suggested the walking tour hoping the shared exhaustion, the shared meals, and the isolation of the mountains might bridge the gap, might make Konrad look back and finally see him. But Konrad only looked forward.

They breached the tree line just as the valley opened up, revealing the Gasthof Silberfuchs. It was a sturdy, timbered structure clinging to the side of the mountain, its dark wood heavy with snow, golden light spilling from the geranium-boxed windows into the bruising twilight. As they approached the heavy oak door, it swung open, spilling the scent of roasting meat and woodsmoke into the cold air. 

A young woman stepped out onto the stone porch, carrying a wicker basket of kindling. She wore a simple dirndl, the dark bodice laced tight over a white blouse, her hair braided and pinned in a heavy crown atop her head. She paused as the two strangers approached, her cheeks flushed from the heat of the kitchen. She had eyes the color of the deep, glacial lakes they had passed that morning, and a quiet, unassuming grace that seemed entirely native to the towering peaks around them. 

Konrad stopped dead in his tracks. Elias, walking a pace behind, nearly collided with him. 

"Grüß Gott," the young woman said softly, her voice carrying the lilting cadence of the high alpine regions. She offered a polite, fleeting smile, stepped aside to let them pass, and disappeared around the side of the inn toward the woodshed.

Konrad stood frozen on the path, his walking stick hovering an inch above the snow. He stared at the empty space where she had been, his jaw slightly slack, the vibrant color of his exertion suddenly draining from his face only to be replaced by a profound, captivated flush. Elias watched the transformation with a sinking heart. He had seen Konrad charmed by girls in Vienna, but it had always been a fleeting, superficial amusement. This was different. The air around Konrad had grown dense, heavy with an instant, crushing infatuation.

"Come, Elias," Konrad said, his voice suddenly breathless, stripped of its usual swagger. "We must secure a room."

Inside, the Gasthof was warm and low-ceilinged, the air thick with the smell of damp wool and onion soup. The innkeeper, a jovial, heavy-set man, welcomed them, but Konrad barely heard him. His eyes were scanning the room, tracking the movements of the young woman, who had returned to clear a nearby table. 

Over dinner, Elias watched Konrad fall in love. It was a spectacular, agonizing descent. Konrad, who could debate philosophy for hours and quote Rilke with effortless arrogance, was reduced to a stammering, transfixed boy. He ordered another mug of mulled wine just to have an excuse to speak to her when she brought it to the table. When she poured the dark, steaming liquid into his earthenware mug, Konrad stared at her hands—the strong, capable fingers, the faint smell of pine resin and soap that clung to her skin. 

"What is your name?" Konrad asked, his voice unusually soft, stripped of its usual performative depth.

She looked up, startled, as if she had not expected the foreign gentleman to address her directly. "Liesel," she said. 

"Liesel," Konrad repeated, tasting the syllables like a rare wine. "It is a beautiful name."

She gave a polite, confused nod, her eyes darting to the table to ensure they had everything they needed, and retreated back to the kitchen. She had not looked at Konrad with any measure of reciprocal interest, nor had she so much as glanced at Elias. To her, they were just two more wealthy, transient travelers passing through the valley, bringing coin to the inn before vanishing down the mountain. 

Elias sat across the wooden table, his own soup growing cold. He watched Konrad’s eyes follow Liesel into the shadows of the kitchen. Elias knew that look. It was the look he himself wore when he thought Konrad wasn't watching. It was the hunger of someone standing outside a warmly lit window in a snowstorm.

For the next two days, the walking tour ground to an abrupt halt. Konrad declared that the weather was turning, that the passes might be blocked, and that they should rest at the Silberfuchs. Elias knew it was a lie. The skies were a piercing, relentless blue. 

Instead of walking, they sat in the parlor. Konrad pretended to read a worn copy of a German periodical, but his eyes were perpetually lifting over the top of the pages, tracking Liesel as she went about her chores. She polished the brass fittings on the bar, swept the stone hearth, and carried trays of dark bread and smoked sausage to the other guests. She moved with an oblivious, rhythmic grace, entirely absorbed in her world of domestic duty. She never noticed Konrad’s gaze burning into her, and she certainly never noticed Elias, sitting in the corner by the window, watching Konrad with the same quiet, desperate longing.

On the second evening, Konrad attempted to bridge the gap between himself and the innkeeper's daughter. When Liesel came to clear their empty plates, Konrad caught her wrist, gently but firmly. 

"Tell me, Liesel," Konrad said, leaning back in his chair, attempting to adopt his old, confident persona, "do you not tire of this mountain? Do you not wish to see the cities? Vienna, perhaps?"

Liesel froze, her eyes dropping to where his fingers encircled her wrist. She did not blush; she did not flutter. A subtle, polite stiffness settled over her features. She gently but firmly pulled her hand free from his grasp. 

"I have everything I need here, mein Herr," she said, her voice flat and entirely devoid of the warmth Konrad was desperately seeking. She gathered the plates, balancing them with practiced ease, and walked away without a backward glance. 

Konrad watched her go, his expression a mixture of dejection and intensified desire. The rejection, polite as it was, only seemed to fan the flames of his infatuation. He spent the rest of the evening staring into the fire, muttering to Elias about the tragedy of a beautiful soul trapped in a provincial life, projecting a depth onto Liesel that she had never asked for. She was a blank canvas onto which Konrad had painted his own romantic ideals, and he was heartbroken that the canvas refused to acknowledge the artist.

Elias listened, his hands clasped tightly in his lap. He wanted to reach across the table, to shake Konrad, to scream that he was looking in the wrong direction. Elias was not a blank canvas; he was a mirror, reflecting Konrad’s own brilliance back at him, waiting, hoping for the day Konrad would turn around and see the devotion that had stood beside him for years. But Elias remained silent. He knew the geometry of unrequited love too well to believe that logic or protest could alter its angles.

On the third morning, the weather finally did turn. A heavy fog rolled up the valley, swallowing the peaks, and a wet, driving snow began to fall. The passes were genuinely closed now. 

Konrad stood by the window of their shared room, looking out at the blinding white wall of snow. His obsession with Liesel had curdled into a melancholic resignation. He had realized, perhaps, that she was immovable, a part of the mountain itself, and he was merely a visitor. 

"We will leave tomorrow, Elias," Konrad said, his voice hollow. "If the snow breaks."

Elias sat on the edge of his bed, lacing his boots. He looked at Konrad’s silhouette against the gray light of the window. He looked at the rigid set of his shoulders, the dejected tilt of his head. Elias knew that by tomorrow, Konrad would have packaged this infatuation into a bittersweet memory, a story he would tell at parties in Vienna about the beautiful, unattainable alpine girl. And they would return to their lives, the chasm between them unchanged, perhaps even widened by the phantom of Liesel.

"Yes," Elias said softly, his voice barely audible over the howling wind outside. "Tomorrow."

He finished lacing his boots and stood up. He crossed the small, dimly lit room and stood beside Konrad at the window. They stared out at the swirling snow together, two men trapped in a whiteout, each desperately in love with someone who did not see them, standing so close their shoulders almost touched, yet separated by an uncrossable, silent distance.
