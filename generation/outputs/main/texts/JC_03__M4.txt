I have known the house in Dean Street these fifteen years and more, and I daresay I shall know it until the last of its plaster cornices comes down into the area, for it has that air of shabby permanence which belongs to places where men conspire and women wait—the one commodity as inexhaustible as the other. It stands at the corner where the street bends, and its windows, being of the old-fashioned kind, small-paned and grudging of light, give back nothing to the passer-by but a dull, fishy glitter, as though the house itself had learned the trick of the informer and could watch without appearing to watch. I have stood in the shadow of the opposite doorway many a night, smoking a cigar that I did not want, and have seen the yellow blind go up in the second-floor room and the shadow of a man pass across it—a shadow only, for the man himself was careful, as men of that trade must be—and I have thought, with a certain grim satisfaction, that the shadow was mine, in a manner of speaking, bought and paid for, and that the man who cast it would no more betray me than I would betray him. That is the whole philosophy of my profession, if a policeman may be said to have one: the shadow belongs to the man who pays the gas.

But I am running ahead of my story, which begins, as such stories will, not with the shadow but with the light—with the new broom at Scotland Yard, the new Chief Constable who came to us from the North with a reputation for severity and a taste for the newspapers that I found, in a man of his cloth, almost indecent. He was a tall, sandy man with a habit of standing at the window of his room while he talked, so that one addressed his shoulder-blades and the grey oblong of the January sky, and he had a way of saying "the public" which made one feel that the public was a large, well-meaning animal that must be fed with arrests at regular hours. I had been summoned to him on a Monday morning, and I remember that the fog lay so thick upon the river that the barges were feeling their way with horns, and the sound of them came up through the closed windows like the lowing of cattle in a byre.

"Verrall," he said—for that is my name, though I have been called other things in Soho—"Verrall, I am not satisfied with the state of things in your district. There is a nest of these foreign rascals in Dean Street and thereabouts, and the public is beginning to ask why nothing is done."

I said that something was done, quietly and continuously, and that Rome was not built in a day, and he turned his sandy face upon me with a look of mild surprise, as though a chair had spoken.

"Rome," he observed, "was not built in a day, but it was built. I want an arrest, Verrall. I want a man in the dock at the Old Bailey, and I want the newspapers to have his name. I am not particular as to the name, you understand. I am particular as to the effect."

I understood him perfectly, and I went out from that room with the fog in my throat and a coldness at the back of my neck that was not altogether the January air. For I knew what he did not know, or did not choose to know: that the nest in Dean Street was my nest, that I had hatched it myself, so to speak, over a period of years, and that the man who kept it warm was named Schiavone—Emilio Schiavone—a Genoese by birth, a waiter by trade, a philosopher by temperament, and the best-paid and most faithful of all the shadows I had ever bought. He had come to me first in the year '89, a little, dusty, threadbare man with the hands of a conjurer and the eyes of a spaniel, and he had offered me, in his broken English, a piece of information about a bomb that was being made in a room in Lambeth—information which proved, upon investigation, to be entirely correct, and which resulted in the arrest of three men and the commendation of myself in the annual report. After that he had become, by degrees, my colleague, my confidant, my friend—if a policeman may use the word of a man he pays—and the arrangement between us was as simple as it was profitable: I gave him money and my silence, and he gave me the names of the men who came and went in Dean Street, the contents of their letters, the drift of their talk, the very colour of their thoughts. He was, in his own way, an artist, and I have often thought that if he had been born in another rank of life he might have been a great novelist, so fine was his sense of character and so delicate his touch upon the springs of motive.

Now I was ordered to arrest him. Or, if not him precisely, then someone; and the someone must be found in Dean Street, and the only man in Dean Street who could be arrested without bringing the whole edifice down about my ears was the man who had built it. I sat in my room at the Yard that afternoon with the gas lit and the fog pressing against the panes, and I went over the thing as a man goes over a ledger, adding and subtracting, and the sum total was always the same: Schiavone must go, or Verrall must go, and Schiavone was a foreigner and a waiter, and Verrall was an inspector with fifteen years' service and a pension in prospect. It is not a pretty sum, but it is the arithmetic of the world, and I have never known a man in my trade who could make it come out otherwise.

I went to Dean Street that evening. The fog had lifted a little and the lamps were burning with that smoky, yellow flare which they have in Soho, and the house at the corner showed its fishy glitter as it had shown it a thousand times before. Schiavone was in the little room at the back of the shop, where he kept his accounts and his secrets, and he rose when I came in with his usual courtesy, and set a chair for me, and stood waiting with his hands folded before him like a waiter in a good restaurant.

I told him, not everything, but enough. I told him that the wind had shifted, that there were new men at the Yard who wanted a sacrifice, and that the sacrifice must be found. He heard me out in silence, his spaniel's eyes fixed upon my face, and when I had done he was quiet for a long moment, and then he said, in his soft, careful English:

"And it is me, signore."

I did not answer. There was no need. He had read the ledger as well as I had, and he knew that the sum total was always the same.

"It is a strange thing," he said, after a while, and he spoke slowly, as a man speaks who is thinking of something else, "that I have lived in this street for twenty years and I have never seen the river. I have seen the fog, and the lamps, and the faces of the men who come and go, and I have heard the horns of the barges in the morning, but the river itself—never. It is a strange thing, is it not, signore?"

I said that it was a strange thing.

"And now," he said, "I think I shall not see it."

He turned away from me and stood looking at the wall, at the cheap print of the Duomo at Genoa which hung there in a frame of tarnished gilt, and I saw his shoulders lift once, as a man's shoulders lift when he draws a long breath, and then fall again. When he turned back his face was composed, and he had the air of a man who has made his peace with the arithmetic of the world.

"What is it that you wish me to do, signore?" he asked.

And I told him.
