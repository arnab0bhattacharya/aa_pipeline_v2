It was upon a Tuesday evening, in the subdued gaslight of their semi-detached villa in Selborne Road, Croydon, that Mrs. Blenkinsop imparted her verdict upon her daughter's future. The meal had progressed with that economy of speech customary to the lower-middle-class dinner table when the joint is a little overcooked and the bread is sparingly buttered. Ethel, who was nineteen and had but the previous week passed the London Matriculation with distinction in mathematics and pure science, sat opposite her brother Percy, who was eating bread and jam with the serene self-absorption of one whose fees for the ensuing engineering term at the Crystal Palace School had already been assured.

"I have been into the matter very thoroughly," said Mrs. Blenkinsop, addressing herself to the tea-urn with a sort of fortified calm. "And I find, Ethel, that there is nothing for it but that you should take the position at Crouch & Pebbler's. Miss Pilkinder was good enough to speak for you, and the cashier's place in the Manchester goods department is to be had for the asking. The salary begins at fourteen shillings, and there is a half-day on Thursdays."

Ethel had been anticipating some such communication for a fortnight, yet the directness of it took her breath away. She had been building up a fabric of argument against the eventuality, but the arguments seemed now to have fled her mind entirely. She looked at her mother, who was a small, faded woman with a capacity for immense domestic resolution, and then at Percy, who had suspended his bread and jam for a moment and was looking vaguely interested.

"You mean," said Ethel slowly, "that I am not to go on with my studies?"

"My dear," said Mrs. Blenkinsop, "you have passed your matriculation. That is a great deal more than most girls do. You have done yourself credit. But the plain fact of the matter is that your father left us very inadequately provided for, and Percy's education must come first. He will be an engineer, and in a very few years he will be in a position to do something for all of us. You must see that."

"I see," said Ethel, "that Percy's education is to cost something like forty pounds a year, and mine is to be cut off at fourteen shillings a week behind a counter."

Percy coloured. "I say, Ethel, you need not put it quite like that."

"How am I to put it? It is the fact."

Mrs. Blenkinsop set down her teacup with a little decisive click. "It is not only the fees," she said. "There is your keep, your clothes, your books. I cannot afford to have you idling at Newnham or Girton or wherever it is, while Percy is working himself into a profession. There is only so much money, and it must go where it will bear fruit. A boy's education is an investment. A girl's is—" she hesitated, and then completed the sentence with a sort of practical finality—"a luxury."

Ethel felt a sudden heat about her neck and temples. "That is illogical," she said. "If my education is an investment, it is an investment in exactly the same sense as Percy's. If I took the B.Sc. and went into teaching, or into the laboratory, I should be earning as much as Percy will earn in his first three years as an engineer. Probably more. The London County Council pays women science mistresses a hundred and fifty a year."

"Women don't get married if they become science mistresses," said Mrs. Blenkinsop, not unkindly. "At least, not as a rule."

"I don't want to get married as a rule. I want to get married as an exception, if at all."

Percy laughed, a little uncertainly. Mrs. Blenkinsop looked alarmed. This was precisely the sort of remark that confirmed her worst suspicions about the effect of higher education upon the female mind. She had been uneasy about Ethel's matriculation year for some months past. The girl had developed a habit of bringing home pamphlets about the position of women, and had been seen reading a volume by Mr. Havelock Ellis which Mrs. Blenkinsop, with the unerring instinct of the ignorant, had identified as dangerous without quite knowing why.

"You talk very wildly, Ethel," she said. "I am sure I have no wish to be a tyrant. I am only telling you what is necessary. We are not rich people. Your father—poor man—did what he could, but there was the insurance, and the mortgage on the house, and Percy's premium was already arranged before—" She stopped. She had a way of stopping short when she approached the subject of her late husband's deficiencies, as though some instinct of loyalty clamped her lips together.

Ethel, in spite of herself, felt the old compunction. It was the trick of such women as her mother to disarm opposition by an appeal to the dead. She collected herself, however. She had been reading widely of late, and had acquired a certain facility in the analysis of motive. She saw that her mother was not consciously unfair, but was merely acting upon a set of assumptions which she had never examined because she had never been compelled to examine them. This made the situation more difficult, not less, because one cannot argue with a person who does not know that the premises of the argument are in dispute.

"It comes to this," said Ethel, leaning forward with her elbows on the cloth—a posture which Mrs. Blenkinsop deplored. "You assume that Percy's career matters more than mine because Percy is a man. You do not put it that way. You put it in terms of investment and return. But the investment argument, if it means anything, means that you should invest where the return is greatest. I have passed my matriculation with distinction. Percy passed his with difficulty, and only because I coached him in mathematics for three months. I could get a scholarship. I could get a studentship. I could—"

"You might get married," interrupted Mrs. Blenkinsop, "and then all the money spent upon your education would be thrown away."

Percy, who had been listening with an air of growing discomfort, interposed. "I say, mother, that is hardly fair. Ethel's brains are her own, and if she can get a scholarship—"

"Be quiet, Percy," said his mother. "This is not your affair."

But Ethel turned upon him with a sudden flash of gratitude. "It is his affair," she said. "He is the one who benefits. He is to go on, and I am to stop. He is to have his chance, and I am to count penny rolls behind a counter. And I am to pretend that this is natural and right because he is a man and I am a woman."

She stood up. Her heart was beating very fast, and she had that sensation of intellectual clarity which sometimes comes with anger, when all the mists of habit and convention seem to burn away and one sees the bones of things, naked and uncompromising.

"I am not going to Crouch & Pebbler's," she said.

Mrs. Blenkinsop stared. "What do you mean?"

"I mean that I shall not take the position. I shall go to Mr. Tregarthen at the Polytechnic on Friday and ask him about the Whitworth Exhibition. If I can get that, it will pay for two years. If I cannot, I shall take a post as a junior mistress in a private school and earn my own fees. But I shall not stand behind a counter while Percy is being made an engineer at my expense."

Percy's face was a study in conflicting emotions. He was a good-natured boy, not without intelligence, but he had been brought up in the faith that his interests came first, and the sudden jolt of having this faith questioned was painful to him. He looked at his mother, and then at his sister, and then at the bread and jam, which seemed suddenly to have lost its savour.

Mrs. Blenkinsop, for her part, was genuinely distressed. She was not a stupid woman, but her mind moved within a narrow circle of accepted ideas, and Ethel's declaration had the effect of a stone flung into a still pond. She perceived, dimly, that something was being challenged which she had always taken for granted, and she did not know how to defend it because she had never imagined it would be attacked.

"You are very unkind," she said, and her voice trembled. "I have done my best. I have scrimped and saved. I have denied myself things that—" She stopped again. The tears were not far off. Ethel, watching her, felt the old weakness in her resolve, the old impulse to surrender and be "sensible." She fought it down. She had learned, in the last year, that the chief obstacle to women's freedom was not the hostility of men, but the emotional blackmail of women—women who had been injured and who passed on the injury, not from malice but from a sort of desperate loyalty to the system that had hurt them.

"I am not unkind, mother," she said, more gently. "I am only being honest. You have done your best for Percy. I want you to do your best for me. That is all."

Mrs. Blenkinsop was silent. She looked at her daughter across the table with an expression in which affection and bewilderment were mingled. She did not understand this creature who had sprung from her own body and who now sat there, calm and resolute, talking of scholarships and exhibitions as though such things were the natural right of women. It was, she felt, somehow against nature. And yet she could not say why it was against nature, and the inability to say why was the most troubling thing of all.

Percy cleared his throat. "I say, Ethel," he said, "if you get the Whitworth, I shall be—well, I shall be jolly glad."

Ethel looked at him for a moment, and then she smiled. It was the first smile that had crossed her face during the evening. "Thank you, Percy," she said. "That is decent of you."

She sat down again and helped herself to a piece of bread, for she was hungry. The meal continued in a silence which was not, perhaps, entirely comfortable, but which was at least thoughtful. Mrs. Blenkinsop poured herself another cup of tea with a hand that shook a little, and Ethel, watching her, thought that the hardest part of being free was not the fighting, but the having to fight against people one loved.
