Mr. Algernon Prewitt was a man who had spent the better part of his fifty years in the firm conviction that the universe was a place of admirable regularity, governed by the rise and fall of consols and the immutable laws of supply and demand. As a stockbroker of considerable standing, he found the world a tolerable enough affair, provided one kept one’s ledgers balanced and one’s emotions in a similar state of tidy equilibrium. His villa in Clapham was a monument to this philosophy, a red-brick edifice of such uncompromising respectability that the very ivy seemed to grow upon it in orderly, premeditated patterns.

It was on a Tuesday morning, amidst the fragrant steam of his coffee and the crisp rustle of the *Financial Times*, that the letter arrived. It was a singularly offensive shade of mauve, and it smelled faintly of sandalwood and some more exotic, unnameable spice. It bore no sender’s mark, only his own name, inscribed in a hand of such florid, aristocratic loops that it seemed to be dancing a saraband across the envelope.

“From a woman, I shouldn’t wonder,” said his wife, Beatrice, from the other end of the table, her voice sharp as a new pin. She was a woman who had long since traded the currency of affection for the small coin of social aspiration, and she viewed all unsolicited correspondence with the deepest suspicion. “No man writes with such a hand. It is either a begging letter or a declaration. I trust you have not been gambling, Algernon.”

“My dear Beatrice,” he replied, with the ponderous dignity of a man who has never in his life done anything remotely impulsive, “I have been gambling since nine o’clock yesterday morning, as is my profession. But I have done so in the City, with other men’s money, which is a form of gambling so respectable it is called speculation.” He opened the letter with the silver knife, a small ritual of order that pleased him.

The contents, however, were anything but orderly. They were, in fact, a bombshell wrapped in courtly Portuguese.

*My dear Sir,* it began, *it is with a heart swollen with duty and a pen dipped in the ink of long-delayed justice that I address you. You are, though you know it not, the sole surviving heir of the Most Noble House of Val-de-Lobo. The late Marquis, Dom Rodrigo de Mello e Castro, died childless in the year ’78, and the title, with its ancient estates and privileges, devolves upon you, his great-nephew, through your maternal grandmother, the Lady Inez. I have traced you through the labyrinth of English probate with diligence and discretion. The papers are in readiness. You have only to claim what is yours.*

Mr. Prewitt read it twice. Then he laughed, a dry, papery sound. “Beatrice,” he said, “it appears I am a Marquis. A Portuguese one. I shall have to learn to dance the fandango and cultivate a taste for sardines.”

Beatrice snatched the letter from his hand. Her eyes, small and pale as gooseberries, flew across the page. When she looked up, her face had undergone a remarkable transformation. The pinched disapproval of the morning had vanished, replaced by a look of such intense, calculating rapture that her husband was quite taken aback. “Algernon!” she breathed. “A Marquisate! The Marchioness of Val-de-Lobo! It has a most distinguished sound, does it not? Rather like a piece of music. I shall need new stationery at once. The Clapham address will never do. We must have a town house. Mayfair, I think. And I shall require a new seal for my letters. A wolf, you say? The Val-de-Lobo wolf. How very distinguished.”

“My dear Beatrice,” he protested, “it is an absurdity. A clerical error. Some Portuguese lawyer with a taste for the dramatic and a faulty grasp of the English family tree. I shall write and tell him so.”

“You shall do no such thing!” she cried, with a vehemence that made the teacups tremble. “You will write and thank the good man for his diligence. You will engage a lawyer. You will claim your title. Think of it, Algernon! No longer will I be merely Mrs. Prewitt, the stockbroker’s wife. I shall be a Marchioness. People will curtsey. They will ask my opinion on matters of taste. I shall be obliged to have a salon.”

He looked at her, at the fierce, almost predatory gleam in her eye, and felt a sudden chill. The letter, which he had dismissed as a piece of charming nonsense, had already begun to work its strange alchemy. It was no longer a piece of paper; it was a seed, and it had fallen upon the fertile, ambitious soil of his wife’s soul.

In the City that day, the letter was a persistent, mauve-coloured ghost at his elbow. The ticker-tape, which usually sang to him of profits and losses, seemed to whisper the strange, sibilant syllables of his new name. *Val-de-Lobo. Val-de-Lobo.* He found himself staring at the other brokers in the Exchange, a herd of sober, black-coated men, and wondering if any of them were secretly princes in exile. The idea was preposterous, of course. And yet… A Marquisate. It was not merely a title; it was a story. It was a door into a world of velvet and gold, a world far removed from the clatter of the Exchange and the dull certainties of Clapham.

He began to imagine the estates. He had never been to Portugal, but he pictured something operatic: cypress trees silhouetted against a setting sun, a crumbling Moorish castle, peasants who doffed their caps and called him ‘Senhor Marquez.’ He would wear a cloak, perhaps, and smoke a thin, dark cigar. He would have a duelling scar, or at least a romantic pallor. The thought was so deliciously at odds with his own prosaic reality that it made him smile, a small, secret smile that his colleagues, had they noticed, would have found deeply unsettling.

That evening, he found his wife in the drawing-room, surrounded by a sea of catalogues. Messrs. Doulton and Sons, purveyors of fine china; a Mr. Liberty, who promised silks of a Persian richness; and a stationer in Bond Street who specialised in crested papers. “I have decided upon a motif,” she announced, without looking up. “The wolf, of course, but rendered discreetly, in silver, upon a ground of the deepest crimson. It suggests both nobility and a certain… passion. I have also written to a genealogist. He is said to be quite the best. He will trace the line back to the Crusades, I am sure. We must have a crest for the carriage.”

“Beatrice,” he said, with a weariness that surprised him, “I have not yet replied to the letter. I have not even decided if I believe it.”

“Belief, Algernon, is a matter of will,” she replied, her voice crisp and final. “One believes what it is advantageous to believe. The question is not whether you are the Marquis, but whether you have the courage to become him. I have ordered the stationery. It is a small thing, but it is a beginning. A man is not made a Marquis by a piece of paper; he is made one by the manner in which he signs his name. You have been signing ‘Algernon Prewitt’ for fifty years. It is time you learned a new signature.”

He stood in the doorway, a solid, unremarkable man in a solid, unremarkable house, and felt the ground shift beneath him. The anonymous letter, with its scent of sandalwood and its outrageous claims, had done its work. It had not merely offered him a title; it had offered his wife a weapon, and she had seized it with a terrifying glee. He looked at the crimson fabric samples, the sketches of wolves, the order forms for crested paper, and saw the future. It was not a future of cypress trees and duelling scars. It was a future of endless, exhausting performance, a life lived upon a stage constructed by his wife’s ambition, with a chorus of curtseying strangers and the constant, gnawing fear that the curtain might at any moment be pulled down, revealing the stockbroker behind the mask.

He turned and walked softly to his study. He sat at his desk, took out a sheet of his own, plain, uncrested paper, and dipped his pen in the ink. For a long time he sat there, the blank page before him, the mauve letter at his elbow. The house was silent, save for the distant, triumphant rustle of his wife’s catalogues. He was a man who had always found his answers in the ledgers, in the neat columns of credit and debit. But there was no column for this. He could write and refuse, and live forever in the shadow of what might have been. Or he could write and accept, and become a fiction of his wife’s creation. He looked at the pen in his hand, and for the first time in his life, Algernon Prewitt felt the terrible, intoxicating freedom of a man who has absolutely no idea what to do next.
