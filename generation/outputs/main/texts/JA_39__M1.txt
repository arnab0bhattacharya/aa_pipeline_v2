The arrival of Mrs. Edmund Ashworth at Weston Park was an event of the first consequence, not only to the Ashworth family, but to the county of Northamptonshire at large. For a twelvemonth, her marriage to the eldest son had been the subject of general congratulation and private curiosity. The former was due to the improvement of the family fortunes; the latter, to the widely circulated report that Mrs. Ashworth, formerly Miss Corbin, was a woman of fashion from London, whose opinions were as decided as her style was elegant.

It was with a mixture of these sensations that the family assembled to greet her. Mr. Ashworth, a gentleman whose chief ambition was the tranquillity of his own fireside, felt a little apprehension lest the peace he so valued should be disturbed. His wife, a lady of placid disposition and unshakable good sense, was merely curious. His daughters, Catherine and Charlotte, felt that a new and brilliant era was about to dawn upon the quiet monotony of their lives.

The lady herself descended from the carriage with an air that seemed to take the measure of the house and find it wanting. Her beauty was of the striking, not the soft, kind; and her manner possessed a certain conscious superiority, as if she were a visiting sovereign graciously consenting to observe the customs of a lesser nation. The greeting she received was all that politeness could require, and the warmth of her husband’s welcome was, for a moment, almost sufficient to melt the frost of her reserve. But the moment passed.

“I am enchanted to see the country at last,” Mrs. Ashworth declared, her eyes resting for a fraction of a second on a particularly rustic arrangement of dried wheat in the hall. “Edmund has written to me so often of its… its natural charms.”

A silence ensued, which the family, with one accord, understood to be an invitation to agree. It was Catherine, the elder daughter, who rose to the occasion. “We are very fond of the country,” she said with simple sincerity. “The park is considered quite beautiful.”

“And so it must be,” Mrs. Ashworth replied, with a smile that did not reach her eyes. “One sees a great deal of green.”

Her reforming zeal did not remain long in the abstract. The first battle was engaged at dinner. The soup, a nourishing and unpretentious mutton broth, was presented in heavy silver tureens. Mrs. Ashworth took one spoonful and set down her spoon with a look of patient suffering. “How very… hearty,” she observed to her husband. “In town, one has of course moved to clear consommés. The heavy, rustic style is thought to be a little old-fashioned.”

Mr. Ashworth, who had been enjoying the soup immensely, looked suddenly crestfallen. His mother, however, continued to eat with serene enjoyment. “It is a receipt from my grandmother,” she said placidly. “She always maintained that a good broth was the foundation of a strong constitution.”

“A most practical philosophy,” Mrs. Ashworth conceded, though her tone suggested that practicality was a virtue to be exercised only in matters of finance. She then turned her attention to the conversation, attempting to introduce topics of London interest—a certain opera, a new scandal in the ton, a question of French versus Italian embroidery. She was met with polite incomprehension.

“My sister and I are fond of watercolour,” Charlotte offered, when the topic of embroidery was exhausted. “We find it a great amusement.”

“How charmingly provincial,” Mrs. Ashworth murmured, a phrase intended as a compliment but which landed with the force of a small, sharp stone.

The next morning, she descended to the breakfast room with a list of improvements already formed in her mind. The curtains in the drawing-room, she declared, were a sad shade of brown and must be exchanged for a fashionable dove-grey. The arrangement of the library was illogical and must be reorganised according to the latest bibliographical systems. The family’s morning walk, which took them through the kitchen garden, was an exercise in discomfort and must be abandoned for the more salubrious gravel path around the formal lawns.

To each of these suggestions, the Ashworths offered a resistance of such exquisite politeness that it was more effective than any open opposition. To the matter of the curtains, Mr. Ashworth replied, “They are of a very durable silk, my dear. They have never faded.”

His wife added, “And they do not show the dust from the road. A point of some consideration in the country.”

On the library, Catherine explained, “We each know where our favourite books are kept. It is a system of our own, and very convenient to us.”

And as for the walk, Mrs. Ashworth led the family to the gravel path, only to find them strolling a few paces behind, their conversation a low murmur, their faces turned towards the productive rows of cabbages and the buzzing of bees in the kitchen garden as if it were a view of the first importance. She stood alone on the smooth, sterile gravel, a fashionable figure against an indifferent landscape, and for the first time felt the profound loneliness of a missionary among the unrepentant.

The true test of wills, however, came in the drawing-room one evening. Mrs. Ashworth, wishing to display the superiority of modern taste, proposed a game of Speculation, a card game she considered vastly more intellectual than the simple whist her parents-in-law favoured.

“Speculation is a great favourite in town,” she announced. “It requires a certain quickness of mind.”

Mr. Ashworth looked at his cards for whist, which he had already dealt. “We are very comfortable with whist,” he said mildly. “It is a game in which we all know our part.”

“But the monotony of it!” Mrs. Ashworth cried, with a delicate shudder.

“There is a comfort in monotony,” his wife observed, arranging her hand. “It leaves the mind free for pleasant conversation.”

Her daughters murmured their assent. The four of them sat, a closed circle of contentment, waiting for her to join them. She was invited to take the fourth place at the whist table, her own game forgotten. She sat, a prisoner of their courtesy, and played her cards with a silent, simmering resentment. The Ashworths played with their usual placid skill, their conversation turning to the health of a neighbour’s sheep and the prospect of rain. It was a conversation of no consequence whatever, and it was conducted with the gravity of a diplomatic negotiation.

As Mrs. Ashworth laid down a card, she realised, with a sudden and unwelcome clarity, that she had been defeated. There would be no reform at Weston Park. She could change the curtains and the soup, but she could not touch the settled, unassailable good humour of the people who lived within its walls. Their politeness was not a weakness to be exploited, but a fortress of their own construction, and she was, and would always remain, outside its gates. Her husband looked at her with a fond, apologetic smile, and in that moment she understood that his allegiance, while perhaps divided, was held in a balance she could not disturb. She was a woman of fashion, but she was also a guest, and it was a role she was now obliged to play with a grace she had not known she possessed. She returned her husband’s smile, and played her card. The game, and the visit, would continue.
