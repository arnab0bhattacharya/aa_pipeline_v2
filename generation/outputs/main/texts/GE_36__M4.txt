The commission came to Edwin Marchmont in a manner calculated to stir the hopes of a man whose genius had long been kept at the door of the Academy by the accident of an unfashionable birth and a temperament too proud for supple courtship. It was Lord Ashborough himself who wrote—a brief, courteous note, in a hand that betrayed the habit of command—requesting that Mr. Marchmont would wait upon her ladyship at Ashborough House on the following Thursday, with a view to the painting of a portrait. The letter enclosed a sum that was not named but that Edwin, unfolding the cheque with trembling fingers in the shabby studio that looked out upon the chimney-pots of Bloomsbury, found to be precisely the amount he had once, in a moment of bitter jest, declared to be the price of his soul. He had laughed then; he did not laugh now, but stood a long while at the window, watching the smoke drift eastward, and thinking that the world, which had been so long in discovering him, had discovered him at last.

The truth was that Lord Ashborough was a statesman of the first magnitude, a man whose name was spoken with deference in the clubs and with something warmer than deference in the country, and whose wife, the Lady Honoria Ashborough, was the subject of that peculiar and somewhat uneasy curiosity which attaches to a woman of whom the world says that she is beautiful, that she is clever, and that she is not happy. Edwin had seen her once, at a distance, at a private view in Bond Street—a tall figure in dark velvet, standing a little apart from the chattering crowd, her face pale and composed, her eyes fixed upon a picture with an expression that he had been unable to forget. He had thought then that she looked like a woman who had been painted by a master and then hung in the wrong light.

The first sitting took place in a long drawing-room on the first floor, a room of faded amber and old gold, where the light came through tall windows that looked upon the park, and where the furniture, though rich, had the air of having been arranged by a hand that had long since ceased to care for it. Lady Ashborough received him with a courtesy that was almost cold, and took her seat in the chair he indicated with the docility of a sitter who has been painted many times and has learned to expect nothing. But when he began to arrange his colours, and to study her face with the peculiar intensity of the artist who is searching for the secret of a countenance, she said, in a voice that was low and clear and had in it a note of something like amusement: “You need not be afraid of me, Mr. Marchmont. I am not one of those women who expect to be flattered, and I have sat to better painters than you, and to worse.”

He looked up, startled, and met her eyes. They were grey, and there was in them a look of weary honesty that he had never seen in any face before. “I am not afraid of you, madam,” he said, “but I am afraid of the picture.”

“Why so?”

“Because I do not yet know what it is that your ladyship wishes me to paint.”

She was silent for a moment, and then she smiled—a smile that was not gay, but that had in it a strange and touching quality of relief, as of a woman who has been long alone and has found at last a listener. “That is the first honest thing that has been said to me in this room for ten years,” she said. “They all wish to paint me as the wife of the Prime Minister—as the hostess of Ashborough House—as the woman who has the ear of the country. And I sit and smile and look my part, and the picture is hung, and everyone says it is very like. But it is not like. It is like a mask that I have learned to wear. I am not that woman, Mr. Marchmont. I am a woman who has read too much and thought too much and felt too little—or felt too much and said too little, I am not sure which—and who is very tired of being looked at and never seen.”

Edwin laid down his palette. The light from the tall windows fell full upon her face, and he saw, for the first time, the lines that the world had not seen—the faint shadow of a disappointment that had been accepted rather than overcome, the set of the mouth that spoke of a will that had been bent to a purpose not its own. He saw, too, the beauty that was still there, but it was a beauty that had been put aside, like a dress that is too fine for the occasion. “Then I must paint what I see,” he said, “and not what the world expects.”

She looked at him with a sudden keenness. “And what do you see?”

“I see a woman who is lonely,” he said, “and who is afraid of being found out.”

The words were out before he could check them, and he felt the blood rush to his face. But Lady Ashborough did not start or frown. She sat very still, and her eyes, fixed upon him, grew slowly bright with tears that did not fall. “You are a brave man,” she said, “or a very foolish one. I do not know which. But you are the first person who has ever said that to me, and I shall not forget it. Paint what you see, Mr. Marchmont. I will sit to you as I am.”

From that day the sittings became a kind of confession. She talked to him with a frankness that she showed to no one else—not to her husband, who was too busy with the affairs of the nation to notice the shadow in her eyes; not to her friends, who were too much occupied with the affairs of society to listen to anything that was not amusing; not to her confessor, who was too much bound by the conventions of his office to understand a soul that had wandered beyond the narrow path. She talked of her girlhood in the country, of the books she had loved, of the poems she had written and burned, of the man she had loved and lost—not by death, but by the slow and terrible process of a marriage that had been arranged by others. She talked of her husband, not with bitterness, but with a kind of wondering pity, as of a man who had been so long in the habit of commanding that he had forgotten how to love. And Edwin listened, and painted, and felt his heart stir with a feeling that was not altogether the artist’s interest in his subject.

The picture grew slowly, as all true pictures do. He painted her not as the world saw her, but as she had revealed herself to him—a woman of fine intelligence and deep feeling, whose life had been a long suppression, and whose face, in repose, had the beauty of a thing that has been tried in the fire and has not been consumed. He painted the shadow of the disappointment, and the light of the courage that had survived it. He painted the loneliness, and the strange, shy trust that had come into her eyes when she looked at him. And when at last he laid down his brushes and stood back to look at what he had done, he knew that it was the best work of his life.

Then came the day when Lord Ashborough came to see it. He stood before the easel for a long time in silence, his hands clasped behind his back, his face unreadable. Edwin waited, his heart beating thickly. At last the statesman turned. “It is a fine picture, Mr. Marchmont,” he said, “and a good likeness. But it is not the picture I commissioned.”

“My lord?”

“You have painted my wife as she is—I do not deny it. But I wished her painted as she appears. The world does not want the truth about its great men’s wives. It wants the image that it has made for itself. You have given it a woman who is unhappy, and the world will not forgive you for it. Nor will I.”

Edwin was silent. He thought of the cheque that had been the price of his soul, and of the long years of poverty and neglect, and of the picture that was the best work of his life. “Then I must ask your lordship to excuse me,” he said at last. “I cannot paint the mask. I have seen the face.”

Lord Ashborough looked at him with a cold surprise. “You are a fool, Mr. Marchmont,” he said, “and you will starve. But I will not be the man to make you. Keep the cheque. The picture is yours.”

He turned and left the room, and Edwin stood alone with the portrait of Lady Ashborough, who was not happy, and who had been seen at last. He did not know whether he had done right or wrong. He only knew that he had been true to the vision that had been given him, and that in the long run, as the wise have always known, it is the vision that remains when the mask has crumbled into dust.
