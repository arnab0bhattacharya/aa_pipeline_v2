The morning-room of the Marchmonts’ house in Half Moon Street was the most perfect expression of Lady Marchmont’s character, being entirely white and gold, with a French clock that had long since forgotten its duty, and several china shepherdesses who had never known one. Into this sanctuary of refined indolence the post arrived each day upon a silver salver, and each day Lady Marchmont regarded it with the same expression of faint surprise, as though correspondence were a form of impertinence that had not been quite stamped out by civilisation.

On the particular morning in question there were three bills, an invitation to a garden party at which she had already determined to be ill, and a letter in a hand she did not know, written upon paper of a violent violet hue, which suggested either a poet or a person of no breeding, the two being, in her ladyship’s opinion, so nearly identical as to make the distinction unworthy of pursuit.

‘How very fatiguing,’ she murmured, and opened it with the ivory paper-knife that had been a gift from a Russian prince who had since been shot, a circumstance that lent the implement a certain romantic interest.

The letter was brief and breathless. Its author declared himself to be Mr. Adrian Ffoliott, whom she vaguely remembered as a young man who had recited poetry at the Bellinghams’ last winter, and who had worn a green velvet collar and looked, she thought, like a rather handsome undertaker. He wrote that he had worshipped her from the moment he had seen her descending the staircase at the Bellinghams’, that her image had never left him, that existence without her society was a desert, and that if she did not grant him a quarter of an hour’s interview that very afternoon he should most certainly put an end to himself with the small revolver he had purchased for the purpose, which he described in some detail, as though it were a pet.

Lady Marchmont laughed. It was a laugh of the lightest silver, entirely without malice, for she was not a cruel woman; she was merely a woman who had been beautiful for so long that she had come to regard the passions of others as a form of flattery that it would be ill-mannered to take seriously.

‘The poor boy,’ she said. ‘A revolver! How very middle-class. And in this weather, too, when everything is so delightfully green.’

She laid the letter on the little table beside her, and thought no more about it, and went to lunch.

It was her husband who found it. Lord Marchmont had a habit of opening his wife’s letters which he justified to himself upon the grounds of a husband’s solicitude, and to his friends upon the grounds that it saved so much time. He was a man of large and comfortable person, with a face that had been handsome in a heavy way and was now chiefly remarkable for its expression of profound and unshakable calm, the calm of a man who has never in his life been surprised by anything except the price of claret.

He read the letter twice. Then he read it a third time, and his calm was observed to suffer a slight disturbance, like the surface of a pond into which a small but determined stone has been thrown.

‘Augusta,’ he said, at dinner, ‘I found a letter of yours this morning. From a young man named Ffoliott.’

Lady Marchmont helped herself to salt. ‘Did you? He is going to shoot himself, I believe. At four o’clock. Or was it five? I have quite forgotten.’

‘He says he will kill himself if you do not see him.’

‘Yes. It is very flattering, is it not? Though I cannot think why he should choose my house as the scene of the tragedy. The carpet in the hall is new, and I am sure he would be inconsiderate enough to bleed upon it.’

Lord Marchmont laid down his fork. ‘Augusta, the boy is in earnest.’

‘My dear Edgar, nobody is ever in earnest about anything except money, and even then they are usually lying. He is twenty-two, he writes poetry, and he has bought a revolver. In a week he will have bought a bicycle and forgotten all about me. It is the way of young men. They mistake their digestions for their souls.’

‘And if he does it?’

‘Then he will have made the greatest mistake of his life, which is saying a good deal, for he has already made the mistake of wearing a green velvet collar in a Christian country.’

Lord Marchmont was silent for a moment. Then he said, in a voice that had in it something of the barrister he had never been, ‘I think I shall go and see him.’

His wife looked up with genuine surprise. ‘You? Why?’

‘Because,’ said Lord Marchmont, with the air of a man enunciating a paradox he does not entirely believe, ‘it seems to me that when a young man threatens to destroy himself over a woman, somebody ought to go and tell him that the woman is not worth it. It is the only kindness that can be done him, and it is a kindness that no woman can do, because no woman believes it.’

Lady Marchmont laughed again, but this time there was a note in it that was almost respect. ‘You are a very strange man, Edgar. I married you because you were the only man in London who did not tell me I was clever. I begin to think I was mistaken.’

‘You were not mistaken,’ he said. ‘I have never told you you were clever. I have merely observed that you are wise, which is a different thing, and considerably more inconvenient.’

He went the next morning to the address upon the violet paper, which was in a street off the King’s Road, of that genteel shabbiness which is the peculiar property of young men who are waiting for their uncles to die. Mr. Ffoliott received him in a sitting-room that smelled of tobacco and turpentine, and was indeed so overcome by the appearance of a peer of the realm upon his threshold that he dropped his cigarette and trod upon it, and then apologised to the carpet.

‘I have come,’ said Lord Marchmont, seating himself without invitation in the only chair that would bear him, ‘about my wife.’

Mr. Ffoliott turned the colour of the violet paper. ‘My lord, I—’

‘You wrote to her that you would kill yourself.’

‘I meant it, my lord. I mean it still. Life without her is—’

‘Is what? A desert, I think you said. My dear young man, I have been married to her for eleven years, and I can assure you that life with her is a good deal more like a desert than life without her, for in a desert one is at least permitted to be thirsty in peace. You are in love with an idea. The idea is charming, I grant you. But the woman is a perfectly ordinary woman who spends too much money on hats and has not read a book since she left school. You would be bored with her in a fortnight, and she would be bored with you in a week, and the only person who would derive any satisfaction from the affair would be your tailor, who is, I observe, a very good one.’

Mr. Ffoliott stared at him. ‘You are very frank, my lord.’

‘I am very fond of my wife,’ said Lord Marchmont, ‘and I have no intention of allowing her to be made ridiculous by a young man with a revolver and a bad complexion. You will not kill yourself. You will dine with us on Thursday, and you will fall in love with the Bellinghams’ governess, who is pretty and poor and will make you perfectly miserable for the rest of your life, which is what you want, though you do not know it. And you will give me your revolver, because I collect them, and because it is a very foolish thing for a poet to have about the house.’

He held out his large calm hand. Mr. Ffoliott hesitated, and then, with a look upon his face that was half shame and half a curious relief, he went to a drawer and brought out the little weapon and laid it in the peer’s palm.

‘It is not loaded,’ he said.

‘I know,’ said Lord Marchmont. ‘It never is. That is the great secret of these affairs, and the reason they are so very seldom fatal.’

He put the revolver in his pocket, and took his leave, and upon the doorstep he paused and turned.

‘Thursday,’ he said. ‘Eight o’clock. And do not wear the green collar. My wife is sensitive to colour, and I am sensitive to my wife.’

And he went home, and told her ladyship that the young man had been persuaded to postpone his tragedy indefinitely, and Lady Marchmont said that she had known it all along, and that she thought it very presumptuous of Mr. Ffoliott to suppose that anybody would trouble themselves to prevent him, and that she had a good mind to punish him by being excessively charming to him on Thursday.

‘You will be charming to him,’ said Lord Marchmont, ‘because you cannot help it, and he will be miserable, because that is what young men are for. And I shall sit between you and smoke a cigar, and reflect upon the extraordinary fact that in a world where everybody is trying to be loved, the only person who ever succeeds is the one who is not trying at all.’

‘Edgar,’ said Lady Marchmont, ‘you are becoming epigrammatic. It is a very dangerous symptom in a man of your size.’

‘It is the only symptom I have,’ he said, ‘and I intend to enjoy it.’
