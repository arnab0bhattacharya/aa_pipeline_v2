The air in the study of the Reverend Thomas Aylmer’s parsonage in Little Wychwood was not merely cool; it was sterile, preserved by a silence so thick it seemed to press against the eardrums. Outside, the October rain lashed the sycamores that bordered the churchyard, a rhythmic drumming that did nothing to soften the tension within the room. Thomas sat behind his desk, his hands folded over a stack of unopened ledgers, his knuckles white. Across from him, in the high-backed chair reserved for visitors of some consequence, sat Elara Vance. She was nineteen, the daughter of the local squire, and she had just finished the third volume of *Jane Eyre*—a fact that had prompted her father’s rather abrupt request for "spiritual guidance regarding the intellect."

They had been sitting for four minutes. In that time, Thomas had swallowed twice, adjusted his spectacles once, and felt the heat rise in a wave that started at the nape of his neck and flooded his face. He had admired Elara from the vicarage window for six years. He knew the color of her ribbons, the way she held her umbrella, and the specific cadence of her laughter when she passed the church gate. He had constructed elaborate, silent dialogues in his mind, where he was witty, charming, and brave. The reality was that he was a man who could not remember the name of the book on his desk.

"Mr. Aylmer," Elara said. Her voice was steady, but her eyes were fixed on a spot on the wall between the Bible and a framed print of the Last Supper. "My father believes that my reading habits have become... distracted. He asked you to advise me."

Thomas cleared his throat. It was a dry, rattling sound. "Indeed. It is a matter of... discernment, Miss Vance. The mind must be kept pure. It is a vessel that, if left unattended, can fill with the dregs of the world."

Elara blinked. "The dregs of the world? I thought we were discussing *Jane Eyre*."

Thomas looked down at his hands. He felt as though he were standing on the precipice of a cliff, and every word he spoke was a step backward. "Ah. Yes. The Brontë. A novel of... considerable passion. And some... moral ambiguity. One must be careful, Miss Vance. Passion is a fire that warms the hearth, but unchecked, it burns the house down."

Elara shifted in her chair. The creak of the wood was deafening. "My father says I read too much. That I am reading into things that are not there. He says I am becoming impractical."

"Impracticality is the enemy of duty," Thomas said, the words falling out of him like stones dropped into a well. He hated himself for the stiffness of his tone. He wanted to tell her that he thought she was brilliant, that he thought her mind was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen, that he would rather burn the house down with her than sit here in this icy void. But he was a clergyman, and he was in love with a woman who looked at him as if he were a piece of furniture with a pulse.

"Practicality," Elara repeated, testing the word. "Is that what you mean? That I should read only books that teach me how to manage a household or how to be obedient?"

"No," Thomas said quickly, then stopped. "I mean... that one must choose books that strengthen the soul. That do not... distract the heart from its proper course."

Elara finally looked at him. Her eyes were a startling grey, clear and unblinking. "And what is the proper course, Mr. Aylmer? For a woman of my station?"

The question hung in the air, sharp as a blade. Thomas felt a sudden, visceral desire to flee. He stood up, his chair scraping loudly against the floorboards. He needed to leave. He needed to go to the churchyard and stand in the rain and let the cold wash this humiliation away.

"I should go," he said. "It is late. You have your father to please."

Elara did not stand. She remained seated, her hands resting on her lap, her posture rigid. "You are not answering the question, Mr. Aylmer. You are hiding. Is that your advice? To hide?"

Thomas froze. He looked at her, really looked at her, and saw not the idealized figure from his daydreams, but a woman who was watching him with a precision that was almost surgical. He realized, with a jolt of terror, that she knew. She knew exactly what he was doing. She knew that he was using his authority to mask his fear.

"I am trying to be... appropriate," he whispered, the words sounding pathetic even to his own ears.

"Appropriate is a safe word," Elara said, her voice dropping to a low, dangerous murmur. "It is a wall. I have built many walls, Mr. Aylmer. My father builds them for me. The village builds them for me. And now you have come here, with your books and your silence, to build one more. Why?"

Thomas sank back into his chair. The fight had gone out of him, leaving only a hollow, aching emptiness. He looked at the rain streaking the windowpane, blurring the view of the graveyard. "Because if I do not build the wall, Miss Vance, I am afraid of what happens when it falls."

Elara stared at him for a long moment. The silence returned, but it was different now. It was no longer sterile; it was heavy with the weight of what had not been said. She reached for the book on her lap—*Jane Eyre*—and opened it to the page where she had left off.

"Jane Eyre is a woman who refuses to be hidden," she said softly. "She is passionate, yes. But she is also honest. And I am tired of being hidden, Mr. Aylmer. I am tired of being safe."

Thomas looked at her, his heart hammering against his ribs. He wanted to reach out and touch her hand, to tell her that he understood, that he was as trapped as she was, that he would rather be burnt than buried. But he was a young clergyman in Hampshire in the 1810s, and the distance between them was measured not just in inches of wood and cloth, but in the iron bars of propriety.

"You should go home, Miss Vance," he said, his voice trembling. "Your father will be waiting."

Elara closed the book. She did not look at him. "I will read it, Mr. Aylmer. I will read it to the end. And I will not apologize for it."

She stood up, smoothing the skirts of her dress with a sharp, decisive motion. She walked to the door, her steps quick and light. At the threshold, she paused and turned back. For a second, their eyes met, and in that fleeting connection, Thomas saw a spark of something fierce and alive, something that burned in the cold room like a small, defiant flame.

"Goodbye, Mr. Aylmer," she said.

"Goodbye, Miss Vance."

The door closed with a soft click. Thomas sat alone in the study, the rain still drumming against the glass. He looked at the empty chair, then at the book she had left behind on the desk. It was *Jane Eyre*. He picked it up, feeling the weight of it in his hands. It was a heavy book, filled with storms and secrets and the dangerous, beautiful act of telling the truth. He opened it to the first page, the ink still fresh, and for the first time in six years, he did not look out the window. He looked at the words, and he began to read, not as a teacher, but as a man who was finally, terrifyingly, awake.
