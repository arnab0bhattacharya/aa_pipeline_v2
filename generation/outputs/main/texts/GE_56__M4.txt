When Mr. Everard Fane quitted the shores of England in the year 1858, it was with the firm persuasion that he was shaking the dust of a narrow and ungrateful country from his feet, and that the Continent—that vast emporium of pictures, operas, and agreeable society—was henceforth to be the theatre of his existence. He was then a man of five-and-thirty, of an agreeable person, with that air of careless good-humour which is apt to be mistaken for depth of feeling, and a fortune which, if not princely, was at least sufficient to procure him the luxuries of idleness. He had travelled, he had collected, he had dined, he had made a little reputation in certain circles for a happy turn of phrase and a talent for quotation; and if his life had been somewhat desultory, he was accustomed to reflect, with a complacency that did him credit, that the most charming persons in history had been nothing if not desultory.

The cousin whom he had left behind—for it was to her that his thoughts recurred with an insistence he was pleased to call occasional—was a woman of a different stamp. Miss Honoria Fane was the daughter of a country solicitor, a man of scrupulous integrity and moderate means, who had died when she was yet a girl, leaving her to the care of a mother whose chief anxiety was that her daughter should be respectable. Honoria had been brought up in the quiet town of Ashcombe, in the shadow of a cathedral, where the days were marked by the chime of bells and the slow procession of the seasons, and where the arrival of a travelling circus was an event to be discussed for months. She had been taught to play the piano with correctness rather than expression, to read the best authors with diligence rather than delight, and to hold her tongue in the presence of her elders, which, in a young woman, was considered the flower of good breeding.

Everard, in the days when he had been a frequent visitor at his uncle's house, had found her a convenient companion for a walk, a listener patient of his anecdotes, a person to whom he might dilate on the advantages of a life spent in the pursuit of beauty, without fear of that cold criticism which the world is too apt to bestow on the man who declines to be useful. He had thought her, in his own phrase, "a dear little thing, with no nonsense about her"; and if he had sometimes detected in her silence a judgment he could not fathom, he had attributed it to the timidity of a provincial, and had been the more confirmed in his opinion that she was one of those women whom Providence has designed to admire rather than to contend with.

He had not proposed to her. To do him justice, he had never seriously contemplated so decisive a step. Marriage, in his view, was a state to be entered upon when a man had exhausted the pleasures of liberty, and he was far from believing that his own stock of those pleasures was yet run out. He had, however, permitted himself certain glances and half-sentences which might have been interpreted by a sanguine woman as symptoms of a warmer sentiment than he intended to convey; and when he had taken his departure for the Continent, he had left her, as he supposed, with a heart in which his image was enshrined, and a future in which he might, at his leisure, condescend to appear.

Ten years had passed. The Continent had been explored, the pictures collected, the operas attended. His fortune had suffered certain diminutions, not from any single act of extravagance, but from that gentle and continuous leakage which attends the habits of a man who never asks the price of anything. He had returned to London, not indeed a ruined man, but with a diminished capital and a certain weariness of spirit which he was pleased to attribute to the climate. It was in this humour that he bethought himself of his cousin.

He had heard of her, in the indirect manner in which a man hears of those whom he has neglected. She had, it seemed, published a book. Then another book. She had become, in the phrase of the newspapers, "a writer of acknowledged authority on questions affecting the social condition of women." Her name was mentioned with respect in circles where Everard's name had never been mentioned at all. She was said to live in a modest house in a quiet quarter of London, to receive a few friends of a Sunday afternoon, and to be, in her own way, a person of considerable influence.

He found himself, one afternoon in October, standing before the door of a house in a street that had the air of having been built in a more leisurely age, when men did not grudge the space for a garden or the time for a tree to grow. The servant who admitted him was elderly and respectable, and showed him into a room where books lined the walls, where a fire burned with a steady and cheerful flame, and where a woman sat writing at a table near the window.

She rose as he entered. She was dressed in black, with a plainness that suggested neither poverty nor ostentation, and her face, which had lost the bloom of youth, had gained that expression of calm intelligence which is not always to be found in the countenance of a woman who has learned to think. She looked at him for a moment without speaking, and then said, in a voice that was low and clear:

"Everard! This is an unexpected pleasure."

The words were civil, but there was in them a something—a coolness, an absence of surprise—that made him feel that he was not, as he had fondly imagined, a figure long expected and now at last arrived.

"I have been abroad," he said, with an attempt at his old manner, "and I find that London has been making a reputation for you in my absence. I could not resist the desire to see with my own eyes the author of—" He hesitated. He had not read her books.

"You are very good," she said, "to take an interest in my small labours. Pray sit down. You will find the chair comfortable; it was my father's."

He seated himself, and there was a silence, during which he was conscious of a certain awkwardness, as of a man who has come to claim a welcome to which he finds his title has lapsed.

"You are surprised, perhaps," he began, "to see me thus suddenly. I have been long away, and I am aware that I have no claim to—"

"Surprise," she interrupted, "is a sensation which I have learned to distrust. It is generally the effect of our own want of reflection rather than of any irregularity in the course of events. I am not surprised to see you, Everard. I have thought of you often."

There was no tenderness in her tone. It was the tone of a person who states a fact, and he felt, with a curious sinking of the heart, that he was being weighed in a balance he had not himself constructed.

"I have thought of you," he said, with a sudden impulse of candour, "with shame. I was a fool in those days, Honoria—a vain and careless fool. I did not know what I was losing."

She looked at him with a steady gaze, in which there was neither anger nor pity, but something that was harder to bear than either—a clear and dispassionate comprehension.

"You did not know," she said, "because you had never taken the trouble to learn. You saw in me a person who was willing to listen, and you concluded that she had nothing to say. It is the common error of men who have been much admired. They mistake silence for assent, and attention for admiration."

He winced. "You are severe."

"I am truthful," she answered. "You have come to me, Everard, because you are weary, and because you think that I, who once admired you, may be willing to admire you still. But I am not the woman you left. I have learned to do without admiration. I have found work, and in work I have found a happiness that your pleasures never gave you."

He sat silent, conscious of a strange confusion of feeling. He had expected reproaches, perhaps tears; he had prepared himself for a scene in which he should play the part of the repentant lover, and she the part of the forgiving woman. He had not expected to be analyzed, and found wanting, by a person who spoke to him as a physician speaks to a patient whose disease he has diagnosed.

"You are right," he said at last. "I came to you because I was lonely, and because I remembered—or thought I remembered—that you had once been kind to me. I did not come to ask for pity, though I suppose I hoped for it. I came—" He paused, and then said, with a smile that was not without its pathos: "I came to ask for your respect. I find that I must earn it."

She rose and walked to the window, and stood looking out at the garden, where the leaves were falling in the quiet air. When she turned again, there was in her face a gentleness that had not been there before.

"That is a better speech," she said, "than any you have made yet. It is something to have learned that respect is not a gift, but a conquest. If you are in earnest, Everard, you may find that there is work for you too, and that the world is not so narrow as you thought it. But you must not come to me for absolution. I am not a priest, and I have no indulgence to sell."

He rose and took his leave, and as he walked through the quiet streets, he was conscious of a new sensation—a sensation of humility, which was not altogether painful. He had lost a dream, but he had gained a lesson; and if the lesson was bitter, it was at least instructive. He had learned that a woman may grow in the shade, and that the man who neglects her may find, when he returns, that she has outgrown him. It is a common story, and it is one of the many proofs that Nature, however prodigal she may be of her gifts, is never wasteful of her lessons.
