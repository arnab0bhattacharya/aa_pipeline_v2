The school-room of the Cinderbridge Mechanics’ Institute—a high, bare, echoing chamber, whose plaster walls were adorned with a map of the Holy Land and a coloured print of a locomotive engine, as if to unite the two great departments of human knowledge—was wont to be filled of a winter’s evening with a very various and a very hungry congregation. There were boys who came for warmth, and boys who came for mischief; there were young women from the weaving-sheds, with shawls over their heads, who learned to write their names with a painful and a blushing industry; and there were grown men, grave and heavy, who had come late to the feast of letters, and who brought to the multiplication-table the same slow, dogged, indomitable perseverance with which they tended a furnace or drove a shuttle. The lamps—four in number, and smelling villainously of the oil that fed them—shed a yellow and uncertain light upon these countenances; the fire roared and spat in the iron grate; and the master, Mr. Ebenezer Quilp, a small, sharp, sallow man, with a black coat much rubbed at the elbows and a black cravat much frayed at the throat, sat at his high desk like a little autocrat upon a little throne, and ruled the assembly with a ferule and a catechism.

Now, this Mr. Quilp was neither a bad man nor a happy one. He had been bred a schoolmaster, and had married a wife who kept a small haberdasher’s shop in the next street, and who was the mother of six small Quilps, all with the same sharp noses and the same sallow cheeks, and all, as it seemed to him, perpetually wanting shoes. He was paid by a committee of the mill-owners—Messrs. Grind and Grist, of the Cinderbridge Cotton Mills, being the principal contributors—and it was a condition of his engagement that the instruction given in the Institute should be confined to “the useful branches”: to wit, reading, writing, arithmetic, and the Shorter Catechism. “We don’t want,” Mr. Grind had observed at the last annual meeting, with a wave of his fat hand, “to make scholars of ’em, Quilp; we want to make ’em handy. Teach ’em to count their wages and to know their duty, and you’ve done your work.” And Mr. Quilp, who had a wife and six small Quilps, had bowed and said, “Certainly, sir; quite so, sir; the useful branches, sir”—and had gone home to his tea with a strange, dull ache at the bottom of his heart, which he attributed to the dampness of the evening.

Among the pupils who most distinguished themselves in the useful branches was a certain Mr. Silas Tapper, the boiler-man at Grind and Grist’s. He was a man of forty years or thereabouts, with a broad, honest, ugly face, a stoop in the shoulders from much stooping over furnaces, and hands so blackened and so hardened by coal and iron that he handled a slate as a bear might handle a teacup. He had come to the school three winters before, unable to read the name of the street in which he lived; he could now read the Bible slowly, write a letter legibly, and cast up a column of figures with a correctness that was the admiration of the class. He was the best pupil in the school—the most punctual, the most patient, the most voracious. He had a way of sitting with his chin upon his black fist and his eyes fixed upon Mr. Quilp’s face, drinking in the lesson as a thirsty soil drinks in the rain; and when he had mastered a difficulty, he would draw a long breath, and a light would come into his dull eyes, and he would say, “Well, now, that’s a thing I never thought to know!”—which was to Mr. Quilp a reward more precious than the salary of the committee.

One bitter January evening, when the frost was on the panes and the gas was flaring in the street, and the class had been wrestling with the rule of three, Mr. Tapper remained behind after the others had clattered out, and stood before the master’s desk, turning his cap round and round in his black hands, and shifting from one foot to the other like a great schoolboy.

“Well, Tapper,” said Mr. Quilp, looking up from his papers, “what is it? You’ve done your sum, and a very good sum it was.”

“It’s not a sum, sir,” said the boiler-man, in his slow, rumbling voice. “It’s—it’s a thing I’ve been thinking on a long while, and I don’t hardly know how to say it. You see, sir, I’ve got a boy at home—my little Ben—and he’s been a-learning of a piece at the Sunday-school, and he says it to me of a night, and it’s about a—a brook, sir, and a—a primrose, and a—a daffodil, and it goes on so beautiful that I can’t make head nor tail on it, and I want to know what it means.”

“It’s poetry, Tapper,” said Mr. Quilp, with a faint smile.

“Poetry!” said the boiler-man, and his face fell. “Is it, though? I thought it was something better nor that.”

“Better!” cried Mr. Quilp. “Why, man, poetry is the best thing in the world—after your dinner.”

“Well, sir,” said Tapper, “I don’t know about that; but I know this—that when little Ben says it to me, I feel as if I was a boy again, and as if I was in the country, and as if the smoke and the clatter was all gone, and there was nothing but the green fields and the sky. And I want to read it for myself, sir. I want to be able to read it for myself. Can’t you learn me to read poetry?”

Mr. Quilp laid down his pen, and looked at the man with a strange expression—a expression of perplexity, of pity, and of a certain guilty self-reproach.

“Tapper,” said he, “I’ll lend you a book with pleasure—I’ve got a volume of Burns at home, and a bit of Milton—but I can’t teach it you. It’s against the rules. The committee—”

“The committee!” said Tapper, and his honest face darkened. “What has the committee to do with it? I pays my money—no, I don’t pay it, but the masters do, and they says it’s for our good. And I’ve learned to count my wages, and to know my duty, and I’m thankful for it. But can’t I learn a bit of poetry too? It’s not as if it was to hurt anybody. It’s not as if it was to make me a worse boiler-man.”

“My good fellow,” said Mr. Quilp, “I have no doubt of it—no doubt at all. But Mr. Grind was very particular. ‘Useful branches, Quilp,’ he said; ‘useful branches.’ And poetry, you know—poetry is not a useful branch.”

“Not useful!” said the boiler-man, and he looked at his black hands, and then at the master, with a kind of wonder. “Not useful! Why, sir, when I’ve been at the furnace all day, and my back is like to break, and my head is like a drum, and I’ve come home and little Ben has said his piece, I’ve been a different man. It’s like a drop of water to a dry plant. It’s—it’s—” and here the poor fellow, being at a loss for words, made a gesture with his cap as if he would have thrown it on the floor, but thought better of it.

Mr. Quilp sat silent for a moment. He was a poor man, and he had a wife and six small Quilps, and the committee paid him his salary quarterly, and he knew what it was to want a pair of shoes. But he was also a man, and he had been a boy, and he had once—before he had married the haberdasher—had a dream of his own, of a little school in a green village, where the children should learn to read and write and cipher, and also to sing and to wonder and to be happy. The dream had long since gone out of him, like the fire in his grate; but the ashes were there yet.

“Tapper,” said he, at last, “I’ll tell you what I’ll do. I can’t teach you poetry in the school-hours—that’s against the rules, and I must keep my place, for I’ve got a wife and children depending on me. But if you’ll come here of a Saturday evening, when the school’s shut, and bring your book, I’ll—I’ll hear you read it, and I’ll explain it to you as well as I can. It’ll be between ourselves, you understand—between ourselves.”

The boiler-man’s face brightened like a furnace-door when the draught is turned on. “Between ourselves, sir,” said he. “I’m your man. And I’ll not forget it, sir—I’ll not forget it.” And he went out into the frosty street with a lighter heart than he had carried for many a day.

But Mr. Grind, who had a way of looking in at the school of an evening, unannounced and unexpected, as if he suspected the multiplication-table of conspiracy, heard of it—how, I know not, unless the walls had ears, which in Cinderbridge they very well might have had—and the next quarter-day Mr. Quilp received a letter from the committee, in which it was observed, with many polite expressions, that “the instruction in the Institute having been strictly limited to the useful branches, the committee regret that they can no longer avail themselves of Mr. Quilp’s services.” And Mr. Quilp read the letter twice, and then put it in his pocket, and went home to his tea, and said nothing to the haberdasher; but that night, when the six small Quilps were asleep, he took down his volume of Burns from the shelf, and read “The Cotter’s Saturday Night” through from beginning to end, and then shut the book, and sat looking at the fire for a long time, with a strange, dull ache at the bottom of his heart, which he attributed to the dampness of the evening.
