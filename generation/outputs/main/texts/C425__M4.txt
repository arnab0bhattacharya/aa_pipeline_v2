The chaise had left the Bath road an hour since, and now the hedgerows of Gloucestershire pressed close on either side, their leaves heavy and dark with the last of the summer. Captain Edmund Hale, late of the 43rd Foot, sat with his back to the horses and watched the familiar country unroll, though his eyes saw it as a stranger might: the steep combs, the pale stone villages, the rookeries black against a sky the colour of pewter. He had been six years gone, and he had come home with a ball in his shoulder and a captaincy bought with blood, and he found that the fields did not care.

His father had died in March. The letter had reached him at Bordeaux, and he had read it in a tent with the rain drumming and thought, with a shame that still visited him at night, chiefly of the inconvenience. Now the inconvenience was a great stone house with its windows shuttered, and a lawyer with a portfolio, and a wedding fixed for the last day of September.

The lawyer was called Mr. Pike, and he came to the library the morning after Edmund's arrival, bringing the smell of wet wool and a leather case that he set upon the table with the delicacy of a man laying out instruments. Edmund had slept ill. He stood by the window with his coffee and listened to the reading of the will, which was short, and to the reading of the accounts, which was long.

'And the source of the income,' said Mr. Pike at last, 'is here set out in the schedule. The plantations at Bellemont, in Antigua, and the property at Halberd's Bay. Your father was a careful man, Captain Hale. He kept the two estates distinct in his books, and he would not have the one confounded with the other.'

'And what is the distinction?'

'Bellemont is sugar. Halberd's Bay is—was—the trading house. The ships.'

Edmund set down his cup. 'The ships,' he said.

'Four vessels, sir, employed in the carriage of negroes from the Guinea coast to the islands. Your father withdrew from the trade in the year nine, when the Act came in, and sold two of the ships and converted the others to the West India freight. But the capital—' Mr. Pike turned a page, and his finger travelled down a column. 'The capital was made there, sir. The house, the land, the settlements upon your sisters. It is all of a piece.'

Edmund looked out of the window at the lawn, where a gardener was scything the grass in long, even strokes, and the sound came through the glass faintly, like a clock ticking in another room.

'Does anyone know this?' he asked.

'Your father was not a man to publish his affairs. The neighbourhood knows him for a planter, which is respectable enough. The details are in the books, and the books are in this room.'

'And Miss Ellwood?'

Mr. Pike hesitated. 'Miss Ellwood's father was, I believe, acquainted with your father's interests. There was some question, years ago, of a subscription to the Sierra Leone Company, and Mr. Ellwood declined to join it, and said something at the table which was not forgotten. But whether the young lady herself—'

'She is an abolitionist,' said Edmund. 'She collects signatures. She has a pamphlet from Clarkson upon her table, and she has read it, and marked it. She will not drink sugar in her tea.'

'Ah,' said Mr. Pike.

Edmund walked to the window and stood with his hands behind his back. Six years of campaigning had taught him to make decisions quickly, and he had thought that he would make this one quickly too; but the war had been simple. You saw the ground, you saw the enemy, you chose. Here the ground was his own lawn, and the enemy was a dead man, and the woman he loved stood somewhere between them, and he could not tell whether she was his ally or the thing he must protect himself against.

He rode to Ellwood Court the next day. The house lay in a hollow beneath the beech hangers, and the last of the sun was on its chimneys, and as he came up the drive he saw Clara at the foot of the steps with a basket on her arm, cutting roses, and she straightened when she heard the horse and looked at him with a face that made him ashamed of every delay.

'Edmund,' she said. 'You are earlier than your letter.'

'The roads were dry.'

She gave him her hand, and he held it a moment too long, and she coloured and drew it away and began to talk of the roses, and of her father's gout, and of the harvest, which would be early. They walked in the garden, and she told him of the petition she was drawing up for the county, and of the meeting at Stroud, and of a pamphlet she had written herself, which she meant to send to a bookseller in London. Her voice was quick and certain, and she looked at him as she spoke, and he thought that he had never known anyone so entirely unafraid.

'You are silent,' she said at last. 'Is it the shoulder?'

'It aches in the damp. It is nothing.'

'You are not nothing,' she said. 'You have been away, and you have come back, and you sit there like a man at a court-martial. What is it?'

He looked at the roses in her basket, and at her hands, which were scratched from the thorns, and he thought: if I tell her, she will not marry me. And then he thought: if I do not tell her, she will marry a lie. And he found that the second thought was worse than the first, though it took him the length of the walk to admit it.

'Clara,' he said, 'I must tell you something of my father's affairs, and I must tell you before the wedding, and I must ask you to hear it all before you answer.'

She stopped, and turned, and looked at him steadily. 'Then tell me.'

He told her. He told her of the schedule and the ships and the capital, and he did not soften it, and he did not excuse it, and when he had done he stood before her with his hat in his hands like a tenant.

She was silent for a long while. The rooks were going over to the hanger, and the light was going, and somewhere in the house a servant was closing a shutter.

'You might have kept it from me,' she said at last.

'I might.'

'And you did not.'

'No.'

She turned and walked a few steps away from him, and stood looking at the darkening beech trees, and when she spoke her voice was not the voice of the meeting at Stroud, but something quieter and more difficult.

'I have spent three years,' she said, 'persuading people that the sin of slavery is not a thing done by monsters in the Indies, but a thing done by ordinary men in England, who drink their sugar and take their dividends and never ask. I have said it in parlours where it was not welcome. I have said it to my own father. And now I find that I am to be one of them.'

'You are not.'

'I am to be married to the profit of it. That is what the world will say, and the world will not be wrong.' She turned back to him. 'What will you do with it?'

'I do not know. Sell the estates. It is not so simple; there are debts and settlements. But I will not keep a shilling of it that I can trace to that trade, and I will say why, and I will say it aloud.'

'It will ruin you.'

'It will cost me the house, perhaps. Not the name. The name is mine to spend.'

She looked at him for a long moment, and then she did a thing he had not expected: she laughed, though it was not a merry sound.

'You are a very tiresome man, Edmund Hale,' she said. 'I had made up my mind to be angry, and you have taken the anger away from me, and now I must be something else, and I do not know what it is.' She held out her hand, and he took it, and this time she did not draw it back. 'We will be poor together, and we will be honest, and I dare say we will be very disagreeable at dinner parties.'

'Very disagreeable,' he agreed.

The moon was rising over the hanger as they walked back to the house, and the roses in her basket had begun to give up their scent to the evening, and Edmund found that the weight he had carried from Bordeaux was gone, and that what remained was only the ordinary weight of a man who has a great deal of work to do and a woman to do it with.
