I had been seated some while at my window, watching the yellow fog creep up from the river and wondering whether the afternoon would produce a client or merely a head-ache, when the door of my consulting-room was opened by my landlady with an air of some importance, and a young lady was shown in.

She was perhaps six-and-twenty, dressed in the plainest good taste, with a face that was handsome rather than pretty, and a manner which spoke of a competence that had never been paraded. Yet there was about her eyes that strained, sleepless look which I have learned to recognise as the sign of a trouble that will not wait.

“Mr. Harlow?” said she.

“I am Mr. Harlow. Be seated, madam, and tell me everything.”

“My name is Edith Rennick. My father is Mr. Josiah Rennick, of Rennick and Wace, the wool-brokers, of Coleman Street. We live at No. 9, Prince’s Terrace, Bayswater. Three days ago my father went out for his usual evening walk and did not return.”

“You have communicated with the police?”

“I have. They are civil, but they are not hopeful. They say that a man of means may vanish if he chooses, and that there is no evidence of foul play. But, Mr. Harlow, they do not know what I know.”

She opened her reticule and laid upon my table a small wash-leather bag, from which she shook out a piece of gold-quartz the size of a walnut, and a scrap of coarse canvas, frayed at the edges, upon which four letters had been painted in faded black—*J. R. M. C.*

“These came by post a fortnight since,” she said. “There was no letter, no word of explanation. My father turned the colour of ashes when he saw them. He put them in his pocket and said nothing. But from that hour he was a changed man. He bought a revolver—he who had never in his life so much as pointed a stick at a dog—and he carried it about with him, even to his office. He would not have the curtains drawn back in the library of an evening. And twice I heard him, when he thought himself alone, muttering a name that I did not know.”

“What name?”

“Carrick. He said it twice over, as a man says a thing he hates. ‘Carrick,’ and then again, ‘Carrick.’ I asked him who it was, and he looked at me as though I had struck him, and bade me never speak the word again in that house.”

I turned the canvas over in my fingers. The paint was of the kind used by teamsters for marking bales and cases; the letters were roughly formed, as though by a hand more used to the pick than the brush.

“Your father was in Australia, I think.”

She looked at me in surprise. “He was at Ballarat in the fifties. He made his fortune there and came home. But how did you know?”

“The nugget is Victorian gold, and the canvas is the canvas of the diggings. The initials, I take it, are not your father’s.”

“His are J. R. only. The other two letters, M. C., mean nothing to us.”

“They meant something to him. Now, Miss Rennick, I must ask you plainly—did your father ever speak to you of his life at the diggings?”

“Never. He would not have it mentioned. He said once that the goldfields were a hell, and that the men in them were devils, and that he thanked God he had come out of them with clean hands. And then he stopped, as though the word had been ill-chosen.”

I put a few questions as to the hour of his going out, the clothes he wore, and the direction he was wont to take; and having noted her answers, I put on my hat and drove with her to Bayswater.

Prince’s Terrace was a row of solid, stuccoed houses, comfortable and unremarkable, the very picture of a prosperity that has been honestly earned and quietly enjoyed. In the library I found the evidences of a methodical man: the papers docketed, the ledgers in their places, the inkstand filled. But upon the mantelpiece, where a man’s eye must fall a hundred times a day, stood a small photograph in a silver frame—a group of diggers, bearded and booted, standing before a tent. One figure in the group had been scratched out with a penknife, so violently that the card was torn.

“Who did that?” I asked.

“My father. I found it so a week before he vanished.”

I examined the group with my lens. The mutilated figure had stood a little apart, at the edge of the company, and there remained enough of the boot and the angle of the shoulder to show that the man had been tall and lean, with a trick of standing with his weight upon one leg. Upon the ground at his feet was a pick, and upon the pick-handle, in white paint, were four letters—the same four letters that were upon the canvas.

“Miss Rennick,” said I, “I will ask you to leave me alone in this room for half an hour.”

When she had gone I went over the library as a terrier goes over a rat-hole. Behind the books in the lowest shelf I found a japanned tin box, unlocked, and in it a bundle of old letters tied with tape, and a newspaper cutting dated Melbourne, 1858. The cutting recorded the finding of a body in a shaft at Dead Man’s Gully, near Ballarat—one Michael Carrick, a miner, believed to have been robbed and murdered by his mate, who had disappeared. The mate’s name was not given, but the initials of the dead man were, and they were M. C.

The letters were from a woman, and they were bitter. One of them, the last, contained a sentence which I have not forgotten: *“You will come back to me, Josiah, or I will follow you to the world’s end, and the world is not so wide but that I shall find you.”* It was signed *Mary Carrick.*

I had just replaced the box when I heard a step in the hall, and the servant showed in a tall, lean man of sixty, grey and worn, with the dust of the road upon his boots and a look in his eyes that I have seen in men who have walked far and seen visions. It was Rennick himself.

He started when he saw me, and his hand went to his pocket.

“Who are you?” he cried. “Has she brought you here? I will have no spies in my house!”

“Mr. Rennick,” said I, quietly, “I am here because your daughter is in great distress, and because I think you are in greater danger than you know. I have seen the nugget and the canvas. I have seen the photograph with the face cut out. And I have read the letters of Mary Carrick.”

The man’s face worked horribly. He sat down heavily, and for a moment he was silent.

“I did not kill him,” he said at last. “I have told myself so a thousand times. We quarrelled over a claim—the claim that gave me my fortune—and he came at me with a pick. I struck him down with my fists and I ran. When I came back, he was dead in the shaft. I might have raised the alarm. I did not. I was young and I was a coward, and I have been a coward ever since. I have paid the woman money—God knows I have paid her—but the money was never enough, and now the son has come.”

“The son?”

“There was a boy. He has written to me. He is in London. He says that he has the evidence that will hang me, and that he will have justice, not money. That is why I have carried the revolver. That is why I have not slept. Three nights ago I went out to meet him—to meet him at the corner of the terrace, where he said he would be—and I lost my nerve and walked the streets until morning. I have been walking ever since.”

I looked at him a long moment. “Mr. Rennick,” said I, “a man who has walked the streets for three nights with a revolver in his pocket is not a murderer. He is a frightened man who has been frightened by a rogue. Give me the letter you had from the son.”

He drew it from his breast-pocket. It was written upon cheap paper, in a hand that was not uneducated, and it demanded a meeting and threatened exposure. But the postmark was not London; it was Liverpool, and the date was five days old. The man who wrote it was not in London at all.

“He is playing upon your fear,” said I. “He dare not come near you, for he has no evidence but what you give him by your flight. Go home to your daughter, Mr. Rennick, and put the revolver in the drawer. If the son comes, let him come to me. I fancy that when he is asked to produce his proofs before a witness who is not frightened, he will discover that he has pressing business elsewhere.”

And so it proved. The young Carrick, when I found him at last in a lodging-house at Islington, was a shabby, shuffling fellow with a story that would not bear examination; and when I told him plainly that his father had died by misadventure and that his threats were a matter for the police, he thought it best to return to the country from which he came. Rennick, I am glad to say, made him an allowance, for conscience is a debt that will not be compounded; but he sleeps soundly now, and the revolver is in the drawer, and the photograph has been taken from the mantelpiece. The goldfields are a hell, as he said; but the devils in them are sometimes only men, and a man’s fear is often a worse enemy than his sin.
