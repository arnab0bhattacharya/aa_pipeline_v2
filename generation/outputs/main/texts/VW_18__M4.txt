The light came in sideways now, the way it did in late afternoon, and it was not the flat, chalky light of the morning but a softer, more golden thing, lying in long bars across the floorboards and climbing the far wall with a slowness that seemed to him almost deliberate, almost tender. It touched the edge of the blackboard, where the faint ghosts of chalk still lingered in the grain, and it touched the rows of desks, each one scored and initialled and worn smooth at the corners where boys’ hands had rested, and it fell at last upon the master himself, Mr. Aldworth, seated at his own desk at the front of the room, his hands folded before him, his spectacles pushed up onto his forehead, and the silence—the great, hollow, ringing silence of a schoolroom emptied of its boys—settling around him like dust.

Thirty-one years. He said it to himself without moving his lips, the way he had learned to say things in a room full of boys, inwardly, privately, while the surface of him remained calm and severe. Thirty-one years of mornings, of the bell clanging in the quad, of boots on the gravel, of the smell of wet wool and ink and chalk-dust and the peculiar sweetness of boys who have been running. And now the last of them had gone, clattering down the steps with their trunks and their cricket bats, their voices thinning and fading across the playing field until the gate had clicked shut and there was nothing left but this, the long room, the slanted light, and the desks.

He rose and walked among them slowly, his fingers trailing along the wood. Here was a knot, dark and whorled, like an eye. Here was a groove where someone had gouged with a compass point. And here—he stopped, bent closer—here were the initials, cut deep and inexpertly, the letters uneven, the E leaning into the A as though for support. *E. A. Ffoulkes*. Edward Ffoulkes, who had come to him in the autumn of 1908 with a stammer and a terror of Latin and a habit of chewing the collar of his jacket, and who had left, six years later, a tall, grave boy with a scholarship to Oriel and a way of holding his head that made the younger boys imitate him. Edward Ffoulkes, who had written from the Somme in the summer of 1916 a letter so cheerful, so full of jokes about the food and the mud and the incompetence of the sergeants, that Mr. Aldworth had read it twice before he understood what it was: a goodbye, carefully disguised. He had kept the letter. It was in the drawer of his desk, with the others.

He moved on. *J. P. Merriweather*. Jack Merriweather, who could not spell, who could not construe, who could not, it seemed, do anything at all except make the other boys laugh—and who had done that so well, so generously, so unstintingly, that the laughter had followed him out of the school and into the world and, presumably, into the trenches, where laughter of that particular kind must have been a kind of currency, a kind of courage. He had heard—from Merriweather’s mother, in a letter that shook in his hand—that the boy had been shot through the throat and had lived for some hours and had, the sergeant wrote, “kept the men going.” Kept them going. Mr. Aldworth stood very still, his hand flat on the desk, and felt the old, familiar pressure behind his eyes, the pressure he had learned to press back down, to swallow, to convert into something useful—a sharp word, a Latin conjugation, the brisk tap of his ruler on the desk.

The sun had moved. The bar of light had climbed higher on the wall and was now touching the map of Europe that hung there, faded and curling at the edges, with its borders drawn in a hand that was no longer accurate. He had taught from that map for twenty years. He had traced the Rhine, the Danube, the Vistula, with the tip of his pointer, and boys had copied the rivers into their notebooks, and none of them—none of them, he thought—had known that they would one day cross those rivers in the dark, under fire, with the water rising to their waists.

He sat down again at his desk and opened the drawer. The letters were there, bundled with string, and he did not untie them. He knew them too well. Instead he took out the register, the long, leather-bound book in which he had recorded, term after term, the names of the boys who had passed through his hands, and he opened it at random and let his eyes fall where they would.

*Pargeter, R. W.* Pargeter. A thin boy with a permanent cold, always sniffling, always producing a handkerchief of dubious cleanliness from his sleeve. What had become of Pargeter? He could not remember. The name was there, in his own careful handwriting, and the dates, and the form, and the house, and beyond that—nothing. A blank. He turned the page. *Quennell, A. H.* Quennell. Fair-haired, he thought. Or perhaps not. Perhaps that was Quennell the elder, who had gone into the church, or Quennell the younger, who had gone into his father’s business in Leeds. Or perhaps neither. Perhaps he was confusing them with the Quigleys, who had been at the school in the nineties, before his time. He turned another page, and another, and the names blurred and ran together, and he was seized with a sudden, vertiginous sense of the sheer number of them, the hundreds, the thousands of boys who had sat in these desks and looked up at him and waited to be taught, and whom he had taught, or failed to teach, or forgotten, or half-remembered, or remembered only as a face, a voice, a trick of the hands, a particular way of slouching in a chair.

*Sillitoe, M. C.* That one he remembered. Sillitoe, who had been caught smoking behind the pavilion and had wept, not from fear of punishment but from shame, and who had gone on to Sandhurst and then to France and then—his mother had written—to a cemetery near Ypres, where the poppies grew, she said, in such abundance that the graves were almost hidden. *Trelawney, J.* Trelawney, who had been killed in the first month. *Underwood, P. R.* Underwood, who had survived, who had written to him only last Christmas, a card with a picture of a robin and a few lines in a hand that shook—the shell-shock, he supposed, though Underwood had not said so—saying that he thought often of the school and of Mr. Aldworth’s Latin lessons and that he hoped the old man was well.

The old man. He smiled at that, a little wryly, and closed the register. The light was going now, the gold turning to grey, the shadows lengthening across the floor. Outside, in the quad, a blackbird was singing, and the sound of it—clear, liquid, indifferent—seemed to him the very voice of the evening, of all the evenings he had spent in this room, of all the evenings that were still to come, when he would not be here, when another master would sit at this desk and open this drawer and find—what? Perhaps nothing. Perhaps the letters would have been burned, or lost, or given to some museum of the war, and the desks would be sanded down and revarnished, and the initials would be gone, and the boys would be gone, and the whole thing—the whole long, patient, hopeful, heartbreaking enterprise—would be as though it had never been.

He stood up and put on his coat. At the door he turned and looked back. The room was almost dark now, the desks receding into shadow, the blackboard a deeper darkness on the wall. He thought of all the boys who had sat there, the living and the dead, the remembered and the forgotten, and he raised his hand—half in farewell, half in blessing—and then he stepped out into the corridor and closed the door behind him.
