There are few things so wearing to the soul as the incessant verification of one’s own perfection. In the Court of Valdoria, where the very tapestries were woven to reflect the light upon her cheek, the Princess Amaryllis had been told, since she was old enough to comprehend the flattery of sycophants, that she was the most beautiful girl in the world. It was a compliment of such monotonous frequency that it had long ago ceased to be a pleasure and had become, instead, a heavy and tiresome burden.

"Beauty," she remarked one morning to her lady-in-waiting, the Countess Valeria, as she surveyed herself in a mirror of polished silver, "is a currency that is spent the moment one is born. I have been bankrupt of novelty for years."

"My dear Princess," Valeria replied, adjusting a sapphire pin in Amaryllis’s hair, "you possess a beauty that is timeless. It is not a novelty; it is an absolute."

"Absolutes are the refuge of the unimaginative," Amaryllis sighed. "I would prefer to be a delightful uncertainty. I am going to the market."

Valeria looked horrified. "The market! That riot of garlic and gossip? Your Highness will be mobbed."

"On the contrary," said Amaryllis, rising and casting aside her gown of white satin for a cloak of rough grey wool, "I shall be entirely invisible. If I am indeed the most beautiful girl in the world, surely I cannot be recognized if I dress like a washerwoman. It would be the ultimate test of the theory."

She tied a scarf over her golden hair and smudged her cheek with a pinch of soot from the hearth. The disguise was, to her mind, a masterpiece of aesthetic negation. She descended into the city of Osteria, where the market square was a riotous mosaic of color and sound.

The air was thick with the scent of bruised mint and roasting chestnuts. Amaryllis walked through the throng, waiting for the gasp of recognition, the sudden hush of awe that usually followed her presence like a shadow. But no one looked twice. A butcher waved a cleaver at her to get out of the way; a boy selling ribbons offered her a faded blue one with the indifference one shows to a stray dog.

It was humiliating. It was also exhilarating. To be ordinary was a liberation she had never tasted. She drifted toward the flower stalls, where the riot of nature seemed to mock the stiff arrangements of the palace gardens. There, beneath a canvas awning that was frayed at the edges, sat an old woman.

She was wrapped in shawls of faded lilac and grey, her face a map of fine lines, and her eyes were clouded with the milky whiteness of blindness. She sat behind a low table covered in pots of violets and tuberoses.

Amaryllis paused, picking up a tuberose. "These are not very straight," she observed, her voice slipping, despite herself, into the haughty cadence of the court. "And the petals are bruised. In the palace, we discard anything that is not symmetrical."

The blind woman did not look up, but her hands, gnarled as oak roots, continued to arrange a bunch of anemones. "Symmetry is for those who have nothing else to offer," the woman said, her voice like dry leaves skittering on stone. "Perfection is a bore. It is the flaw that makes the flower interesting, just as it is the sorrow that makes the song."

Amaryllis was taken aback. "You speak strangely for a flower seller."

"I sell flowers to those who can see," the woman replied. "But I listen to those who can be heard. You have a lovely voice, child. Like honey poured over broken glass."

Amaryllis preened instinctively. "I have been trained by the finest masters in Valdoria. My recitation of the epics is said to be—"

"I did not say you recited well," the woman interrupted gently. "I said you had a lovely voice. And, if I am not mistaken, you sing beautifully."

Amaryllis froze. "Sing? I do not sing in public. It is considered... undignified for a person of my station."

"Station is merely the cage we build for ourselves," the woman murmured. "But your song... I heard it just now, when you stepped into my stall. It was not with your throat, but with your spirit. It is a sad song, but a very pretty one."

"I have no idea what you mean," Amaryllis said, clutching the tuberose tighter.

"Let me tell you a story," the woman said, leaning forward. "There was a lark who was told she was the most beautiful bird in the sky. Her feathers were gold, and her wings were like the wind. But she was unhappy, because she wanted to know if she could fly without her golden feathers. So she plucked them out, one by one, until she was naked and ugly. And then she flew, and she sang, and the world listened, not because she was gold, but because she was free."

The blind woman reached out and touched Amaryllis’s hand. Her fingers were cool and dry. "You are afraid," the woman whispered. "You are afraid that if you are not beautiful, you are nothing. But you are not nothing, child. You are a song."

Amaryllis felt a strange sensation in her chest, a tightness that threatened to dissolve into tears. She had been praised for her eyes, her hair, the curve of her neck, the whiteness of her teeth. She had been praised for the way light fell upon her. But she had never been praised for the sound of her soul.

"Thank you," Amaryllis whispered, the words feeling clumsy and insufficient.

"Take the tuberose," the woman said. "It is imperfect, but its scent is intoxicating. It will remind you that beauty is not what is seen, but what is felt."

Amaryllis placed a gold coin on the table—far too much, but she felt the need to give something of value.

"Keep your gold," the woman said, waving a hand dismissively. "Gold is heavy. I prefer the sound of your gratitude."

Amaryllis left the market stall in a daze. She walked back toward the palace, the grey cloak suddenly feeling like a costume rather than a disguise. The city was no longer a backdrop for her beauty; it was a living, breathing entity, full of noise and imperfection and life.

When she reached the palace gates, the guards bowed low. "Your Highness," the captain said, his eyes widening as he recognized her beneath the soot. "We thought you were lost!"

"I was," Amaryllis said, stepping into the cool marble hall. "But I have found my way."

She went to her chambers and stood before the great silver mirror. She looked at her reflection—the flawless skin, the symmetrical features, the legendary beauty that had defined her existence. She waited for the usual swell of vanity, the confirmation of her supremacy.

But it did not come.

Instead, she thought of the blind woman’s words. *You sing beautifully.*

It was a paradox that delighted her. She had gone to the market to discover the truth of her face, and had returned with the truth of her voice. She was pleased, yes, but it was not the pleasure of vanity. It was the pleasure of being known. She picked up the tuberose, bruised and crooked as it was, and held it to her nose. It smelled of rain and earth and something wild.

"You are the most beautiful girl in the world," she said to the mirror.

And then, for the first time in her life, she laughed. It was a sound that had nothing to do with beauty, and everything to do with joy. She began to hum, a low, tuneless melody that rose and fell, filling the room with a sound that was entirely her own. It was not a song fit for a court, nor a melody for a queen. It was the song of a girl who was, at last, imperfectly, wonderfully, alive.
