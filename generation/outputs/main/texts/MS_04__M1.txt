It is now a year, a long and desolate year, since the sea, that which I once thought the emblem of our eternal love, gave up its dead and returned to me only the cold, lifeless form of my husband. The waves which had whispered promises of our future with their gentle murmur, the same waves that had borne us upon their sun-dappled crests in an ecstasy of shared joy, became his shroud and his executioner. In the space of a single hour, the vast and beautiful expanse of water that had been the theatre of my happiness was transformed into my eternal tormentor, its ceaseless motion a cruel mockery of the stillness that had fallen upon my heart. Since that dreadful day, I have sought refuge in this solitary cottage, perched upon the cliffs of Devon, where the wind and the sea are my only companions. I desired only to be left alone with my grief, to commune with the elements that had stolen my life from me, and to let the salt spray and the howling gales erode my being as they have the very rocks upon which this humble dwelling stands.

I have forbidden all company, turning from the pitying eyes and well-meaning but hollow consolations of my neighbours as one would turn from a physical blow. Their words are but empty sounds, their sympathy a shallow imitation of a feeling they cannot comprehend. How can they speak of time’s healing balm, when for me, every tick of the clock is but a reminder of the moments I have lived since he ceased to exist? How can they speak of God’s inscrutable will, when the heavens themselves seem to weep in furious protest at the brutal indifference of the world? I have locked the world from my door, and in the profound and crushing silence of my solitude, I have found a strange and terrible comfort. Here, I am not the object of pity, but a creature of the storm, my soul as bleak and as tempestuous as the horizon I survey. The cottage, with its salt-stained windows and the perpetual damp that clings to its stone walls, is my tomb and my sanctuary, a place where my sorrow is not an affliction to be cured, but a state of being to be endured.

It was in the deepest part of my self-imposed exile, some months after my arrival, that I first noticed the intrusion. Upon my doorstep, which is swept clean each morning of the grit and seaweed that the gales deposit there, lay a small, delicate posy of wild flowers. There were sea-pinks, their tiny heads nodding bravely against the wind, and the hardy, purple thrift that grows in the very cracks of the cliff-face, and a sprig of heather, its bells still heavy with dew. I was at first struck by a surge of irrational anger. Who dared to violate my solitude with this fragile, living thing? It was a mockery of the life that had been so brutally taken. I swept the offering into the churning grey water below the cliff and watched the waves scatter its petals as they had scattered my hopes.

But the next morning, and the morning after, another bouquet appeared. It was always different, always composed of the humble, resilient flora of the coast, yet always possessing a simple, unadorned beauty that seemed to accuse my own barren desolation. I began to watch from my window, hiding behind the thick curtain, my heart a frantic drum against my ribs. Then, one misty dawn, I saw her. It was a child, a girl of no more than fifteen, with the sharp, pale features of those who live by the sea and hair the colour of dried seaweed. I knew her by sight; she was the daughter of the fisherman whose cottage lies further down the cove, the one whose own mother was claimed by the waves in a storm the winter before mine. She moved with a shy, hesitant grace, her bare feet silent on the damp stone, her eyes fixed upon my door with an expression I could not decipher. She laid the flowers down and, with a quick, fearful glance at the silent cottage, fled like a startled seabird.

I felt a strange conflict within my breast, a war between the bitter solace of my isolation and a sudden, unwelcome pang of something akin to curiosity. This child, this creature of the same unforgiving sea that had widowed me, was she offering pity? Or was it something else, a silent communion of shared loss, a recognition of the deep and abiding wound that the ocean leaves upon the soul? For weeks I left the flowers to perish where they were laid, their slow decay a mirror to my own. But the girl came on, a relentless, gentle tide of silent sympathy. Her persistence was not an assault, but a quiet, steadfast offering, a small light held up against my immense and raging darkness. It began to work upon me, not with force, but with the slow, insidious power of a single drop of water wearing away at stone.

One evening, as a furious storm, the likes of which had not been seen since the night of my husband’s death, lashed the coast with all its fury, I saw her again. The rain fell in grey, driving sheets, and the wind howled like a great, wounded beast. Yet there she was, battling against the gale, a small bundle of thrift clutched to her chest. The wind tore at her, and she stumbled, nearly falling upon the slick, treacherous path. A sudden, overwhelming impulse seized me—an impulse born not of thought, but of a primal, human instinct I had thought long dead within me. I threw open the heavy oak door, the wind rushing in to snatch the very breath from my lungs.

“Child!” I cried, my voice sounding raw and strange even to my own ears, torn away by the gale. “Come in from the storm! You will be swept from the cliff!”

She froze, her eyes wide with a terror that was not of the storm, but of me. For a long moment, we stood thus, she a fragile figure against the elemental rage, I a ghost in the doorway of my sepulchre. Then, slowly, hesitantly, she took a step forward, and then another, until she stood upon my threshold, dripping and shivering, the wild flowers still clutched in her small, cold hand. As she passed me to enter the cottage, I felt a shudder that was not from the cold, and I knew with a certainty that struck me to my very soul, that the world, in its cruel and beautiful design, had sent me not a comforter, but a test, and that my long and sacred sorrow was about to be challenged by the quiet, persistent, and utterly unwelcome miracle of a shared humanity.
