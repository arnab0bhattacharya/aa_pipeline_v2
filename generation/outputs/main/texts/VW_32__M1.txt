The light in Andalusia was not the soft, diffused grey of England; it was a substance, a white gold that pressed upon the eyelids and bleached the colour from the very stones of the terrace. Miss Pemberton sat with the morning’s post laid out before her—three letters for Mrs. Ashcombe, heavy with English stamps, and a single, thin envelope for herself, which she did not open, merely turning it over and over until the paper grew warm and pliable in her fingers.

It was the silence that struck one first, or rather, the absence of the particular silence she had known at home, where silence was merely a pause between the sounds of the wind or the distant rumble of a cart. Here, the silence was filled with a low, vibrating hum of heat, a drone of insects that seemed to rise from the very earth itself. Mrs. Ashcombe was upstairs, resting through the heat of the day, the shutters drawn, her mind presumably occupied with the dismal state of the Yorkshire moors or the health of her sister, a state of being so entirely English that it seemed to float like a bubble of soap above the heavy, red-tiled roof of the villa.

But for Eleanor Pemberton, the world had shifted. She looked out over the valley, where the olive trees stood in silver-grey clouds, their leaves trembling with a metallic light. She had been here three months now. Three months since the train had shuddered to a halt in the dust, and the Ashcombes had looked out with that particular expression of baffled dismay that the English wear when confronted with the raw, unvarnished face of the continent. They had required her then as a tool, a key to unlock the doors of this strange house and to scold the servants in a language that sounded to them like the rattling of dry bones.

Yet, as the weeks had passed, the key had turned into something else. She felt it now as she descended the steps into the garden, the dry earth crunching beneath her thin soles. She was not merely translating; she was existing in the space between. When old Isabella, the cook, brought out the bread and the oil, her face a map of deep lines and sun-weathered patience, she did not look at Eleanor as she looked at the mistress. With the mistress, Isabella’s eyes were guarded, her mouth a tight line of submission and secret amusement. But with Eleanor, her gaze softened; she spoke of her son in Granada, of the price of flour, of the saints and the festivals, her hands moving in rhythmic, expressive gestures that required no translation at all.

"Eleanor!" The voice came from the upper window, sharp and distinct as a pebble thrown into still water. "Eleanor, have you seen the blue shawl?"

Eleanor looked up. The shutter was thrown back, and Mrs. Ashcombe’s face, pale and framed by the dark interior, hovered like a ghost against the blinding sky. "I believe it is in the cedar chest, Mrs. Ashcombe. I shall look for it directly."

"Please do. And tell them—tell the woman—I want tea at four. Not five. Four."

The command hung in the air. Eleanor felt a curious sense of duality. She was Mrs. Ashcombe’s voice, her ears, her hands; she was the buffer between the English will and the Spanish reality. Yet, as she turned back to the garden, the sensation of subordination fell away like a heavy cloak. The gardener, Miguel, was raking the gravel path, his movements slow and hypnotic. He paused as she passed, lifting his hat.

"Buenos días, señorita. The heat, it is heavy today, no?"

"Very heavy," she replied in Spanish. And the words did not feel like the clumsy, stumbling recitations of a schoolroom. They flowed, rounded and warm, rising naturally in her throat. She saw the recognition in his eyes—a nod of complicity, of shared endurance against the sun. She belonged to the landscape in a way the Ashcombes never could. They were interlopers, sitting on the terrace with their parasols and their complaints about the dust; she was learning the language of the light.

She walked down to the gate, where the road wound away toward the white village that clung to the hillside like a cluster of salt crystals. The village was calling to her, not with noise, but with a kind of magnetic pull. In the house, she was a governess, a role defined by the boundaries of the nursery and the drawing-room. But down there, where the shadows lay thick and blue against the white walls, she was simply the Englishwoman who spoke, the one who could explain the letter from the bank or the medicine from the doctor.

She remembered the first week, how the silence had terrified her. She had felt her own identity dissolving in the heat, becoming as insubstantial as the dust devils that spun across the road. But now, the silence was a companion. It held the weight of the siesta, the slow passing of centuries. She touched the wall of the villa, the stone hot beneath her palm. It was solid. She was solid.

A moth, pale and fluttering, circled the oleander bush near her hand. It moved with a frantic, erratic energy, a small speck of life against the vast, indifferent afternoon. Eleanor watched it, feeling a sudden, sharp surge of emotion that she could not name. It was not happiness, exactly, nor was it sorrow. It was a sense of being exactly where one was meant to be, suspended in the amber of the Andalusian afternoon. The Ashcombes would leave in October; she knew this. The season would turn, the rains would come, and they would retreat to the damp greys of the north. But the thought of leaving did not bring despair. It brought a strange, sharp clarity, like the taste of the local wine—dry and astringent, yet leaving a warmth behind.

She turned back toward the house. Mrs. Ashcombe would be waiting for the shawl. The order of the day must be maintained. But as she climbed the steps, the gravel crunching underfoot, she felt the village at her back and the sun above, and she knew that she carried this place inside her now. She was no longer just a visitor looking in through the glass; she was a part of the frame. She picked up the shawl, the silk cool against her fingers, and prepared to be the voice that the silence required.
