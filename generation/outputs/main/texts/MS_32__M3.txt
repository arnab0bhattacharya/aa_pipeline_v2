It was in the autumn of the year 1832, when the heavy and unceasing rains had swelled the waters of the Severn to a fearful height, that I sat alone in my private chamber at Ilchester, weighing in the secret and tortured recesses of my own spirit a load of misery such as, I do verily believe, has seldom fallen to the lot of human endurance. I was a man upon whom the world had smiled from my earliest youth; a man whose name had become a word of terror to the wicked, and of profound trust to the upright, for I had been called to the high and awful office of a judge of assize. I had walked the earth with a serene brow and an untroubled heart, esteemed by my brethren, honored by the sovereign, and beloved by a family who looked up to me as to a being removed beyond the sphere of common frailties; yet upon that night, as the wind swept in fierce and melancholy gusts around the ancient walls of my dwelling, I felt myself to be the most wretched and abandoned of all mankind. I had been the minister of a deadly and irrevocable wrong. I had condemned an innocent man to die.

The confession lay before me upon the table, damp with the dews of a recent grave, for it had been written by the hand of one who, in the very article of death, had sought to disburden his soul of its most grievous sin. The true murderer, a man of low and desperate fortune, had lingered for some weeks in the hospital at Exeter, and there, visited by those pangs of remorse which sometimes awaken a tardy and terrible conscience in the most hardened bosoms, he had dictated to a priest a full and circumstantial account of that deed for which another had suffered. He had told how he had entered the dwelling of the old usurer at midnight; how he had struck the mortal blow in the frenzy of avarice; and how, conscious of his guilt and trembling for his life, he had suffered another to be accused, and had exulted, with a fiend-like cruelty, in seeing the fearful sentence executed upon a head which had never harboured a thought of blood.

The victim of this monstrous error had been one Walter Lesingham, a young man of humble birth, but of a singularly gentle and ingenuous disposition, who had been engaged to the usurer's daughter, a maiden of surpassing beauty and devoted affection. I remembered the trial as though it had been but yesterday. The evidence, though circumstantial, had been of that dark and damning character which leaves no loophole for doubt; the young man's defence had been feeble and incoherent, overwhelmed as he was by the magnitude of his misfortune; and I, in my vain reliance upon my own penetration, had summed up the evidence with a severity which I now perceived to have been but the blind and cruel obstinacy of a self-deceived understanding. I had believed that I was doing God service; I had clothed my harshness in the solemn robes of justice; and I had seen the unfortunate youth, his eyes raised to heaven in an agony of unavailing supplication, dragged from the bar to meet a public and ignominious death. I had even congratulated myself upon the firmness I had displayed, and had returned to my own hearth, and folded my innocent children to my bosom, with a heart unsoftened by pity and unstung by remorse.

But now the scales had fallen from my eyes, and the hideous reality was disclosed in all its naked and appalling horror. I had murdered the innocent; I had broken the heart of the father; and I had cast a blight upon the fair name of a gentle maiden, which had driven her into a premature and melancholy grave. I was a murderer, not in the heat of passion, nor in the darkness of ignorance, but in the cold and calculating exercise of that authority which had been conferred upon me for the preservation, not the destruction, of my species. The consciousness of this guilt was more terrible than any human tongue could utter; it seemed to me as though a hand of iron were grasping my heart-strings, and as though the very walls of my chamber were closing around me to suffocate me in the gloom of my own despair.

The question which now agitated my soul, and which made the cold drops of agony start upon my brow, was whether I should make this dreadful truth known to the world. I had but to seal my lips, to burn the confession, and the secret would be buried in my own bosom, and the world would still go on, believing me to be the just and upright judge. But could I do this? Could I, who had so often called upon others to confess their sins, and had pronounced the heaviest sentences upon those who had endeavoured to conceal their guilt, now shrink from the disclosure of my own? Should I, by my silence, tacitly sanction the continuance of that false judgment which had branded an innocent name with infamy, and should I suffer the memory of the dead to be thus loaded with an undeserved curse? The voice of conscience, loud and unceasing, called upon me to speak; it told me that I had no right to enjoy the respect of my fellow-men, which was now but a miserable delusion, and that I ought to stand forth before them as a monument of human fallibility and human error. But then there arose before my imagination the picture of my own family, of my wife, whose delicate health and sensitive spirit would be crushed beneath the weight of such a blow; and of my children, who would blush to bear the name of him who had sent an innocent man to the scaffold. Should I, for the sake of a mere abstract justice, which could not restore the dead to life, nor undo the past, involve them in such a catastrophe? Could I bear to see the finger of scorn pointed at them as they walked the streets, and to hear the whispers of the multitude, "There go the children of the unjust judge"?

I paced my chamber in a state of mind bordering upon distraction. The storm without had increased in violence, and the wind, as it swept through the branches of the ancient elms, seemed to utter moans and lamentations, as though nature herself were mourning over the iniquity which had been committed. I thought of the young man, of his pale and entreating face as he had stood at the bar; I thought of the agonized father, of the broken-hearted maiden; and I felt that, come what might, I could not any longer wear the mask of innocence and justice. I had too long played a part which was not my own; and though the disclosure of my error would cover me with shame and confusion, and would tear from me all that made life valuable, yet I felt that it was better to suffer the extremity of human woe than to live in the enjoyment of a happiness which was based upon a lie. I would not, by my silence, add hypocrisy to my other sins; I would not, for the sake of a transient and false peace, sacrifice the eternal and unchangeable dictates of truth. I would make known the confession to the world; I would publicly acknowledge my error; I would implore the forgiveness of those whom I had injured, if forgiveness were possible; and I would resign the high office which I had so unworthily filled, and retire into the obscurity of private life, there to weep over the past, and to prepare, by a life of penitence, for that dreadful tribunal where the secrets of all hearts are known, and where the innocent and the guilty shall at last receive their final and unerring award.
