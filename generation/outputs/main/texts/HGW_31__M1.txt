It is a curious thing how quickly the novelty of the Far South wears away, to be replaced by the grinding routine of the station. For three months I had been the ship’s doctor aboard the *Boreas*, a vessel now held fast by the winter ice some two miles from the whaling station of Port Providence. My duties were few—treating cases of scurvy, the occasional frostbite, a fisherman with a hand crushed by a block of ice—and I found myself with a great deal of time to observe the strange, brutish life of the place. The men were a rough collection of Norwegians, Scots, and Englishmen, all bound by the common desire to slaughter as many leviathans as the sea would yield before the thaw.

The station was dominated by the figure of its manager, a man named Silas Croft. He was a large, florid man with a voice that could carry across a gale and a commercial instinct as keen and cold as the ice itself. He saw everything in terms of profit and loss, from the barrels of whale oil to the number of nails required to mend a hut. It was Croft who, one Tuesday afternoon, came stamping into the mess hall where I was reading a three-month-old copy of *The Lancet*.

“Doctor! A word,” he bellowed, his cheeks ruddy with cold and excitement. “The ice has split, a mile out. And it has given up a prize. A prize beyond oil and bone.”

I followed him out into the biting wind. A party of men had gathered at the edge of the ice shelf, pointing and shouting. They had found a fissure, and from it, they had hauled something upon the ice. It was a creature, frozen solid in a contorted, terrible posture. It was the size of a large bear, but it was no bear. The head was blunt and broad, with a heavy, shovel-like lower jaw that suggested a life spent rooting or burrowing. Its body was thick and powerful, covered not in fur, but in a dense, greyish hide that seemed almost leathery. Four short, immensely strong legs ended in claws like black flints. But the most astonishing feature was the head. It possessed a single, enormous, central eye, now clouded and white with frost, set deep within a bony socket. The creature was a biological impossibility, a thing that belonged in some fanciful geological epoch, not in the ice of the Southern Ocean.

“Great God,” I whispered, my scientific curiosity warring with a profound sense of unease. “What in the name of nature is it?”

“Nature? Who cares for nature!” Croft roared, slapping me on the back. “This is money, Doctor! A fortune! A one-eyed polar bear! Think of the crowds! Think of the lectures! We’ll have it shipped to London in a block of ice. A king’s ransom, I tell you!”

The men, superstitious and awestruck, began to cross themselves. They saw not a prize, but an omen. But Croft was relentless. He had them rig a sledge and, with much grunting and cursing, the monstrous thing was dragged back towards the station, its rigid limbs scraping a furrow in the snow.

For two days, the creature lay in the open, a subject of constant, morbid fascination. Croft stood guard over it like a dragon on its hoard, arguing with anyone who came near. I took the opportunity to examine it as best I could. The hide was unlike any I had ever felt—thick and yielding in some places, hard as horn in others. The single eye was covered by a nictitating membrane, a translucent lid that was frozen shut. There was no sign of hair, no scales. It was, quite simply, a creature from another world.

It was on the third day that the trouble began. The weather had turned, a warm wind blowing up from the north, unseasonal and disquieting. A fine drizzle began to fall, melting the frost on the station roofs. I was passing the sledge when I noticed a dark, wet patch on the snow beneath the creature’s head. A single drop of water fell from the point of its shovel-jaw and splashed into the puddle. Then another.

I knelt, my heart beginning to beat a little faster. I pressed my fingers to the creature’s hide. It was no longer hard and unyielding. There was a give to it, a horrible, flesh-like softness. The frost was gone from its surface. The great central eye, which had been a milky, frozen orb, now seemed to have a different quality. The cloudiness had lessened, and beneath it, I could almost discern the structure of a vast, dark lens.

“Mr. Croft,” I said, my voice tight. “You must get this thing into the cold store. At once.”

He was busy tallying figures in a ledger. “Eh? What for? It’s a fine day. Let the sun shine on it. It’s worth a few drops of meltwater.”

“It’s not the meltwater I’m concerned with,” I insisted. “The thaw is setting in. It’s… it’s beginning to soften.”

He looked up, his eyes narrowing. “Softening? Of course it’s softening, you fool. It’s ice and flesh. What did you expect? It’s dead, man, frozen solid as a cannonball. It’ll keep. Now leave me be.”

I argued, but it was useless. He saw only a valuable commodity, a thing to be sold. He could not comprehend the biological reality of what was happening. That night, I could not sleep. I lay in my bunk, listening to the wind and the drip-drip-drip of water from the eaves. The image of that single eye haunted me. The idea of a slow, inexorable return to life from such a state of frozen death was a horror that transcended simple curiosity. It was a defiance of every natural law I understood.

Before dawn, I pulled on my boots and coat and went outside. The air was thick with mist. The puddle beneath the creature was now a small pool. The hide was dark and wet, and I could see the powerful muscles of its haunches beneath the skin. I reached out a trembling hand and touched the great, central eyelid. It was pliant. I pressed gently, and to my revulsion, it slid back an inch. The eye beneath was no longer clouded. It was a vast, black, liquid thing, and in its depths, I felt a profound and ancient intelligence, a consciousness that was stirring from its long slumber.

A sound, faint and wet, like a thick tongue being drawn across a palate, came from the creature’s throat.

I scrambled back, my blood turning to ice. It was alive. The thaw was not merely melting the ice; it was reanimating the beast. I ran to Croft’s hut, hammering on the door. He answered it in his nightshirt, his face purple with rage.

“What in blazes is it now?”

“It’s alive!” I gasped. “The creature! It’s thawing! I heard it breathe!”

He stared at me for a moment, then burst into a great, bellowing laugh. “Alive? Doctor, you’ve been at the laudanum! You’ve got ice on the brain. It’s a carcass! A slab of meat and fat! Now go to bed before I have you clapped in irons for hysteria.”

He slammed the door in my face. I stood there in the freezing mist, utterly alone. I looked back towards the sledge, a dark shape in the gloom. The drizzle was turning to rain. The thaw was accelerating. I knew then that my duty was not to the station, nor to Croft’s profit, but to whatever terrible new reality was about to be born from the melting ice. I had to find a way to stop it, before this thing from the world’s forgotten dawn opened its eye upon our own.
