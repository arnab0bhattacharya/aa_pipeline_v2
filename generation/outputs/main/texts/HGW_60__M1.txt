The rumour began, as such things do, with the evening papers and the telegraph boy, and by seven o’clock it had saturated the quiet, respectable streets of St. Ebba’s with a peculiar, feverish dread. The scientific correspondent of *The Times* had noted the unprecedented proximity of the Leonid swarm, and a Professor at Cambridge, with a flair for the dramatic, had calculated a one-in-a-thousand probability of a direct collision. It was this figure, I think, that did the damage. A certainty of doom is a simple thing to face; a probability is a monstrous, teasing uncertainty that gets into a man’s bones.

At No. 14, Canon’s Walk, the household of the Reverend Arthur Penhaligon gathered for what they all privately assumed would be their last supper. The gas lamps hissed in their brackets, casting a soft, yellow light on the polished mahogany and the crisp linen. Arthur, a man whose life had been a comfortable architecture of dogma and daily routine, carved the joint with a steady hand, though his knuckles were white. His wife, Eleanor, a handsome woman whose placid surface hid a surprisingly sharp intellect, watched him, her own thoughts a maelstrom of practical concerns and unspoken regrets. Their son, Julian, home from his engineering studies in London, sat opposite, his face pale and set. He had spent the afternoon calculating trajectories himself and found the professor’s mathematics alarmingly sound. And young Beatrice, barely eighteen, watched them all with the huge, tragic eyes of a girl who reads too much poetry and understands too little of life.

The silence was thick with the things unsaid. It was Julian who broke it. He pushed his plate away, the scrape of china unnaturally loud.
“It is no use pretending,” he said, his voice tight. “The calculations are clear. If it strikes, it will be at midnight. We have perhaps four hours left to be human beings, instead of… this.”
He gestured vaguely at the strained civility of the table.

Arthur looked up, his clerical collar seeming to tighten around his throat. “My boy, we must put our faith in a higher power. This is not a matter for mere mathematics.”
“It is *entirely* a matter for mathematics, Father!” Julian retorted, his frustration boiling over. “And physics! A mass of rock and iron, indifferent to our faith, is hurtling towards us. Don’t you see? It makes all this”—he swept a hand to take in the heavy furniture, the portraits of solemn ancestors, the very air of the cathedral city—“utterly meaningless. All our little plans, our vanities, our secrets… they’ll be dust. Less than dust.”

The word ‘secrets’ hung in the air. Eleanor flinched. Beatrice began to cry, silently, the tears tracking down her cheeks.
“What secrets?” Arthur asked, his voice dangerously soft. “What is this talk of secrets?”

Julian gave a short, bitter laugh. “Oh, come now. We are a family. We are supposed to love one another. But do we even know one another? Before we are atomised by a cosmic accident, let us at least be honest.”

Eleanor placed a hand on her husband’s arm. “Arthur. Julian is right. In the face of… this… what is the point of concealment?” She took a deep breath. “I shall begin. For twenty years, Arthur, I have found your sermons on the damnation of the unrepentant to be a source of profound spiritual distress. I have never believed in the hell you preach. I believe in a God of immanent, practical love, not of eternal fire.”

Arthur stared at her as if she had suddenly begun speaking an alien tongue. His wife, the silent, supportive fixture of his life, a heretic? It was more shocking than the meteor.

Julian, emboldened, leaned forward. “My turn. I have failed my examinations in thermodynamics. I have not told you because I could not bear the shame. I am not the brilliant engineer you believe me to be. I am a failure. And I have been living on the allowance you send me, spending most of it on drink and cards with men you would not care to meet.”

The blow landed. Arthur’s face, already pale, went grey. His son, his hope, the future of the Penhaligon name. A failure. A drunkard.

It was Beatrice’s turn. She looked up, her face streaming with tears. “I am in love,” she whispered. “Not with the curate, as you think. But with Thomas, the gardener’s boy. We have… we have met in the potting shed. I am going to have a child.”

The confession fell into the room like a stone into a still pond. Eleanor reached for her daughter’s hand. Arthur made a small, strangled sound in his throat. His world, his respectable, ordered world, had not merely cracked; it had been pulverised. His wife a doubter, his son a wastrel, his daughter… it was unthinkable.

And so it fell to him. He looked at the three faces staring at him, stripped bare by the prospect of oblivion. They had offered him their ugliness, their weakness, their sin. What had he to offer in return? The truth.
“I have… I have not been a good husband to you, Eleanor,” he began, his voice a dry rustle. “Not for many years. There is a woman. In the town. A widow. She has been… a comfort to me. A comfort that you, in your piety, could not provide.” He looked at Julian. “And I have used the funds for the new church roof to settle her brother’s debts. There is no money left.”

The silence that followed was absolute. The great, grinding machinery of their family life had seized up. They were not the Penhaligons of Canon’s Walk, the respectable clergyman and his brood. They were simply four flawed, frightened people, marooned together in a pool of yellow gaslight. The pretence was gone, burned away by the imagined heat of an approaching star. They looked at one another and saw, for the first time, not the roles they played, but the awkward, suffering creatures beneath. Arthur saw his wife’s strength. Eleanor saw her husband’s pathetic loneliness. Julian saw the crushing weight of his father’s expectations. And Beatrice saw that her terrible secret was, in the grand scheme of their mutual revelations, merely one more piece of wreckage.

They sat thus for a long time. The clock in the hall began to chime the half-hour. Eleven-thirty. The end was near. Arthur reached across the table and took his wife’s hand. It was a gesture he had not made in a decade. Julian poured a glass of water for his sister. The air was not heavy with dread anymore, but with a strange, exhausted peace. They had confessed their sins and were waiting for absolution, not from God, but from a falling star.

They counted the final minutes together, their eyes on the longcase clock. The chimes began for midnight. One. Two. Three. They held their breath, a single, silent organism. The chimes continued, clear and resonant in the stillness. Four. Five. Six. Julian stood and went to the window, drawing back the heavy velvet curtain. Seven. Eight. Nine. The street outside was empty and silent. The sky was a vast, star-dusted blackness. Ten. Eleven. Twelve. The final chime faded.

Nothing. No cataclysmic roar. No blinding flash. No shattering of glass. Only the quiet ticking of the clock and the distant bark of a dog. The meteor had missed.

A collective sigh escaped them. Julian turned from the window, a look of dazed wonder on his face. “It has passed,” he said, his voice full of a profound, almost painful relief. “It has missed us.”

Eleanor began to laugh, a wild, hysterical sound that quickly turned to weeping. Beatrice buried her face in her hands. Arthur stood up, walked to the sideboard, and poured four glasses of brandy with a trembling hand. He passed them round. They drank in silence, the raw spirit a shocking, vital burn in their throats. They looked at one another across the ruined remains of their dinner. The world was saved. And their own, it seemed, had just ended. Or perhaps, Arthur thought, looking at his wife’s tear-streaked face, it had just begun. The morning, he realised with a sudden, terrifying clarity, was going to be very difficult indeed.
