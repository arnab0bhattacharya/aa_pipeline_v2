If any honest chronicle of human perseverance were to be inscribed upon the tablets of memory, there are few institutions in this realm that would furnish a more patient, a more meek, or a more uncomplaining hero than the counting-house of Grimes, Grinder, and Gripe, situate in that venerable square of Lincoln’s Inn Fields, where the very lamp-posts seem to wear a legal aspect, and the very ruts in the pavement are worn deep by the slow erosion of attorneys’ clerks. Among the high stools of that establishment, for nine-and-thirty years, sat Mr. Nehemiah Noggs. Nine-and-thirty years of perpetual copying! Nine-and-thirty years of damp parchment, of legal cap, of red tape, of sand-shakers, and ink, and the monotonous scratching of a quill pen, from the opening of the office shutters at nine of the clock in the morning, to the closing of the same at six in the evening, save only on Saturdays, when the establishment relaxed into a premature dusk at four! He had never missed a day. Summer’s heat, that made the pitch bubble in the surrounding courts, and winter’s frost, that turned the same courts into slides for the errand-boys of the law, had never kept Nehemiah from his stool. He was an immutable fact in the universe of Grimes, Grinder, and Gripe.

Nehemiah had but one solitary pleasure in this weary world, one solitary vanity, one solitary dependent. It was a canary-bird. A little golden, pulsating atom of life, named Dick, who hung in a tiny cage in Nehemiah’s modest lodging in Pentonville, and who compensated for all the leaden prose of legal conveyancing by the joyful poetry of his song.

It was upon a raw, foggy afternoon in November, in the year of our Lord one thousand eight hundred and forty-something, that Mr. Gripe, the surviving partner of the firm—a gentleman of a countenance like a red-hot warming-pan, and a disposition to match—summoned Nehemiah to the awful presence. Mr. Gripe was sitting before a roaring fire, which he appeared to be hoarding for his own exclusive benefit, as if the warmth of it were a perquisite of the firm not to be shared with humbler clerks. 

"Ahem!" said Mr. Gripe, looking not at Nehemiah, but at the silver inkstand upon the table, as if he expected the inkstand to communicate some intelligence. "Hem! Noggs. Thirty years, I think you said, Noggs?"

"Nine-and-thirty, sir, come Martinmas," replied Nehemiah, in a voice that rustled like a dry leaf.

"Nine-and-thirty! Dear me, dear me!" murmured Mr. Gripe, wiping his brow, as if the mere contemplation of such antiquity produced a prickly heat. "Time flies, Noggs; time flies, and—and—the march of intellect, you know, Noggs. We are in a new age, sir. Steam, sir! Machinery, sir! We—we require new blood, Noggs. A young man from the new school. A youth who understands the modern system of double-entry, and the use of the metallic pen. We must part, Noggs. Here is a month’s wages in lieu of notice."

Nehemiah took the money. He tied it up in a corner of his pocket-handkerchief with a trembling hand, and nodding his head twice, as if he were mechanically assenting to some proposition made by the air, he went back to his high stool, sat down upon it for the last time, and gazed vacantly at the blank wall opposite, which had been his unvarying landscape for nearly forty years. 

He untied his apron, folded it with a precision that would have done honour to a Dutch painter, placed it in the drawer, and taking up his hat, went out. Out into the fog, and the mud, and the gathering gloom of Lincoln’s Inn Fields. 

He walked homewards. The way was long, and Pentonville was a weary step for stiffened legs. The fog seemed to enter into his very soul, and to wrap it in a damp and heavy shroud. What was he to say to Prue? Prudence Noggs, his sister, who kept the lodging in Pentonville, and who had knitted him innumerable worsted comforters, and brewed him innumerable cups of tea, during the whole of that nine-and-thirty years? Prue, who believed him the greatest clerk, and the firmest prop of the law, that the world had ever seen? 

He could not tell her. He would not tell her. He would wait until the morning. He would wait until he had formed some brilliant plan for their mutual sustenance. Thus he resolved, as he turned the corner of the street where the little parlour window shone like a solitary star through the London mist.

He opened the door with his latch-key, and entered. The room was warm and bright. A cheerful fire was burning, and before the window, in his cage, sat Dick, the canary, puffing out his little golden throat, and trilling out a song of welcome that sounded, in Nehemiah’s ears, like a requiem over departed greatness.

"Why, Nehemiah, dear!" cried Prue, a little woman of much the same texture as himself—thin, pale, and anxious—rising from her chair and taking his damp coat. "You are early tonight! And how is Mr. Gripe, and the young men, and all?"

"Mr. Gripe is—quite well, Prue," said Nehemiah, sitting down in his easy-chair, and staring very hard at the fire. "The young men are—quite well, Prue."

"And the business, Nehemiah? Is it brisk?"

"Brisk, Prue?" repeated Nehemiah, in a voice that had a faint resemblance to a groan, but which he immediately corrected into a cough. "Yes, yes. Brisk. Very brisk. A great deal of—of copying to be done. A great deal." 

He stopped. The fire burned brightly, and the kettle sang upon the hob, and Dick fluttered his wings, and sang too, as if he were the very embodiment of joy and security. And Nehemiah thought of the new young man, with his metallic pen, and his modern system of double-entry, sitting upon his stool, and looking at his blank wall, and he groaned in spirit.

"You are quite well, Nehemiah?" asked Prue, anxiously, as she spread the cloth for tea. "You are not looking yourself tonight. Your face is as long as—as the law itself."

"I am quite well, my dear," said Nehemiah, forcing a ghastly smile. "I am only—a little tired. It has been a long day. A very long day. But I must not complain, when I have such a home to come to, and such a sister, and such a—such a bird."

He looked at Dick. Dick looked at him, with his bright, knowing little eye, and nodded his head, and piped a note or two, as much as to say, "Don't be down-hearted, old boy! I am here, at any rate."

And Nehemiah, taking refuge in the thought that the bird, at least, was not replaced, and that the kettle, at least, was boiling, and that the worsted comforter, at least, was warm upon his neck, bent his head over his tea-cup, and said no more. But the great sorrow was eating into his heart, and the great fog was creeping up the window-pane, and the great city was roaring in the distance, and the little parlour in Pentonville was but a speck of light in a world of darkness, and Nehemiah Noggs, sitting in his easy-chair, felt that he was a solitary, useless, and forgotten man. 

He drank his tea in silence, and Prue, who was a person of much observation, though of few words, said nothing, but went quietly about her household duties, and left him to his thoughts. And Nehemiah thought of the nine-and-thirty years, and of the high stool, and of the blank wall, and of Mr. Gripe, and of the metallic pen, and of the new young man, and of the fog, and of the mud, and of the future, which was a blanker wall than the one he had left behind him. And he thought of Dick, who was singing his little heart out in the cage, and he wondered, with a kind of dull, heavy wonder, whether the new young man would like canaries, and whether the new young man would have a sister, and whether the new young man would ever know what it was to be nine-and-thirty years in the service of a firm, and to be turned adrift in his old age, like a worn-out horse, to die in a ditch. And then he thought of Prue, and of the tea, and of the fire, and he felt that he was not quite alone, and that there was yet a little warmth and a little light left in the world, and he thanked God for it, and went to bed.
