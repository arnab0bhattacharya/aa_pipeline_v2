In the remote and somewhat dreary village of Little Fenning, where the marshes exhaled a perpetual dampness and the reeds stood up to their knees in black water, there lived a widow by the name of Mrs. Pumble. She was a woman of slender means but of a heart so capacious that it might have been said to contain, not merely one son, but a whole dynasty of them, had Providence so willed it. But Providence had willed only one—Samuel.

Samuel Pumble was the sole idol of his mother’s worship, the great sun of her domestic firmament, around which she, poor satellite, revolved in a constant and admiring orbit. He was a youth of considerable beauty, of a complexion that defied the marsh mists, and of a vanity so delicate and sensitive that it required the most expensive silks to touch it without pain. Mrs. Pumble, who had laboured for twenty years in the service of a certain Sir Barnacle, and had saved, penny by penny, a sum of money that was to her as the gold of Ophir, looked upon Samuel and saw not a boy, but a Gentleman in the rough. She saw him as the future ornament of London, the refined being who would walk the streets of the great city not as a pedestrian, but as a triumph.

‘Samuel,’ she would say, on those winter evenings when the wind rattled the casement and the candle threw long, dancing shadows on the low ceiling, ‘you were not made for Little Fenning. You were made for the best tables, my dear. You were made to be admired.’

And Samuel, who had never known a want that was not instantly satisfied, and who had been taught to believe that his hands were too fine for work and his mind too lofty for study, agreed with her entirely. He possessed a talent for admiration—both to receive and to give it—which is the first requisite of the fashionable world. He could admire a coat, a cravat, a carriage, or a waiter with an air of condescension that was perfectly charming. He was, in short, a gentleman by the grace of his mother’s delusions.

When the time came for Samuel to leave the nest, Mrs. Pumble unlocked the little iron box where her treasures lay. She counted out the sovereigns and the banknotes, her hands trembling, not with regret, but with the ecstasy of sacrifice. She gave him the whole; she gave him everything. She gave him the savings of her servitude, the wages of her future, the very bread of her old age, that he might go to London and cut a figure worthy of his natural gifts.

‘Spend it, my angel!’ she cried, embracing him as the coach waited in the mud. ‘Spend it and be happy! Let them see that a Pumble can live like a lord!’

And Samuel went.

Now, if there is a place in this world where money can be made to fly faster than a swallow in June, it is London. And if there is a man who can assist money in its flight, it is the man who is determined to be a Gentleman without the trouble of earning the title. Samuel Pumble descended upon the metropolis like a golden meteor. He took up his abode at the ‘Grand Porphyry Hotel,’ a vast and glittering edifice in the West End, where the floors were polished to such a pitch that one seemed to walk upon ice, and the chandeliers were so numerous and bright that they made the sun look like a provincial tradesman.

For a year, Samuel was the King of the Porphyry. He dined at the best table, in the corner by the window, where the light fell just so upon his exquisite cravat. He ordered the most expensive wines, not because he liked them, but because he liked the sound of the names as he spoke them, and the way the sommelier bowed. He tipped the porters with a liberality that was positively reckless; he flung silver to the boots, gold to the waiters, and smiled upon the chambermaids with a benevolence that cost him a shilling a look. He was the sun around which the hotel revolved. He was the glass of fashion and the mould of form.

But, alas! The sun, however bright, must eventually set, especially if it is burning up the very oil that keeps it alight. The iron box in Little Fenning was empty. The savings were gone. The banknotes had melted away like snow in April. Samuel, who had lived like a prince, found himself suddenly, and with a shock that was almost physical, reduced to the position of a pauper.

He could not return to Little Fenning. The idea was insupportable. To face his mother, to see the adoration in her eyes turn to horror, to confess that the Gentleman was a bankrupt—it was a fate worse than death. No, he must stay in London. He must work.

And here, in the workings of that mysterious Providence which governs the affairs of men, we behold a coincidence so sharp, so biting, and so perfectly calculated to humble the pride of man, that it might have been invented by a novelist of the most satirical turn. Samuel Pumble, in his desperate search for employment, applied for a post at the only establishment he knew well enough to navigate: The Grand Porphyry Hotel.

The manager, a man with a face like a stone wall and a heart like a ledger, looked at Samuel. He saw the fine clothes, now somewhat frayed, the elegant carriage, now somewhat drooping. He saw a young man who knew the difference between a Lafite and a Latour. He hired him.

And so it came to pass that Samuel Pumble, the former idol of the Porphyry, became a waiter in its halls.

It was a scene for a painter, or perhaps for a moralist. The room was the same room. The chandeliers were the same chandeliers. The silver was the same silver. But Samuel was no longer the one who sat; he was the one who stood. He was no longer the one who commanded; he was the one who obeyed.

On a busy Tuesday evening, when the hotel was thronged with the wealthy and the wanton, Samuel was serving at Table Number Four—the very table where he had once held court. The new occupant was a stout gentleman with a red face and a voice like a foghorn, who was entertaining a party of friends with a display of vulgarity that would have made a billiard-marker blush.

‘Waiter!’ roared the stout gentleman. ‘More wine! And be quick about it!’

Samuel, who had learned the art of invisibility that is the first lesson of servitude, glided forward. He kept his eyes downcast, his expression neutral. He poured the wine with a steady hand, though his heart was beating a tattoo against his ribs.

‘And tell me,’ said the stout gentleman, peering at Samuel with a bleary eye, ‘have I not seen you before? You have a familiar look. A sort of washed-out look.’

Samuel felt a hot flush rise from his collar to his hairline. It was the voice of a man he had tipped lavishly a year ago—a man to whom he had tossed a half-sovereign for fetching a hat.

‘I think not, sir,’ Samuel murmured, his voice barely a whisper. ‘I am new here.’

‘New, are you?’ bellowed the gentleman. ‘Well, you pour like an old hand. But look sharper, my man! I don’t pay for service to be slow!’

And then, the gentleman did a thing that struck Samuel to the soul. He reached into his pocket, pulled out a greasy handful of coppers, and tossed them onto the tablecloth.

‘There,’ said the gentleman, with the air of a monarch bestowing a dukedom. ‘That’s for you. Don’t spend it all at once.’

Samuel looked at the coins. They lay there, dull and dirty, against the white linen. They were worth perhaps fourpence. A year ago, he would not have stooped to pick up such a sum; he would have left it there as a tip for the scullery boy. Now, it was his wages. It was his bread.

He reached out. His hand, which had once worn rings of gold, trembled slightly as he swept the coppers into his palm. He bowed—a deep, abject bow that bent his spine and broke his spirit.

‘Thank you, sir,’ said Samuel Pumble. ‘Thank you.’

He turned and walked away, his tray held high, his step measured. In the corner of the room, a young couple were dining. They were looking at him with the same expression of condescending benevolence that he had once bestowed upon the porters. They were admiring his livery; they were commenting on his neatness. They were, in short, treating him as a piece of the furniture—useful, necessary, but not quite human.

Samuel went into the pantry. He stood by the sink, where the steam rose in clouds and the clatter of dishes was a constant din. He looked at the four dirty coppers in his hand. He thought of the marshes at Little Fenning, of the damp air, and of his mother sitting by the fire, waiting for a letter from her Gentleman that would never come. The grandeur of London, the glitter of the Porphyry, the taste of the wine—it all vanished, leaving only the bitter taste of reality. He was a waiter. And the world, which had once bowed to him, now expected him to bow to it.
