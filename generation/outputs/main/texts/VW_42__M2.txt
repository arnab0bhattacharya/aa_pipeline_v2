The light in the drawing room fell in long, pale slats across the carpet, cutting the dust motes into suspended galaxies, and Elara watched them drift, unaware that they were already settling into the pattern of a life half-remembered. It was 1898, or perhaps 1902; the dates had a way of blurring, like ink dropped in water, leaving only the color and the sensation of the moment. She sat with her hands folded on her lap, the fabric of her dress stiff with starch, a small, white fortress against the softness of the afternoon. Across from her, Clara stirred her tea, the spoon clinking against the porcelain with a sound so sharp it seemed to shatter the quiet.

“Do you remember the pond?” Clara asked, her voice thin, stretched over the years. “The one behind the school? We used to dip our hands in it until they were blue.”

Elara did not answer immediately. She looked at Clara, really looked, and saw not the girl with the wet sleeves and the mud-caked knees, but the woman who now sat opposite, her hair pinned up in severe loops, her eyes bright with a fever that had not broken in twenty years. There was a gap between them, a void filled with unspoken things, with the years of silence that had grown thick as fog. To speak of the pond was to admit that they had been children, that they had been free, and that freedom was a thing that could be lost, like a glove, or a name.

“Blue,” Elara said, and the word felt strange in her mouth, cold and wet. “Yes. The water was so cold it hurt.”

Margaret, who sat by the window, turned her head. Her profile was sharp, angular, a series of hard lines against the grey sky. She had always been the serious one, the one who kept the books, who counted the pence and the hours. Now she was counting the days, perhaps, or the breaths. She did not join the conversation; she merely watched the steam rise from her cup, watching it curl and dissipate, like the smoke from a fire that has gone out.

The third woman, Lydia, was not there. She had died, or she had left, or perhaps she had simply ceased to be, as people do when they are no longer needed. Her chair was empty, a void in the circle, a missing tooth in a jaw. Elara felt the absence of Lydia as a physical pressure, a tightening in the chest. Lydia had been the bridge, the one who connected them, the one who laughed too loudly and cried too easily. Without her, the triangle had collapsed into a line, rigid and brittle.

They had met before, at the wedding of their cousin, a ceremony of white flowers and cold air. Elara had stood in the back, watching the bride, thinking of the permanence of the bond, the way it bound people together in a knot that could not be untied. But bonds, she realized, were not like that. They were like the dust motes in the light, drifting, separating, finding their own paths.

Now, in the railway station, the air was thick with the smell of coal smoke and damp wool. It was 1924, and the world had changed, had broken and reformed itself, and they were still here, still standing, still waiting. Elara watched the clock on the wall, its hands moving with a slow, deliberate grace. Clara was beside her, holding her hand, her fingers cold and trembling.

“We should go,” Clara said. “The train is coming.”

Elara looked around the platform. The faces of the strangers were blurred, indistinct, a mask of anonymity. She saw a man in a flat cap, a woman with a basket of vegetables, a child clinging to a mother’s skirt. They were all waiting, all suspended in that moment between departure and arrival, between the known and the unknown.

“Where are we going?” Elara asked, though she knew the answer. They were going home, or to the grave, or to the next meeting, which would be the last.

“Home,” Clara said. “To the country house.”

The country house, where the gardens grew wild, where the ivy covered the walls, where the memory of the pond was still fresh in the earth. It was a place of ghosts, of echoes, of the past pressing in on the present. Elara felt the weight of it, the burden of the years, the accumulation of small moments, of words left unsaid, of glances exchanged and missed.

The train arrived with a hiss of steam and a clang of metal, a sudden intrusion into the stillness. The doors opened, and the noise of the city poured in, a roar of voices, of wheels, of life. Elara stepped onto the platform, her shoes clicking on the gravel. Clara followed, her hand still in Elara’s. Margaret was already inside, her silhouette visible through the window, looking out at them with an expression of mild surprise, as if she had not expected them to come.

They boarded the train, finding seats in the corner, their knees touching, their shoulders close. The train began to move, the landscape sliding past the window, a blur of fields and trees, of houses and farms. Elara watched it go, the world flowing away, leaving only the interior, the small, enclosed space of the carriage, where the three of them sat, bound together by the thread of their history, by the weight of their silence.

The light changed, the sun dipping below the horizon, casting long shadows across the floor. The air in the carriage grew cool, and Elara pulled her shawl tighter around her shoulders. She looked at Clara, who was staring out the window, her face pale, her eyes bright. She looked at Margaret, who was reading a book, her lips moving silently.

They were old now, she thought. Their faces were lined, their hair grey, their bodies fragile. But there was something there, something that remained, a spark, a flame that did not go out. It was in the way they looked at each other, in the way they sat together, in the shared silence that was not empty, but full, full of memory, of love, of loss.

The train moved on, through the night, through the darkness, carrying them towards the morning, towards the country house, towards the end. Elara closed her eyes, and in the darkness, she saw the pond, the blue water, the mud, the laughter. She saw Lydia, smiling, reaching out her hand. And she reached back, in the dream, in the memory, in the life that continued, beyond the reach of time, beyond the reach of death.

The wheels hummed, a low, steady note, a lullaby, a song of departure and arrival, of beginning and end. And in the quiet, in the dark, in the space between the stations, they were together, as they had always been, as they would always be, bound by the invisible, the unbreakable, the eternal thread of their friendship.
