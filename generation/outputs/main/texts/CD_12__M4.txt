The morning on which I was to go aboard the *Perseverance*—a name which, I am bound to say, she bore with a grim and dogged inconsistency, for she persevered in nothing but the rolling and pitching that turned my very soul within me—was a morning of raw, grey mist, through which the masts and funnels of the Tyne went up like the ribs of drowned leviathans, and the water lapped against the quays with a cold and greasy sound, as if it were licking its lips over some private and disagreeable reflection. I stood upon the stones with my little bundle under my arm, a bundle containing all my worldly goods, which were not many, and which were tied up with a piece of string that had once belonged to my poor father’s Sunday neckcloth; and I looked at the black hull of the brig, and she looked at me, and I thought—though I did not say it, being of a shy and silent habit, and never apt to put my feelings into words—that we were two of the most miserable objects in that miserable port.

My father had been a clerk in a merchant’s office in Newcastle, a small, spare, stooping man, with a cough that shook him like a leaf, and a way of looking at me over his spectacles as if he were sorry to have brought me into a world that he found so little to his liking. When he died—and he died in the winter, quietly, with his hand in mine, and the rain beating on the window—there was nothing left for me but the charity of distant relatives, who, being poor themselves, and having no great love for the encumbrance of a boy, had contrived, by dint of much letter-writing and many shakings of the head, to get me apprenticed as cabin-boy aboard a collier trading between Newcastle and London. And so it was that I found myself, on that raw morning, standing on the quay, with my father’s neckcloth string cutting into my fingers, and the smell of tar and rope and coal-dust in my nose, and a great lump in my throat that I could not swallow down, do what I would.

The captain of the *Perseverance* was a small, dry, brown man, like a nut that has been kept too long, by name Captain Rumbold, who took me by the shoulder and turned me round as if I were a bale of goods, and said, “Cabin-boy, hey? You’ll do. Don’t want any whining aboard my ship. Keep your mouth shut and your hands busy, and you’ll get on well enough.” And with that he pushed me down the companion-ladder, and I found myself in the dark, close, fusty little cabin, where the lamp swung to and fro, and the smell of stale tobacco and salt beef was so thick that I could have cut it with a knife.

It was there that I first saw the cook. He was a giant of a man, with a great red face, and a nose that had been broken in some forgotten quarrel, and one eye—a small, bright, twinkling eye—that seemed to take in everything and to be amused by all of it; for the other eye was gone, and in its place was a black patch, or rather a sort of leathern socket, which gave him a most ferocious and alarming appearance, so that at first sight I trembled in my shoes and wished myself back in Newcastle, even in the cold and the mist and the loneliness of it. He was standing by the stove, stirring something in a great iron pot, and he looked at me with that one eye, and he said, in a voice that was like the growl of a bear with a sore head:

“What’s this? A cabin-boy? Lord help us, they’re sending ’em out younger and younger every voyage. What’s your name, boy?”

“Nicholas, sir,” says I, in a very small voice.

“Nicholas what?”

“Nicholas Penny, sir.”

“Penny, hey?” says he, and he stirred the pot, and he looked at me again, and then he said, “Well, Penny, you’re a very small coin for a very large ship, but I daresay you’ll pass. Can you eat?”

“Yes, sir,” says I, though in truth I was so frightened that I could have eaten nothing.

“Then you’re better off than most,” says he, and he turned back to his pot, and that was the end of the conversation for that day.

But I soon found that the cook—whose name was Matthew Grimes, though he was known aboard as “One-Eye Grimes,” or more commonly, “the Cook”—was a man of a most uncertain and violent temper, who would roar at the least provocation, and who had been known, so the sailors said, to throw a saucepan at a man’s head for looking at him crooked. He swore at the sea, and he swore at the wind, and he swore at the meat, and he swore at the stove, and he swore at me most of all, for I was clumsy and slow and frightened, and I dropped things, and I burned things, and I was always in the way. And yet, in the midst of all his roaring, there was something in his one eye that was not unkind; and sometimes, when he had been cursing me for a fool and a lubber and a land-lubbering son of a sea-cook, he would fall suddenly silent, and look at me with a queer, puzzled expression, as if he were trying to remember something that had slipped his memory, and then he would turn away and stir his pot and say no more.

We had been out three days, and the weather had been foul, and I had been sick—oh, so sick!—and had lain in my little bunk in the dark, with the water swashing against the side of the ship, and the timbers groaning, and the men trampling overhead, and had wished that I might die and be buried in the deep, where at least there would be no more rolling and pitching. And on the third night, when the wind had fallen and the sea had gone down, and I was lying in my bunk, weak and trembling and miserable, the door of the cabin opened, and the Cook came in, with a lantern in his hand, and stood looking down at me with that one eye.

“Well, Penny,” says he, “how are you?”

I was so surprised that I could not answer him at first, for it was the first time that any one aboard had asked me that question, and it came from the last man on the ship from whom I should have expected it. And then, before I could speak, he sat himself down on a chest by my bunk, and he set the lantern on the floor, and he said:

“I had a boy once. A little chap, no bigger than you. He was my sister’s son, and she died when he was born, and I took him and brought him up, and he was the joy of my life. And I lost him. He was drowned—drowned in the river, not far from Newcastle Bridge—and I was away at sea, and I never knew till I came home. And I have never been the same man since.”

He said this in a low, gruff voice, looking at the lantern and not at me, and I lay in my bunk and listened, and I felt the tears coming into my eyes, for I thought of my father, and of the rain beating on the window, and of his hand in mine.

“So when I see a little chap like you,” says the Cook, “I think of my boy, and I—well, I don’t know as I’m always so gentle with you as I might be. But I mean no harm, Penny. I mean no harm.”

And then he got up, and he took the lantern, and he said, “You’ll be all right in the morning. I’ll make you some gruel.” And he went out, and shut the door behind him, and I lay in the dark and cried, but not because I was unhappy; and from that night, though he still swore at me and called me a lubber and a fool, I knew that there was one person aboard the *Perseverance* who cared whether I lived or died, and who would have asked me how I was, even if I had been the poorest and smallest cabin-boy that ever went to sea.
