In the small Midland market-town of Chadbourn, where the slow-moving waters of the Stour reflect the grey stone of the ancient bridge and the dissolving hues of the twilight sky, there stood, a little removed from the principal thoroughfare, a substantial red-brick dwelling known to the townsfolk as Birkett House. It was a house that bore upon its very façade the quiet annals of a woman’s surrender; for Mrs. Deborah Birkett, its widowed mistress, had, upon the sad demise of her husband in the prime of his days as a prosperous wool-stapler, deliberately narrowed the boundaries of her life to a single, absorbing point of devotion—her son, Ambrose. It is a common error in the youthful to suppose that the sacrifice of personal inclination must necessarily evoke a corresponding gratitude in the object of such self-abnegation; for human nature, so far from being a chemical compound that yields a predictable precipitate when acted upon by a given force, is rather a vital organism, expanding or contracting according to laws of its own, and often resenting the very pressure that was meant to mould it into perfection. Deborah, however, was not given to such generalising reflections. She saw only that she had surrendered society, travel, and the cheerful intercourse of kindred minds, and that she had thereby secured for Ambrose the undivided fruits of her small fortune and her ceaseless care. That he, in return, should feel his liberty somewhat galled by the invisible threads of her watchful tenderness was a thought she would have repudiated as an instance of the most monstrous ingratitude.

It was on a damp evening in late October, 1842, that Ambrose, now a young man of four-and-twenty, entered the parlour where his mother sat by the fire, her hands busy with a piece of fine needlework. The room was lighted by a solitary lamp, which threw its disc of yellow illumination upon her grey silk gown and the sharp, clean lines of her profile, leaving the corners in a dusk that seemed to harbour the very spirit of respectable reserve. Ambrose was a tall fellow, carrying his head with a certain buoyant defiance that Deborah was wont to attribute to the consciousness of an unspotted moral character, though it might, by a less partial observer, have been set down to the mere animal spirits of an excellent constitution. He drew a chair to the opposite side of the hearth, and after a few moments of silence, during which he gazed into the coals with an intensity that was not the result of any geological curiosity regarding their composition, he spoke.

“Mother, I have something to tell you. I have done what you will perhaps think very foolish, but I cannot think it so. I am married. I was married to Susannah Linnett this morning at St. Botolph’s.”

The needlework fell from Deborah’s hands. She did not immediately speak; she only fixed her eyes upon her son’s face with a look in which astonishment, pain, and a slow, gathering anger were mingled, like the conflicting currents of a stream that meets an unexpected obstacle. At last she said, in a voice that was unnaturally calm, “To Susannah Linnett—the linen-draper’s daughter from the Meeting-house?”

“Yes, mother. We have been engaged this twelvemonth and more.”

“A twelvemonth!” she repeated, slowly. “And you have kept this from me—you, Ambrose, whom I have nursed and tended, for whom I have spared no sacrifice, you have lived a lie at my very table for a twelvemonth!”

“Nay, mother, not a lie,” said Ambrose, colouring deeply and shifting his position with a restlessness that argued an uneasy conscience. “I kept it to myself because I knew you had a prejudice against the Linnetts. And I was afeared—”

“Feared!” she broke in, her voice rising. “You feared your own mother! And what is this prejudice you speak of? Is it nothing that old Linnett has preached sedition against the Church from that conventicle of his in Silver Street? Is it nothing that his daughter has been brought up to look upon our worship as a mockery, and our prayers as a form of wordly idolatry? And you have linked your name with hers, and brought this slur upon your father’s house!”

She had risen, and stood before him, a woman of striking presence still, her dark eyes flashing with the inherited fire of a long line of sturdy conformists. Ambrose did not flinch, but he looked at her with a sort of dogged patience.

“Susannah is not to be judged by her father’s preaching, mother,” he said. “She is a good girl, and she will make me a true wife. I love her, and I could not help it. I did not mean to deceive you, but I knew you would be set against it, and I could not give her up.”

There was a silence. The fire burnt low, and the lamp flickered, as if it, too, were aware of the tension in the room. Deborah sank back into her chair, and her hands, clasped in her lap, trembled. She was conscious of a keen, almost physical, anguish; not merely that her son had chosen a wife from a sect she despised, but that he had done this thing in secret, had deliberated and acted without her counsel, as if she were a stranger to his confidence. It was the first thorough disillusionment of her life. She had believed that she held the first place in his heart, and now she saw that another had displaced her, had, indeed, been in possession for a twelvemonth, while she, in her fond blindness, had been worshipping an image that had no counterpart in fact.

“Where is she now?” asked Deborah, at length, in a hard, dry tone.

“She is at her father’s, until I have spoken with you. She did not wish to come here till you knew. She is not wanting in respect, mother. She bade me tell you so.”

“I am obliged to her for her condescension,” said Deborah, bitterly. “And what is it you ask of me? Do you come for my blessing on this deceit?”

“I come because I cannot do without you, mother,” said Ambrose, and his voice broke. “I have done wrong to keep it from you, I know. But I could not bear to pain you. And now I ask you—will you not receive her? She is my wife, and she will be gentle and dutiful to you, I am sure. I have not forgotten what you have been to me. I love her, but I love you too. I cannot be happy if you are against me.”

He had come nearer, and was standing beside her, looking down at her with an expression that recalled the little child who used to bring her his broken toys and expect her to mend them. The resemblance was keen enough to pierce through her armour of resentment. She looked up at him, and the tears started to her eyes. It is hard for a woman who has lived for one object to see that object suddenly transformed into something independent of her will, something that has struck root in a soil she cannot reach; but it is harder still to resist the appeal of a voice that has been the music of her life.

“You have given me a heavy stroke, Ambrose,” she said, at last, in a voice that trembled. “I cannot pretend that I feel it lightly. You have broken my trust in you, and it will take long to mend. But you are my son, and I will not cast you off. I do not say I can receive her to-morrow, or the next day. I must get used to the thought. But you shall not want a home, and she shall not be turned from my door, if she is what you say. Only, do not ask me to bless what I cannot approve. I will bear it, because I must. But a blessing—that is more than I can give yet.”

She held out her hand, and he took it, pressing it to his lips. He did not speak, for he felt that her concession was wrung from her by a struggle that left no room for words. And so they parted for the night, Ambrose going out into the damp air with a heart lightened of its burden, yet sobered by a new sense of the cost of his happiness; and Deborah sitting alone by the dying fire, with the consciousness that the long labour of her life had ended, not in the triumph she had dreamed, but in a slow, patient endurance of that which she could neither alter nor fully accept.
