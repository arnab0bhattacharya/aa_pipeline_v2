In the city of Aurea, which men call the City of the Golden Gate, there were born upon the self-same night, and almost at the self-same hour, two children: the son of the wealthy merchant Chrysogonus, and the son of the poor cobbler Philotas, who mended the sandals of the poor in a little shop beneath the shadow of the palace wall. And because the night was wild with stars, and the moon walked in her silver sandals over the roofs, there came to the two cradles a fairy, who loved, they say, the smell of new-born things, and the small clenched hands of sleepers.

To the child of Chrysogonus, who lay in a cradle of ivory inlaid with amber, and whose coverlet was of cloth-of-gold, she gave the gift of beauty, and kissed him on the brow. ‘Thou shalt be fair,’ she said, ‘fairer than the morning, and men shall forgive thee everything for the sake of thy face.’ And to the child of Philotas, who lay in a cradle of wattled osier, and whose coverlet was the warm cloak of his mother, she gave the gift of a kind heart, and kissed him on the lips. ‘Thou shalt be loving,’ she said, ‘and thou shalt suffer, for a kind heart is a lantern that burns in the wind; yet by its light shalt thou see what the fair face never sees.’

Then she went away over the roofs, and the stars sang together, and the two children slept.

Now the name of the rich man’s son was Hyacinth, and the name of the poor man’s son was Dorian. And Hyacinth grew as the narcissus grows, in a garden of pleasant waters, and his beauty was a thing men spoke of in the market-place. His hair was the colour of honey in the sun, and his eyes were as two violets drowned in dew, and when he walked in the streets the people turned to look after him, and the young men sighed, and the young women blushed, and the old men said, ‘Surely this is a god who has forgotten to go home.’ And because he was beautiful, he was forgiven all things: his pride, his idleness, his little cruelties, which were many. For beauty, said the world, has the right to be cruel, since she gives so much and asks so little.

But Dorian grew as the common grass grows, unnoticed and unwatered, in the dusty street where the cobbler’s shop stood. His face was plain, and his hands were hard with the wax and the awl, and he mended the sandals of the beggars for nothing, and gave his bread to the hungry, and his coat to the naked, so that often he went cold that another might be warm. And men said of him, ‘He is a good lad, but he is not clever, and he will never be rich, for he gives everything away.’ Yet the heart within him was like a fountain in a courtyard, and whoever drank of it went away refreshed.

Now it came to pass, when the twenty years were over, that the King of Aurea held a great festival at the palace, and all the city was bidden, both the rich and the poor, for the King was old and wished to see the faces of his people before he died. And Hyacinth came to the palace gate in a chariot of silver, with a cloak of peacock’s feathers, and a rose in his hand, and the crowd drew back as the sea draws back before a swan. And at the self-same gate came Dorian on foot, with his leathern apron, and his sleeves rolled up, and a bundle of mended sandals on his back, for he had been working since dawn and had not stopped to wash the dust from his hands.

And the two young men met at the gate, and the one looked at the other, and the one was beautiful and the other was plain.

‘Stand aside, cobbler,’ said Hyacinth, with the careless insolence of a face that has never been refused. ‘Dost thou not see that the gate is too narrow for the both of us?’

But Dorian smiled, for he had a smile that was like the opening of a door in a dark house, and he said, ‘The gate is wide enough for all the world, fair sir, and I would not rob thee of thy place. Go thou in first, for thou art more comely than I, and the King will be glad to see thee.’

And Hyacinth was pleased with the compliment, for he loved flattery as a cat loves cream, and he went in with a toss of his honey-coloured head. But as he passed, Dorian saw a beggar-child weeping at the gate because the guards had driven her away, and he stopped and took the child by the hand and led her in with him, and gave her a sweetmeat from his pocket, and the child laughed, and the people blessed him.

Now it chanced that the King, who was old and weary of flattery, sat upon his throne and looked into the faces of the crowd, and his eyes were dim with much seeing. And he saw Hyacinth, and he said, ‘Ah, there is beauty; let her come near, that I may look at her before I die.’ And Hyacinth came and knelt, and the King was pleased, and gave him a chain of gold. But then the King saw Dorian, who stood in the shadow with the beggar-child in his arms, and the child was asleep, and Dorian’s face was tired and kind, and the King looked long at him, and his old eyes were wet.

‘Who is that?’ said the King.

And they told him, ‘It is the cobbler’s son, who mends the sandals of the poor for nothing.’

Then the King said, ‘Bring him to me.’ And Dorian came, and the King said, ‘Why dost thou carry the child?’

And Dorian said, ‘She was weeping at the gate, and the gate is wide enough for all the world, and she is small, and I am strong.’

And the King said, ‘Thou hast a kind heart.’

And Dorian said, ‘It is a poor thing, sire, and it has no beauty, but it is all I have.’

Then the King was silent a long while, and at last he said, ‘Beauty is a gift, and a kind heart is a gift, and the giver of gifts is the same. But beauty is a flower that fades, and a kind heart is a root that grows. I have seen much beauty in my day, and it has wearied me; but kindness I have never wearied of. Let the fair youth take the chain of gold, for such things are for those who have need of them; but do thou, cobbler, take this ring from my finger, and be the keeper of my poor, for thou knowest the way to the gate that is wide enough for all the world.’

And Hyacinth went out from the palace with his chain of gold, and the people praised his beauty, and he was happy, for he loved praise. But Dorian went out with the ring, and the beggar-child still in his arms, and the people did not praise him, for they did not see him, and he was happy, for he loved them.

And the fairy, who had watched it all from the roof of the palace, laughed softly to herself, and said, ‘I gave them each a gift, and they have each received the other’s, and the world will never know the difference, for the world loves a fair face better than a kind heart, and yet it is the kind heart that saves the world.’

And she flew away over the roofs, and the stars sang together, and the city slept.
