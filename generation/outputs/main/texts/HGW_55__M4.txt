It was on a Tuesday, I believe, that the horses first went wrong. I had noticed nothing of it myself until I came into the yard behind the Bell and Whistle, where I kept my cab and my horse, Bess, and found the ostler, a young fellow named Hibbins, standing with his mouth agape and his bucket spilled at his feet.

“She won’t have it, Mr. Pardoe,” he said, as though this were the end of the world. “She won’t have the water nor the feed, and she’s been standing there this half-hour with her head against the wall, shivering like a leaf.”

I went to her. She was a good mare, a bay with a white star, and I had driven her for six years and knew every mood of her. But she was not herself that morning. Her eyes had a queer, flat look, as if she were seeing something a long way off, and when I put my hand on her neck she flinched as though I had struck her. I spoke to her softly, and she turned her head and looked at me, and I would swear to this day there was something in that look that was not horse at all. It was a sort of desperate, wondering appeal, such as you might see in the eyes of a man who feels himself going mad and cannot stop it.

“It’s the heat,” said Hibbins, who was a great one for explanations. “Or the new feed. There’s a lot of it about.”

But it was not the heat, and it was not the feed. By noon the whole of London was in an uproar. I had gone out with a fare to Bloomsbury, and in the Tottenham Court Road I saw a dray-horse—a great, patient, heavy brute that had plodded the streets for years—suddenly rear up and come down on the shafts, smashing them, and then go tearing down the pavement, scattering the people like chaff. The driver was thrown clear, and lay in the gutter swearing and white as paper. A cab-horse went past me at a gallop, the cab swaying behind it, the driver sawing at the reins and shouting, and the horse’s mouth was a froth of blood. I pulled Bess into a side street and sat there, my heart going like a trip-hammer, while the noise of the city rose into a sort of screaming pandemonium.

It was a madness, you understand. There is no other word for it. The horses of London went mad that day, all in a few hours, as though some invisible hand had touched them all at once. They kicked and bit and screamed and ran; they hurled themselves against walls and shop-fronts; they trampled their drivers and the people in the streets. The police were helpless. The military were called out, and there was shooting in the Strand and in Oxford Street, and by evening the streets were choked with overturned vans and dead horses and broken glass, and the whole city was in a panic that I can only compare to the stories one reads of the plague.

I got Bess home by a miracle of quietness and cunning, through back streets and mews, talking to her all the way, and she was trembling and sweating and starting at every sound, but she obeyed me. And when I had her in the yard I stood and looked at her, and I knew what they would do. They would come round with rifles, the authorities would, and shoot every horse in London that could not be got under control. It was the only sensible thing, I suppose. I am not a fool, and I could see that. A mad horse in a crowded street is a machine for murder. But I looked at Bess, and I thought of the six years we had had together, the wet nights and the cold mornings and the fares that had paid my rent and bought my children their shoes, and I said to myself, “They shall not have her.”

I had a cellar under the yard, a great, damp, brick-walled place that had been used for storing coal and was now empty. It had a door that opened from the yard and a ramp down to it that had been used for the coal-carts. I got Bess down that ramp that night, with Hibbins helping me and swearing he would say nothing, and I made her a stall in the driest corner with straw and an old blanket, and I brought her water in a pail and a mash of oats and bran. She would not eat at first, but stood in the dark with her head down, and I sat on an upturned box beside her and talked to her as I had never talked to her before, telling her about the old days and the good fares and the bad, and how we had come through the fog together, and how she was the best mare in London and I would not let them touch a hair of her.

For three days I stayed with her, going up only at night to get food and to hear the news. The news was bad. The madness had spread to the dogs, they said, and to the cats, and there was talk of rats. The city was under a sort of martial law. The streets were being cleared, and the dead horses were being burned in great pyres in the parks, and the smoke hung over London like a brown fog. My wife cried and begged me to come away, to take the children to her sister’s in Croydon, but I would not leave Bess. I sent them, and I stayed.

On the fourth day Hibbins came down to me, white-faced, and said the soldiers were in the next street, shooting every horse they found, and that they had a list of the cab-yards and were coming to ours. I heard them come into the yard above, the boots and the voices, and I stood in the cellar with my hand on Bess’s neck and my heart going like a drum. She was quiet now, quieter than she had been, and she turned her head and nuzzled my shoulder as though she knew. I heard the sergeant say, “Nothing here,” and the boots go out again, and I let out my breath and sat down on the box and shook like a man with the ague.

But the quiet of her was a strange thing. It was not the quiet of a horse that is well. She would stand for hours with her head against the brick, and her eyes had that flat, far-away look, and sometimes she would shiver all over as though a fly had walked on her, though there was no fly. And once, in the night, she made a sound that was not a horse’s sound at all—a sort of low, long, quivering moan, like a woman crying in her sleep. I put my hand on her and spoke to her, and she came back to herself, but I knew then that the madness was in her, and that it was only a matter of time.

I have thought about it a great deal since. I am not an educated man, and I do not know what it was that came over the beasts of London that week. Perhaps it was some poison in the air, some miasma, as the doctors said. Perhaps it was the beginning of something new, some change in the world that we had no notion of. But I know what I saw in Bess’s eyes, and I know it was not the look of a sick animal. It was the look of a creature that has been patient and obedient all its life and has suddenly seen the bars of its cage. It was the look of something that has been used and driven and had its mouth pulled about with bits, and has suddenly understood that it need not be.

On the seventh day she died. She did not go mad, not in the way the others did. She just lay down in the straw and put her head in my lap and looked at me with those flat, far-away eyes, and her breath got slower and slower, and then it stopped. I sat with her for a long time in the dark, and I do not mind saying that I cried.

When I went up into the yard at last, the city was quiet. The madness had passed as suddenly as it had come. The streets were being swept, and the dead were being buried, and the people were going about their business with white, shocked faces, as though they had all had the same bad dream. I have my cab still, but I have no horse, and I do not think I shall get another. There is something in me that has gone quiet, like Bess, and I do not think it will ever be quite right again. I keep thinking of that look in her eyes, and of the moan she made in the night, and I wonder what it was she saw, there in the dark of the cellar, with the whole city going mad above her. I wonder if she saw the bars, and if she understood, at the last, that I had only been trying to save her.
