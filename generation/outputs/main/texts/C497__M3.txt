The heat was the first thing to betray him. Elias had crossed the Mediterranean with a head full of Byron and a heart steeled by the marble severity of the Parthenon, but Greece, in the summer of 1822, offered no marble, only mud. The air in the Peloponnesian camp at Koryos was a thick, yellowish vapor that tasted of rotting figs and unwashed bodies. It clung to the pores, a physical weight that made the act of breathing a labor. He had come to fight for the absolute, inviolable right of a people to govern themselves, a cause so pure in the drawing rooms of London that it seemed to shimmer with its own divine light. Here, the light was merely the bruised, purple dusk of a mosquito-infested swamp, illuminating a squalid encampment of feuding captains.

Elias sat on an overturned olive cask, his back against the crumbling mud wall of a storage hut, attempting to write. The paper on his knee was damp, the ink bleeding slightly at the edges of his nib. He paused, listening to the distant, argumentative chatter of the Greek irregulars. They were not the harmonious brotherhood of liberty he had envisioned. They were klephts, mountain bandits whose allegiance shifted with the promise of loot, and Maniots who fought as fiercely against each other as against the Ottomans. Just that morning, Elias had witnessed two captains, Petrobey and a man named Grigorios, draw their yataghans over the distribution of a stolen Ottoman cargo ship. The cargo consisted of English manufactured goods, ironically paid for by London philanthropists, and the ensuing standoff had nearly fractured the entire western encampment. The cause of liberty, Elias was learning, was draped in the petty, provincial greed of men who viewed the war not as a nation’s awakening, but as an opportunity for local dominance.

He looked down at his half-finished letter. It was addressed to his sister, Clara, in Hertfordshire. She was the one who had fanned the flames of his idealism. Clara, with her sharp intellect and her circles of radical friends, had read him the pamphlets of the Philhellenes. She had spoken of the crossroads of civilization, of the duty of the enlightened to strike a blow against tyranny. She had practically packed his trunk herself. Now, staring at the ink, Elias felt a profound, exhausting disillusionment. He could not write to her of the glory of the struggle. He could only write of the reality, and the reality was a disease. 

Dysentery had swept through Koryos in late July. It took more men than the Ottoman cavalry ever had. Elias had spent the previous afternoon holding the hand of a young Athenian volunteer, a boy of sixteen who had recited Homer in the trenches, while the boy voided blood and wept for a mother who would never know how he died. The romance of war was a luxury afforded only to those who did not have to bury the dead in shallow, lime-covered pits. Elias dipped his pen again, the scratch of the nib the only sound in the stifling, darkening alley between the huts. 

In England, the autumn rains had begun, washing the streets of Hertfordshire with a clean, cold fury. Clara sat in the bay window of the parlor, a wool shawl drawn around her shoulders. The house was quiet. Her father was asleep in the study, and the servants were below stairs. The only sound was the ticking of the longcase clock in the hall and the crackle of the fire. Clara held Elias’s latest letter in her trembling hands. The paper felt foreign, brittle and slightly warped from its journey. She read the words again, though they were already seared into her mind. 

"The Greeks are fighting for their freedom," Elias had written, "but freedom, it seems, wears the face of a brigand. The camp is divided, Clara. The men follow captains, not constitutions. I have seen a man killed for a pair of Ottoman boots. The stench of this place is indescribable, a mixture of death and despair that no amount of Mediterranean wind can cleanse. I no longer see the poetry in the ruins. I see only the dust."

Clara pressed the letter to her lap, closing her eyes. The guilt was a tight, suffocating band around her chest. She remembered the afternoon he had told her of his decision. She had been so proud, so fiercely certain of their shared righteousness. "You will be part of history, Elias," she had told him, her eyes shining. "You will help birth a nation." She had believed it with the untested certainty of a woman who would never have to sleep in a trench or witness a boy bleeding to death in the mud. She had sent her brother into a slaughterhouse, armed with nothing but sonnets and philosophical abstractions. 

Elias set down his pen. The dusk had deepened into a dark, moonless night. In the distance, the sporadic crack of a musket echoed across the valley. It was probably nothing—a nervous sentry, or a private quarrel. He folded the letter, sealing it with a drop of wax. He would give it to the supply courier in the morning. He stood up, his joints aching, and walked to the edge of the camp. Below him, the valley was a black void, punctuated by the faint, scattered orange lights of distant villages. Somewhere out there, the Ottoman army was massing. The Greek commanders were arguing in their tents, drinking retsina and plotting against one another. 

Elias felt a profound emptiness. He was a stranger in a strange land, fighting for a cause that was crumbling into factionalism before his eyes. The great, romantic tide of the Philhellenic movement felt like a cruel joke played from afar. He thought of Clara, of her bright, earnest face, and he felt a sudden, sharp pang of resentment, followed quickly by a weary pity. She did not know. She could not know. She was safe in her parlor, believing in the perfection of the world, while he stood on the precipice of a brutal, chaotic reality. He turned back to the camp, to the mud and the disease, to the squalid, unromantic business of survival. The war was not a poem. It was a fever, and he was trapped inside it.

Clara folded the letter and placed it in the wooden box on her desk. She looked out the window at the rain-soaked English garden, the neatly trimmed hedgerows and the orderly stone paths. Everything here was so neat, so contained. She had thought the world was a puzzle to be solved with the right ideas, the right passions. Elias’s letters were dismantling that belief, word by word. She felt a sudden, overwhelming desire to write back, to tell him to come home, to abandon the foolish, bloody endeavor. But she knew she could not. He was bound by a duty she had instilled in him, a duty to a cause that was proving to be a mirage. She pulled the shawl tighter around her shoulders, staring into the fire, listening to the ticking of the clock, and waiting, with a growing, hollow dread, for the next letter to arrive.
