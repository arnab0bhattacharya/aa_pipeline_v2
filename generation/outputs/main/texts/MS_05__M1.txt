It was not in the first days of her residence at Wychwood that Clara felt the change, for then she was but a child, and the kindness of Mrs. Ashworth seemed as boundless and as natural as the light of heaven. The great house, with its ancient oaks and its prospect of the Hampshire downs, had been to her an earthly paradise; she had been received not as a dependant, but as a daughter, her poor parent’s dying request honoured with a tenderness that sought to obliterate the very memory of her orphan state. Mrs. Ashworth had placed her own little girl’s ribbons in Clara’s hair, and had called her “my sweet second hope,” and the words had sunk into the young girl’s grateful heart, there to be cherished as the most sacred of truths.

But a change, slow and terrible as the approach of winter, had of late crept over the spirit of the house. It began when her foster-brother, Edward, returned from his travels, no longer the careless boy who had taught her to ride, but a man, with a man’s ardent gaze and a voice that trembled when he spoke her name. When he first avowed his love, Clara felt a joy so pure and profound that it seemed a new faculty of her soul; yet even in that moment of exultation, a shadow fell upon her heart, for she had seen the look that passed across Mrs. Ashworth’s face—a look not of anger, but of a sudden, chilling dismay.

From that hour, the paradise of Wychwood became a gilded prison. Mrs. Ashworth did not forbid the attachment—her pride would not allow so vulgar a display of tyranny—but she began to remind Clara, gently and constantly, of the debt she owed. At first, the reminders were but as passing clouds over a summer sky. “It is a great thing, my dear Clara,” she would say, her hand resting lightly on the girl’s arm, “for one in your position to have been raised to a level with my own Edward. You must never forget the hand that lifted you.” Clara would protest her eternal gratitude, her voice thick with unshed tears, but the words seemed only to feed the very sentiment they sought to allay.

Then the comparisons began, delicate as frost, yet as piercing. When Clara, in a moment of girlish delight, praised a new gown, Mrs. Ashworth would sigh, “Ah, yes. It is a fine silk. I remember when my own dear mother gave me my first such dress—it was for my presentation at court. Your poor mother, I fear, could never have aspired to such things.” When Edward spoke of their future home, his mother would interject, “A modest establishment, no doubt, for a beginning. Edward’s wife will have to learn the virtues of economy, a lesson she may not have gathered in a house where everything has been provided from another’s bounty.” Each word was a link in a chain, not of iron, but of gossamer, which yet bound Clara’s spirit with a weight no iron could equal.

The storm in her soul found its parallel in the elements. One evening, as a tempest broke over the downs, rattling the ancient casements of Wychwood and making the very foundations of the house to tremble, Clara stood at the window of her chamber, gazing out at the chaos of wind and rain. The sublime fury of the scene was but a mirror to the tumult within her. Was she a creature of ingratitude? Had she, in daring to love Edward, forgotten the source of all her happiness? The question was a serpent, coiling in her breast. She saw herself now not as a daughter, but as a thing adopted, a stray bird fed from a rich man’s hand, and for this charity, she was to surrender her very self, to be reminded at every turn that her heart was not her own to give.

She resolved to speak with Edward, to lay bare the torment that was consuming her. She found him in the library, the storm still raging without. When she told him of his mother’s words, of the crushing weight of obligation that was laid upon her, his face, usually so open and joyful, became a mask of anguish. “She wounds you to part us!” he cried, his voice breaking. “She would have me wed Lady Beatrice, with her acres and her empty title! But she shall not succeed. Clara, my Clara, your love is the only wealth I desire.”

His passionate assurances were as balm to her wounded spirit, yet they could not heal the source of the hurt. The next day, Mrs. Ashworth summoned Clara to her morning room. The storm had passed, leaving the world washed clean and glittering, but the atmosphere within the house was heavy and still. Mrs. Ashworth sat by the fire, a letter in her hand.

“Clara,” she began, her tone one of weary resignation, “I have been speaking with Edward. He is infatuated, and I cannot reason with him. But you, who are sensible and good, you must see the impossibility of this match. Think, child, of the disparity. Think of the world’s opinion. It would be said that we had raised a beggar from the dust only to have her spit upon the hand that fed her.”

Clara felt the blood drain from her face. “Madam,” she whispered, “I have done nothing but love you and your son.”

“Love!” Mrs. Ashworth exclaimed, rising and pacing before the fire. “What is love against duty? Against gratitude? I gave you a home when you had none. I gave you a mother’s care when you were motherless. And this is my repayment—to see my son throw himself away, to see my family’s name linked with that of a girl who has nothing, absolutely nothing, but what she owes to my charity.” She stopped before Clara, her eyes, usually so calm, now burning with a cold fire. “I ask you, Clara, to release him. It is the only honourable thing you can do. It is the only way to repay the debt you can never, ever discharge.”

The words fell upon Clara not as a request, but as a judgment. The chain of obligation, which she had felt for so long, was now forged into a fetter, and its weight was the weight of her own life. She looked at Mrs. Ashworth, at the woman who had been her mother, and saw only a creditor, demanding a payment for which she had no coin. The love she bore for Edward, once a source of joy, now seemed a crime, a theft from the woman to whom she owed everything. In that moment of desolate clarity, she understood that she could not stay. To accept Edward would be to live as an ingrate; to refuse him would be to break her own heart. There was but one path left, a path into the wilderness of the world, alone.

Without a word, she turned and fled the room, her sobs catching in her throat. She ran through the silent, echoing halls of Wychwood, past the portraits of generations of Ashworths who looked down on her with cold, aristocratic eyes. She ran until she reached the great door, and, throwing it open, she rushed out into the bright, pitiless morning. The downs stretched before her, vast and empty, a sublime and terrible landscape that offered no comfort, only the promise of solitude. She did not look back at the great house that had been her paradise and her prison. She walked on, a small, solitary figure against the immense and indifferent sky, her only inheritance a debt that could never be paid, and a love that had become the instrument of her ruin.
