The rain in Paris did not fall so much as it descended, a grey, determined curtain that seemed to smother the very stones of the Rue de la Horloge. Inside the shop of Master Étienne Vauquelin, the air was thick with the smell of brass filings, whale oil, and the peculiar, stale dampness of a man who had spent fifty years listening to the heartbeat of the city. Étienne was a creature of angles and edges, his face a map of deep, intersecting furrows that might have been carved by the very chisels he used on his escapements. He sat hunched over his workbench, a magnifying glass screwed into his left eye, his right hand holding a pin so fine it looked as though it had been drawn from a spider’s leg. His apprentice, young Lucien, stood in the doorway, trembling not from the cold, which was bitter, but from the sheer, vibrating terror that hung in the room like a struck bell.

It was only an hour since the men in red had left. They had come with the swagger of those who believe they own the sun, the moon, and the minutes of the day. One of them, a man with a face like a bruised plum and a voice that sounded like gravel in a sack, had demanded that the Great Clock of the Hôtel de Ville be stopped. Not stopped, mind you, but *changed*. The numbers on the dial, those ancient, Roman, Christian, respectable numbers, were to be scraped off. In their place, they wanted the new calendar. The Republican Calendar. January was to become Vendémiaire, February Brumaire, and so on down the line, a new order of time to match the new order of blood and revolution. Étienne had looked at the man, through his magnifying glass, with an expression of such profound, mechanical boredom that the official had laughed, a dry, hacking sound.

"You are old, old man," the official had sneered. "Your head is full of the dust of kings. We are making a new world. The clock must serve the Republic, or you will serve the guillotine."

"I will serve the mechanism," Étienne had replied, his voice dry as old parchment. "The mechanism does not know the name of the month. It knows only the tooth and the gear. I will not touch the dial."

And so, here they were. The shop was dark, save for the single tallow candle that sputtered in the draught, casting long, dancing shadows that looked like the ghosts of all the hours ever lost. Lucien, who was only seventeen and had already learned that in Paris, one could lose a limb for a remark and a head for a silence, could not stand the stillness. He paced back and forth, his shoes squeaking on the floorboards.

"Master," Lucien whispered, breaking the silence that felt heavier than the rain outside. "Why? Why did you say no? They have the power. They have the cannon. They have the... the thing in the square."

Étienne did not look up. His hands, steady as stone, continued their delicate work, placing a jewel into the bearing of a tourbillon. "Lucien," he said, his voice low and rhythmic, like the ticking of a watch. "Do you think I said no because I love the kings? Do you think I said no because I care for the names of the months?"

Lucien stopped pacing. "Then why, Master? I am frightened. I am afraid for you, and I am afraid for me. If we do not change the clock, they will say we are enemies of the Revolution. They will say we are royalists in disguise."

"Let them say it," Étienne murmured. He finally looked up, and for a moment, the magnifying glass reflected a glint of light in his eye, a cold, fierce spark. "The clock is not a statement of politics, Lucien. It is a statement of truth. It is the one thing in this wretched, chaotic world that does not lie. When I wind the mainspring, I am not serving the King. I am not serving the Committee of Public Safety. I am serving the second. The second is eternal. It does not change because a man in a red cap shouts a new name at it. If I were to alter that dial, I would be breaking the law of physics. And if I break the law of physics, I break the law of God. And if I break the law of God, I am no longer a clockmaker. I am a forger. And a forger is a thief. He steals time from the people. He steals the truth from the face of the day."

Lucien frowned, his brow furrowing in confusion. "But the people... the people want the new calendar. They are told that the old calendar is the calendar of priests and tyrants."

"Then let them be told," Étienne said, a flash of indignation crossing his lined face. "Let them shout their new names into the void. But the sun will not rise on 'Vendémiaire' any more than it will on 'January'. The sun rises because the earth turns. The clock keeps time because the pendulum swings. It is a mechanical truth, Lucien. It is as hard and cold and unyielding as the iron of the guillotine. You cannot argue with a gear. You cannot persuade a spring. If I change the numbers, I am admitting that time is subjective. That truth is something that can be voted into existence by a committee. And if that is true, then we are all lost. We are all adrift in a sea of lies, where a man can be a hero one day and a criminal the next, and the only thing that remains fixed is the blade."

He picked up a small brush and dusted the face of the watch he was repairing. "I have kept the clocks of this city running through the Wars of Religion, through the Fronde, through the terror of the Terror, and through the reign of the Bastard. I have seen men come and go. I have seen empires rise and fall like the tides. But the clock? The clock remains. It is the anchor. It is the rope that holds us to the earth. If I let them change it, I let them change the ground beneath our feet. And when the ground shifts, Lucien, the whole building falls. Not just the clock. The shop. The street. The city. The world."

Lucien looked at the rain streaking the window, at the grey, indifferent sky. He thought of the official’s bruised-plum face, the way he had spoken of death as casually as a man speaking of the weather. He looked at Étienne, who was bent over his work again, his lips moving silently, counting the teeth of a wheel.

"But Master," Lucien said, his voice barely audible. "If they come back... if they force us..."

"Then we will see," Étienne said, not looking up. "But until then, the second ticks. And the second does not care for your politics. It does not care for your hunger. It does not care for your fear. It simply is. And so must we be. We are the keepers of the truth, Lucien. And the truth is the only thing they cannot hang."

The candle flared, then dimmed, as a gust of wind rattled the shutters. In the darkness, the tick-tock of the shop’s clocks continued, a steady, unbroken rhythm, indifferent to the storm outside, indifferent to the revolution, indifferent to the fear that gripped the hearts of men, but faithful, always faithful, to the only master that mattered: time itself. Étienne’s hands moved, swift and sure, placing the next gear into place, locking the truth into the mechanism, one tooth at a time.
