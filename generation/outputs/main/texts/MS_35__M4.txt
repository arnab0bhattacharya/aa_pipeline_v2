The candle guttered, and I set down my pen to trim the wick, for the night had grown deep and the silence of the town pressed upon my chamber windows like a living thing. I had been long at my labour, and my desk was strewn with the memorials of that great sorrow which it was my purpose to transmit to posterity: the bills of mortality, the physicians’ hurried notes, the letters of the bereaved, all the frail paper witnesses of a world that had well-nigh perished. For I, Edmund Ashworth, Fellow of St. Catherine’s in the town of Merrowford, had undertaken a task which I deemed sacred—to compose a faithful history of the Great Pestilence that had swept over England in the years of my grandfather’s youth, that those who came after might know the measure of the calamity and the manner of their deliverance.

My grandfather, Josiah Ashworth, had been a physician in this very town, and had kept a journal through the worst of the visitation; and from that journal, together with the public records and the testimony of the few survivors, I had laboured to reconstruct the whole. I had written of the first rumours from the eastern ports, of the shutting of the colleges, of the carts that went at night through the silent streets, and of the great pits beyond the abbey wall where the dead were laid in rows like sheaves at harvest. I had written of the courage of the few, and of the madness of the many; of the sermons preached to empty pews, and of the children who played at funerals in the ruined market-place. And I had come at last to the year of the abatement, when the pestilence, having spent its fury, withdrew as a tide withdraws, leaving the shore strewn with wreckage; and I had permitted myself to write of the slow return of hope, of the reopening of the colleges, of the first fair held again in the square, and of the Te Deum sung in the abbey on a morning of October, when the bells, long silent, rang out over a town that wept and laughed by turns.

It was at this point of my narrative that I had paused to trim my candle; and as I paused, I became aware of a sound in the street below—a hurried footfall, then another, then a knocking at a distant door, and voices subdued yet agitated. I rose and went to the window. The night was clear and cold, and the moon rode high above the chapel roof; and in the street I saw three figures standing together, of whom one held a lantern, and their heads were close together as though they conferred upon some matter of dread. Presently one of them broke away and ran towards the house of Doctor Ellwood, the physician, and beat upon the door with the flat of his hand, calling his name aloud; and the door was opened, and the doctor came forth in his nightcap, and there was much earnest speech, and gestures of dismay.

A coldness, not of the night air, crept over me. I knew not why, but my heart began to beat with a strange and terrible anticipation, as though some hidden sense within me had already divined the truth which my reason refused to entertain. I returned to my desk, and my eyes fell upon the page before me, where I had written of the joy of the town at its deliverance; and the words seemed to me, in that moment, the words of a fool or a madman.

I took up my grandfather’s journal, which lay beside my manuscript, and turned the leaves with a hand that trembled. I sought the entries of the first days of the sickness, when the nature of the malady was yet unknown; and I read how the patients had been seized with a sudden languor, with a flush upon the cheek and a strange brightness of the eye, with a cough that yielded no phlegm, and a fever that mounted swiftly to delirium; and how, in the space of three days, the greater number had died. And I read how my grandfather had noted, in a hand that shook with horror, that the contagion seemed to pass not only from the sick to the sound, but through the very air itself, so that whole households were struck down together, and the physician who entered a sick-chamber came forth already doomed.

I laid down the book, and my hands were cold as ice. For I had heard that morning, in the market, a rumour which I had dismissed as idle—that a sailor lately come from the port of Gravesend had been taken ill at the inn, and had died in a strange manner, and that the landlord’s daughter, who had tended him, was now likewise sick. And I had laughed at the rumour, and had said that in these days of sanitation and science such things were impossible; and I had gone home to write of the deliverance of the town from a pestilence that could never return.

But now, as I sat in the silence, the truth came upon me with the force of a revelation, and I saw, as in a vision, the whole fabric of our security crumble like a house of cards. I saw the colleges emptied again, the streets deserted, the carts going at night, the pits reopened beyond the abbey wall. I saw the faces of my friends, and I knew that I should see them no more. And I saw myself, alone in my chamber, writing the history of a catastrophe that was even then beginning anew—writing it, it might be, for no eyes but those of the dead.

I started from my chair and went again to the window. The group in the street had dispersed, but lights were moving in the windows of the college, and I heard the sound of many feet in the quadrangle below. There was a knocking at my own door. I opened it, and my friend, Henry Vaughan, the junior dean, stood upon the threshold, pale and breathless, with a paper in his hand.

“Ashworth,” he said, “have you heard? It is the sickness—the same—the very same. Two dead in the town since sunset, and seven sick. The doctor has sent to London for instructions, but there is no one in London to give them. The pestilence has returned.”

He spoke, and I stood silent, gazing at him with a countenance which, I doubt not, was as pale as his own. For I thought of my manuscript upon the desk, and of the fair words I had written of hope and deliverance, and it seemed to me that I had been engaged upon a mockery and a lie.

“You are silent,” said Vaughan. “Do you not believe it?”

“I believe it,” I answered, “and I have been forewarned. I have been writing the history of the pestilence, and it has risen from the page to confront me. The past is become the present, and the dead are the teachers of the living. God help us all.”

He looked at me as at one who raved; but I took him by the arm and drew him to my desk, and showed him the journal of my grandfather, and the entries of the first days of the sickness; and as he read, his face grew graver still, and he said at last, in a low voice:

“Then we know what is to come. We have the map of our own destruction.”

“We have,” I said; “and it is a fearful thing to know. But it is something, too, to know; for the men of that former time walked in darkness, and we walk in the light, though it be the light of a funeral torch. Let us go forth, then, and do what may be done; and let us keep such records as shall serve those who come after, if any come after, to know how men bore themselves when the world was ending.”

So saying, I closed my manuscript, and took my hat and cloak; and we went down together into the street, where the lanterns were already moving towards the house of the sick, and the bells of the abbey began to toll, slow and solemn, through the silence of the night.
