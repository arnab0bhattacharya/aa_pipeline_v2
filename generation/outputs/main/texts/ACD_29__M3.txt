It was upon a raw November morning, with a thick drizzle sweeping in from the German Ocean, that I first set eyes upon Ravenscar Keep. The edifice stood upon the very edge of the Northumberland cliffs, a stark, grey pile of stone bristling against the weather, as though bracing itself against the relentless northern wind. I had been summoned thither by a letter bearing the desperate seal of Miss Eleanor Vane, a young woman of my slight acquaintance, who had lately come into an unexpected inheritance.

I was met at the heavy oak door by Miss Vane herself. She was a figure of striking pallor, her dark eyes betraying the sleepless nights she had endured. She drew me hastily into the dimly lit entrance hall, where a peat fire smoked sullenly in a vast grate.

“Mr. Penhaligon,” said she, her voice low and trembling, “I am most grateful that you have come. I had nowhere else to turn. You see, they believe me an impostor.”

She gestured about the hall, and I observed that we were not alone. A rigid, grey-haired man of middle age, dressed in the sombre black of a butler, stood observing us from the shadows of the staircase. His face was set in an expression of profound and uncompromising distrust.

“That is Grimsby,” Miss Vane whispered. “He has served the Ravenscar family for forty years. Since my arrival three days ago, he has refused to acknowledge my authority, and the maids follow his lead. They will not bring me meals unless I ring for them in the presence of witnesses, and they speak of me in the corridors as ‘the pretender.’”

“It is a grave accusation for a household to level against its mistress,” I remarked, glancing sharply at the butler, who retreated a pace into the gloom. “Upon what do they base their suspicions?”

“Upon the suddenness of my claim, and the death of Mr. Tibbett, the family solicitor. My uncle, Sir Hesketh Vane, perished in a fall from these very cliffs a fortnight ago. I am his only living relative. Mr. Tibbett had written to me in London to say that he possessed documents proving my lineage beyond dispute. But before I could arrive, Tibbett was found dead in his office at Alnwick. The doctor declared it an apoplexy of the heart. Without Tibbett’s testimony, the household has closed ranks against me.”

I considered the matter for a moment. “Is there no other evidence, Miss Vane?”

“Only that,” she answered, pointing to the high, panelled wall opposite the fire.

I followed her gesture and beheld a portrait of considerable size, executed in the heavy, sombre style of the early century. It depicted a young woman seated in a high-backed chair, dressed in a gown of crimson silk. The likeness to Miss Vane was, indeed, nothing short of startling. The same dark, earnest eyes, the same proud arch of the brow, and the singular curve of the mouth were present in the canvas. It was as though my companion had stepped bodily from the frame.

“That is my grandmother, Lady Arabella Vane,” Miss Vane explained. “The resemblance is a mere trick of blood, Mr. Penhaligon, but it avails me nothing here. Grimsby declares it is a common likeness, and that many a village girl might bear it to deceive a stranger.”

I walked to the portrait and examined it closely. The varnish was cracked with age, and a thin film of dust lay upon the surface. As I leaned in, my attention was caught by something far more pertinent than the painted features. The lower edge of the canvas appeared slightly uneven, and there was a faint, fresh scratch upon the gilded frame. I pressed my thumb against the lower right corner, and to my surprise, the panel gave way slightly. It was hinged.

“Who has access to this hall during the day?” I asked, turning sharply.

“Only Grimsby and the housekeeper, Mrs. Hockley. Why do you ask?”

“Because this portrait has been disturbed very recently,” I said. “The dust has been wiped away from the frame’s edge, and the panel is loose. I suspect it conceals a hollow space behind the canvas.”

At that moment, the butler advanced into the light. His face was flushed, and his hands were clenched at his sides.

“You have no right to touch the property of Ravenscar,” he cried, his voice shaking with a sudden, barely contained fury. “You are a fraud and a adventuress, and I will not see you pilfer the master’s secrets!”

“Grimsby!” Miss Vane recoiled, visibly startled by the sudden outburst.

The man’s sudden vehemence was a revelation in itself. I stepped between him and Miss Vane, holding his gaze steadily.

“You are very protective of this particular piece of property, Grimsby,” I observed quietly. “So protective, in fact, that you have been unable to conceal your alarm. Tell me, what lies behind this portrait? Is it the proof of Miss Vane’s identity, or is it something else entirely?”

The butler’s jaw tightened. For a moment, I believed he would strike me. Then, with a visible effort, he mastered himself and stood rigid once more.

“There is nothing behind it but old stonework,” he muttered.

“We shall see,” I replied. “Miss Vane, I must ask you to step back.”

I took out my penknife and inserted the blade into the slight aperture at the corner of the panel. With a gentle prising motion, the concealed door swung open, revealing a shallow cavity within the thickness of the wall. From this recess, I drew a small, leather-bound writing case, sealed with a red wax stamp. The seal had been recently broken and clumsily re-pressed.

I turned the case over in my hands. It bore the initials “H. V.” for Sir Hesketh Vane.

“I believe, Grimsby,” said I, watching the man’s face closely, “that Mr. Tibbett did not die of an apoplexy, and that he did not die with his business concluded. I believe he came here to place this case for safe-keeping, and that someone broke the seal, hoping to find and destroy the documents within.”

The butler’s face went ashen. His eyes darted towards the front door, then back to me, like a cornered animal.

“You know nothing of it,” he snarled, but the bluster had entirely drained from his voice.

“On the contrary,” I returned, opening the case. Inside, I found a folded parchment and a sealed letter. The parchment was a certified copy of Miss Vane’s birth registry, authenticated by the parish of St. Jude’s in London, and a sworn affidavit from the late Mr. Tibbett, detailing her right to inherit. “Someone opened this case hoping to destroy these very papers. Finding them written in a hand they could not readily imitate, and sealed with legal formalities they could not easily forge, they were forced to leave them be and content themselves with the death of the solicitor.”

I looked steadily at Grimsby. “You were seen, Grimsby. A fisherman on the shore below observed a man of your description meeting Mr. Tibbett upon the cliff path on the afternoon of his death. The local constabulary has been making discreet inquiries. They have been waiting only for a witness to connect the meeting at Ravenscar with the sudden death at Alnwick.”

It was a bold assertion, delivered with an air of unwavering certainty, though I had, in truth, no such witness. It was a calculated blow, struck at the psychological moment. The effect was instantaneous. The butler’s rigid composure shattered.

“It was not meant to kill!” he cried out, sinking into a chair and burying his face in his hands. “He was going to acknowledge her! I begged him to wait, to search further for a male heir, but he was inflexible. I only meant to detain him upon the path, to plead with him once more. He stumbled, and the loose gravel gave way beneath his feet. God forgive me, I swear he fell by accident!”

Miss Vane gasped, but I kept my eyes fixed upon the broken man.

“And the case?” I prompted.

“He had placed it there that very morning,” Grimsby sobbed. “He told me he had made provision for the estate. I found the panel after he left for the cliffs. I broke the seal, intending to burn the papers, but they were too formal, too legally bound. I knew that if I destroyed them, the courts would make inquiries. I thought if Tibbett were silenced, the matter would rest in limbo, and the estate would fall to the next of kin, my wife, who is a distant cousin of Sir Hesketh.”

The confession, once begun, poured forth in a torrent. The man was broken, his loyalty to the old name having curdled into a desperate, criminal attempt to manipulate the inheritance. I sent a note to the constabulary in the village, and by noon, Grimsby was taken into custody.

As the police cart rattled away down the long drive, Miss Vane and I stood together in the hall, the writing case resting upon the table between us. The storm had broken, and a thin, pale sunlight was beginning to touch the sea below.

“It is a melancholy inheritance, Mr. Penhaligon,” she said quietly, looking at the portrait of her grandmother. “But it is mine.”

“It is yours,” I agreed, replacing the documents in their case. “And you may rest assured that Ravenscar Keep will trouble you no more.”
