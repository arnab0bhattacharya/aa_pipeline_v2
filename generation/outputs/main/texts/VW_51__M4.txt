The gates of the gardens, iron and ceremonious, clanged shut behind her with a sound that was final and yet, at the same time, freeing; and immediately the noise of the Bayswater Road—that grinding, hooting, restless tide—fell away, became a murmur merely, a distant hum, so that it seemed to Margaret she had stepped not into a park but into a room, a vast green room with the sky for its ceiling, and the trees, in their first pale gauze of leaf, standing about like guests who had arrived early and did not quite know what to do with themselves.

The boy—his name was Peter, though she called him Bunny more often than not, a name that would embarrass him dreadfully in a few years’ time, she supposed, and yet she could not stop herself—the boy shot from her side the instant the gate was passed, a small projectile of navy-blue coat and bare knees, making for the Round Pond with the unerring instinct of a pigeon for home. She watched him go, his legs pumping, his arms outspread as if he were an aeroplane, and felt the familiar double pull, the cord that stretched between her breastbone and his small receding back, slackening now, now tautening, and thought, as she had thought a thousand times, how strange it was to love anyone so much and to be so continually, so blessedly, released from them.

She found a bench. It was her bench, or so she had come to think of it, though of course it belonged to the Commissioners of Works, and to the old men who fed the pigeons, and to the nursemaids with their perambulators, and to the lovers who sat too close together on summer evenings. But this morning it was hers, and she settled into it with a little sigh, arranging her skirts, taking off her gloves finger by finger, and lifting her face to the sun, which came through the branches in coins and splinters of light, warm on her eyelids, so that she might have been a cat, or a lizard, or any creature whose whole business is to be still and to be warm.

The pond was a sheet of tin, hammered and shining, and upon it the toy boats—the *Endeavour*, the *Swallow*, the *Golden Hind*—dipped and curtseyed, their sails bellying, their little flags erect. Bunny was already in conversation with another boy, a stout child in a red jersey, and they were launching something—a stick, a leaf, a piece of bark—with immense seriousness, as if the fate of empires hung upon the current. Margaret watched them, and then she did not watch them, for her eyes had gone beyond them, to the water, and to the light upon the water, and then, insensibly, to somewhere else entirely.

Her mother. Her mother had brought her here, to these very gardens, thirty years ago, or was it thirty-five?—the arithmetic of memory was always so unreliable—and had sat, perhaps upon this very bench, in a dress of grey alpaca, with a hat that had a bird upon it, or was it fruit?—and had watched her, Margaret, in a white pinafore, sailing her own boat, a poor thing of tin and paint, and had thought—what? What had her mother thought, sitting here, with the sun on her face and the child at the water’s edge? Had she thought of her own mother, and of the person she had meant to become? Had she felt this same slackening and tautening, this same sweet and terrible pull?

It was impossible to know. Her mother had been a woman of few words, and those few, towards the end, had been mostly about the draught under the door and the price of coal. And yet there had been a moment—Margaret could not have been more than seven—when her mother had taken her hand, here, on the path by the pond, and had said, quite suddenly, as if the thought had escaped her against her will: “I wanted to be a painter, once. Before I married your father. I wanted to paint the light on water.” And then she had coloured, and had said something about the wind being cold, and had buttoned Margaret’s coat up to her chin, and never spoken of it again.

The light on the water. Margaret looked at it now, the shifting, sliding, liquid light, and thought of her mother’s hands—those capable, chapped, reddened hands that had peeled potatoes and darned socks and held her own small hands so firmly—and wondered whether they had ever held a brush, whether they had ever known the particular joy of a colour laid down just so, and she felt a pang, sharp and sweet, for all the paintings that had never been painted, all the light that had gone unrecorded, and she thought, I must write to Hugh about this, I must tell him—and then she remembered that Hugh was in Manchester, or was it Leeds?, and that he would not understand, not really, for Hugh was a man who dealt in facts and figures and the sensible arrangement of things, and who loved her, she knew, in his own way, which was a way of asking whether the boiler had been seen to and whether she had remembered to order the coal.

The book. She had meant to write a book. Not a novel—she had no patience for the invention of characters, for the elaborate machinery of plot and counterplot—but something else, something that would catch the thing itself, the look of the light on the water, the sound of the gates clanging shut, the feel of a child’s hand in yours, warm and sticky and trusting. She had begun it, once, in a notebook with a marbled cover, and had written three pages, and had put it away in the drawer of the writing-desk, and had not opened the drawer for—how long? Two years? Three? The notebook was still there, she knew, under the box of stationery and the tins of stamps and the letters she had meant to answer. She could see it, in her mind’s eye, the marbled cover, the three pages, the rest blank, waiting. And she thought, with a sudden, fierce, unreasonable resentment, of all the hours of her life that had been spent in doing things that left no trace—the meals prepared and eaten and forgotten, the beds made and unmade, the small crises of childhood smoothed over and consigned to oblivion—and she thought, I am forty-two years old, and what have I made? What have I done?

But then Bunny turned, and saw her, and waved, his face one wide delighted beam, and shouted something that was lost upon the wind, and she waved back, and the resentment dissolved, or rather it was transformed, became something else, something that had in it both joy and grief, both pride and a kind of sorrow, for he would grow up, this boy, he would go away, he would forget her, he would become a man with a life of his own, and she would be—what? A photograph on a mantelpiece. A name spoken less and less often. A woman in a grey alpaca dress, sitting on a bench in Kensington Gardens, watching the light on the water.

“Mrs. Ashby! Mrs. Ashby! How delightful!”

It was Clara Winterbourne, advancing along the path with her little dog—a Pekinese, all fur and indignation—tugging at its lead. Clara was a woman whom Margaret had known since girlhood, and whom she liked, and whom she sometimes found unendurable, for Clara had never married, had never had children, had never, so far as Margaret could tell, wanted anything that she had not been able to obtain, and there was about her a briskness, a competence, a kind of cheerful certainty that made Margaret feel, by contrast, vague and formless and perpetually in the wrong.

“Clara! How nice.” Margaret made room on the bench, and Clara sat, and the dog sat, and there was a flurry of gloves and parasols and how-are-yous, and then Clara said, “And how is the little man? Growing like a weed, I see. And Mr. Ashby? Still in the North?”

“Still in the North,” said Margaret.

“And you—you are well? You look well. A little tired, perhaps. You must not let the boy wear you out. They are little vampires, you know, at that age. They drain one.”

Margaret laughed, and said something about the fresh air, and the spring, and how lovely the gardens were in April, and Clara agreed, and spoke of the concert at the Queen’s Hall, and of the new novel everyone was reading, and of the shocking price of butter, and Margaret listened, and nodded, and made the appropriate sounds, and all the while, beneath the words, beneath the chatter, she was thinking: she does not know. She does not know about the notebook, or the light on the water, or my mother’s hands. She does not know that I am not here, not really, that I am somewhere else, somewhere I cannot describe, even to myself.

And then Bunny came running, his cheeks flushed, his hands muddy, his eyes bright with some urgent news about a boat, a race, a boy called Wilfred who had a boat with a real sail, a sail that worked, and Margaret said, “Clara, you must excuse me,” and rose, and took his hand, and felt the cord between them tauten and slacken, tauten and slacken, and she thought, This is it. This is the thing itself. This is what I would write, if I could write it. And she walked with him to the water’s edge, and knelt beside him, and helped him launch his stick, his leaf, his piece of bark, and watched it drift out upon the shining surface, and did not think of anything at all.
