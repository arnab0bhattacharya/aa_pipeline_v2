The city of Auren Vey was built on a hill of pale stone, and on the night the two boys were born, a comet hung over the palace like a drawn sword. In the great house on the eastern slope, where the windows were glazed and the floors were warmed by hypocausts, the rich merchant Cassian Vore received his firstborn son and named him Lucien, for the light. In the warren of tenements by the river, where the fog smelled of tallow and wet rope, the laundress Maren Holt delivered her third child and named him Corin, for no reason she could later remember except that she liked the sound of it. Both boys drew their first breath within the same hour, and the comet, indifferent, slid westward over the roofs.

The fairy came at midnight, as fairies do when they come at all. She was small and grey and smelled faintly of rain on hot stone, and she stood at the foot of the two cradles in two distant rooms at once, which is the sort of thing that only happens once in a great while and is never believed afterward. To the child in the glazed house she gave beauty—not the ordinary beauty of a well-favored babe, but the kind that would make strangers fall silent and then speak too quickly to cover it. To the child in the tenement she gave a kind heart, which is a gift harder to see and harder to keep. Then she was gone, and the comet passed, and the city went about its business.

Lucien Vore grew up as beautiful children do, which is to say that the world arranged itself around him without his ever noticing the labor. By seven he had learned that a certain tilt of the head could end an argument; by twelve, that his face opened doors before his hand reached them. His father hired tutors in rhetoric and swordplay and the history of the city's founding families, and Lucien excelled at all of it, though whether from talent or from the tireless encouragement of everyone he met was a question no one asked. He was not cruel. Cruelty requires attention to other people's pain, and Lucien had never needed to pay attention to anyone. He was simply and serenely certain that the world was a well-made machine and that he stood at its center, and this certainty sat on him as gracefully as his fine grey cloak.

Corin Holt grew up in the tenements, and the gift of a kind heart is a troublesome thing in a poor quarter. It meant he gave his bread to the beggar at the bridge and went hungry; it meant he carried water for old Teva on the fourth floor when his own back ached; it meant he wept at things other boys laughed at and laughed at things that wounded him. His mother watched him and worried, for she knew what the world did to kind men. He was apprenticed to a cooper and was not especially good at it, being prone to giving away finished barrels to families who could not pay, and his master finally let him go with a sigh and a coin and the advice to find work that did not require a head for profit. So Corin carried messages, and ran errands, and by twenty he was lean and quick and knew every alley in Auren Vey, and people said of him, without quite knowing why, that if you were in trouble you should find Corin Holt.

In the twentieth year, the king of Auren Vey announced that his daughter would choose a husband from among the young men of the city, and that any man of good standing might present himself at the palace gate on the morning of the choosing. The city boiled with it. Tailors raised their prices; fathers raised their hopes; young men oiled their hair and practiced speeches to the moon. Lucien Vore, who had met the princess once at a winter feast and had seen her look at him twice, considered the matter settled. His father bought him a cloak of silver-grey wool and a horse the color of cream, and on the morning of the choosing Lucien rode up the hill to the palace with his chin lifted and the comet's old promise shining in his face.

Corin Holt had no thought of princesses. He had been sent up the hill that morning with a satchel of letters for a clerk in the palace kitchens—the cooper's widow, who could not read, had a son in the guard and needed a letter carried—and he was climbing the long stair to the gate, keeping to the edge of the crowd, when he saw the old woman fall.

She went down at the foot of the gate itself, in the press of bodies, and the crowd surged around her the way crowds do, everyone looking and no one stopping. She was old and thin and her basket of turnips had gone rolling down the steps, and her mouth worked as she tried to get her breath. Corin did not think about the letters or the guard or the princess or the fact that he was nobody in a crowd of somebody's sons. He knelt and got his arm under her and lifted her out of the flow of feet, and he carried her to the shade of the gate arch and set her down and fetched her turnips one by one from the steps, and he stayed with her until her breath came even, and he gave her the water from his own flask and the last of the bread in his satchel, which he had been saving for his dinner.

At the gate, on the cream-colored horse, Lucien Vore looked down and saw a lean young man in a patched coat kneeling in the dust over a turnip-woman, and felt a flicker of something he could not name—not contempt, exactly, but the mild discomfort of a man watching someone do a thing he had never once thought of doing. He looked away. The gate opened. The horns sounded, and Lucien rode through with the crowd cheering his beauty, and the princess, watching from a high window, saw him and caught her breath, as everyone always did.

But Corin did not see any of that. He was helping the old woman to her feet, and she was pressing a turnip into his hands and calling him a good boy, and he was laughing and telling her he had no use for turnips, and she was insisting, and the horns were sounding and the crowd was cheering and the two young men—one riding in and one walking out—passed within a dozen feet of each other at the palace gate and did not meet at all.

The fairy, who was small and grey and smelled of rain, sat on the wall above the gate and watched them both go. She had given one a face and the other a heart, and she had known, even then, which gift the world would reward and which it would use up. She did not interfere. Fairies do not interfere; they only give, and then they watch, and sometimes—though not often, and never where anyone can see—they weep.
