Miss Helena Fortescue had never been a young woman to alter her resolutions, and her determination to withhold her approbation from her brother's intended bride was not lightly to be shaken. That Mr. Edward Fortescue, a young man of excellent understanding and unblemished character, should have suffered himself to be captivated by a Miss Clara Vernon, was a circumstance which his sister could only lament as a grievous misalliance of temperaments. Miss Vernon's history, as far as it had reached Surrey, bespoke a young woman trained in the superficial accomplishments of town, who danced with extraordinary spirit and spoke with even more; who wore ribbons of an unnecessarily vivid hue; and who had, upon one reported occasion, laughed quite uproariously in a public assembly. Helena, who found her own quiet felicity at Woodcroft sufficiently ample, and who considered a measured deportment as the first of female virtues, could not but feel that her brother was throwing himself away on a girl whose conversation could never rise above the cut of a pelisse or the price of a feather.

It was in this frame of mind that she received the intelligence of Miss Vernon's impending visit to Woodcroft. She declared her intention, with a calmness that partook more of stubbornness than of philosophy, of confining herself to her own apartment during the greater part of the visit. She would not, she protested, be a party to the encouragement of a connection she could not approve; and though Mrs. Fortescue, their widowed mother, gently represented that civility demanded some acknowledgement of their future relation, Helena remained resolute in her conviction that she could not, with any truth, welcome a woman whose disposition she had already condemned.

Miss Vernon arrived on a Tuesday in October, attended by a maid and a quantity of luggage which Helena, from her window, observed with silent disapprobation. She did not descend to the drawing-room. Edward, who had ridden over to meet his betrothed, was evidently hurt by his sister's absence, but he was too much engrossed by the pleasure of conducting Miss Vernon into the house to press the point. Helena heard the murmur of voices below, a light laugh, and the rustle of silk on the stairs, and drawing her chair nearer to the fire, opened her book with a settled air of indifference which she hoped might convey, to any who observed it, the strength of her disapprobation.

For two days she maintained this seclusion, emerging only to take a solitary walk in the garden before breakfast, when she could be certain of encountering no one. Her mother bore this conduct with the patience of a woman who had long since discovered that Helena's opinions, once formed, were not to be dispersed by argument. Edward's letters, which he left on her dressing-table, spoke with some warmth of Clara's sweetness, her animation, and her eagerness to recommend herself to his family; but Helena read these effusions with a sceptical eye, attributing them to the blindness of a lover rather than to the merits of the beloved.

On the third morning, however, the course of events was abruptly altered. Edward, who had ridden out in a sharp rain to inspect a distant farm, returned home in a high fever, shivering and scarcely able to dismount. The surgeon from the village was sent for, and pronounced the complaint to be an inflammatory fever of a kind that would require careful nursing. Mrs. Fortescue, whose health was never robust, was persuaded with some difficulty to resign the chamber; and Helena, upon whom the duty naturally fell, prepared to take her station at her brother's bedside with that grave sense of obligation which distinguished her character.

She had not, however, reckoned upon Miss Vernon.

She found her already there. Miss Vernon was seated on the far side of the bed, her muslin gown covered by a plain apron, engaged in stirring some gruel with an attention which argued no trifling acquaintance with the office. She looked up as Helena entered, and the expression of her countenance was so wholly free from embarrassment or defiance that Helena was, for a moment, deprived of any pretext for displeasure.

"I hope I do not intrude," said Miss Vernon, rising. "Edward—that is, Mr. Fortescue—was restless, and I thought someone ought to be with him. Mrs. Fortescue was looking so pale that I ventured to send her to lie down."

Helena could not, in conscience, find fault with this arrangement, however much she might have wished to. She took her seat on the opposite side in silence, and for some minutes they sat thus, divided by the narrow bed and the laboured breathing of the sufferer, each occupied with her own thoughts. Helena, who had expected to be annoyed, found herself instead in the peculiar position of having nothing to say. Miss Vernon, for her part, did not force conversation. She adjusted the blanket, felt her patient's pulse with a hand whose steadiness spoke of more than common resolution, and once, when Edward stirred and muttered, leaned forward to bathe his forehead with a cloth, murmuring something so low and gentle that Helena, though she caught the words, could hardly believe they came from the same young woman who was reported to have laughed in a public assembly.

The hours passed. The surgeon returned, and his directions, which were various and exacting, were received by Miss Vernon with an attention which Helena, in spite of herself, could not but approve. She did not flutter; she did not exclaim; she asked one or two questions which betrayed a practical intelligence that no report of ribbons and feathers had ever led Helena to expect. When the gruel was rejected, she did not despair, but prepared a fresh draught with a composure that seemed to belong to a woman of twice her years.

It was towards evening, as the light faded and the candle threw long shadows across the counterpane, that Helena's reserve began to yield. She had observed, throughout the afternoon, that Miss Vernon had not once quitted the room, had not once spoken of herself, and had not once, by look or gesture, suggested that her situation in the house was anything but secure. This last observation struck Helena with particular force, for she knew that her own coldness must have been felt, and she could not but admire a temper which could sustain neglect without resentment.

"You have been very constant," she said, the words escaping her before reflection could restrain them. "You must be greatly fatigued."

"I am not in the least fatigued," replied Miss Vernon, meeting her gaze with a directness which was as far from boldness as it was from timidity. "I could not be elsewhere. But I am sensible of your kindness in saying so, Miss Fortescue, for I know that my being here is—not what you would have wished."

Helena coloured. The speech was too honest to be met with evasion, and too gently delivered to be resented. She was silent for a moment, and then, with an effort which cost her something, she said: "I have perhaps been hasty in my judgment. I formed my opinion from report, which is seldom a just foundation. I beg you will not think I am insensible of—" She stopped, finding the complete confession harder to utter than she had anticipated, and finished rather lamely, "of your attention to my brother."

Miss Vernon's eyes filled, but she answered with a steadiness which did her credit. "I shall not think so. I have long wished for your good opinion, Miss Fortescue, for Edward's sake; but I would rather earn it by deserving it than solicit it from politeness."

From that moment, a tacit compact of friendship was established between them. They nursed Edward by turns, and in the intervals of watching, when he slept, they talked—quietly at first, and then with increasing freedom—of books, of music, of the small concerns of country life. Helena discovered that Miss Vernon's acquaintance with literature was wider than her own, that her remarks, though delivered with an ease which had perhaps been mistaken for levity, were founded on observation and reflection, and that the vivid ribbons, which had so offended her imagination, were the innocent indulgence of a generous and youthful spirit, rather than the badges of a frivolous one.

When Edward's fever broke, on the fifth morning, and he opened his eyes and whispered Clara's name, it was Miss Vernon's hand that he sought; but it was Helena who, pressing the other, murmured, with a conviction she no longer wished to conceal, that he had chosen well.
