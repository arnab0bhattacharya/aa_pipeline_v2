In the parish of St. Buttolph’s-without-the-Gate, where the air was a perpetual, greasy soup of coal-smoke, boiled beef, and damp flannel, there stood a little cookshop belonging to the widow Mrs. Pettifer. It was not a handsome shop; its windows were dimmed by the breath of a thousand hurried dinners, and a bell above its door announced every customer with a sound like a startled cough. Yet it was a prosperous concern, for Mrs. Pettifer possessed that rare combination of a heavy hand with a rolling-pin and a light one with the scales, and her savings, accumulated in a series of teapots and tucked into the linings of her gowns, were a subject of quiet, yet intense, speculation in the neighbourhood. Mrs. Pettifer herself was a woman of monumental proportions and equally monumental ambitions; she looked upon the world as a vast kitchen in which she had, as yet, only been given charge of the vegetables.

Into this scene of culinary commerce and unspoken avarice there stepped the imposing figure of Mr. Bartholomew Grudge. He was the parish constable, a man whose uniform was so tight across the chest that it seemed less a garment than a declaration of his own importance. Mr. Grudge moved through the parish not as a servant of the law, but as its living embodiment, a walking, talking monument to the principle of order. He had a face like a boiled pudding, pale and puffy, from which two small, suspicious eyes peered out as if from behind fortifications. His voice was a low, authoritative rumble, and his chief pleasure in life was the pronouncement of his own name and title in the most resonant tones possible. ‘Grudge, of St. Buttolph’s!’ he would boom at a startled pickpocket, or ‘Constable Grudge, at your service, ma’am,’ with a bow that was all chest and no bend.

Mr. Grudge, it must be confessed, was not a wealthy man. His pay was a pittance, and his uniform, though magnificent, was his only asset. He had surveyed the parish and its inhabitants with the cold eye of a matrimonial strategist, and his gaze had fallen upon Mrs. Pettifer’s teapots. Here, he reasoned, was a solid foundation upon which to build the latter half of his life: a warm hearth, a well-stocked larder, and a wife whose respect for his office would be as substantial as her own figure. And so, he became a regular patron of the cookshop, consuming innumerable pork pies and jellied eels, and paying for them with a ponderous gravity that he felt must surely impress his hostess.

Mrs. Pettifer, for her part, saw in Mr. Grudge a magnificent prize. Here was not merely a man, but an Institution! To be the wife of the Constable of St. Buttolph’s would be to ascend from the greasy smoke of the cookshop to the very pinnacle of parish respectability. She imagined herself sweeping into church on his arm, the other wives shrinking back in awe of her silk bonnet and her husband’s brass buttons. She did not desire Mr. Grudge for himself—his conversation was as dry as a biscuit and his affections seemed directed entirely towards his own reflection—but for the glorious, shining fact of his office. Their courtship was therefore a masterpiece of mutual deception, a carefully choreographed dance where he praised her ‘substantial comforts’ and she admired his ‘commanding presence,’ each speaking a truth that was entirely beside the point.

The arrangement, once made, was as formal and joyless as a summons. They were to be married at St. Buttolph’s on a Tuesday morning. The day dawned, as London days often did, in a shroud of grey fog that seemed to muffle the very church bells. Mr. Grudge arrayed himself in his most ceremonial uniform, a garment so stiff with pipeclay and authority that he could barely sit down. He proceeded to the cookshop to collect his bride, his heart filled not with love, but with the quiet satisfaction of a man who has successfully concluded a long and difficult piece of business.

He found Mrs. Pettifer in a state of tremendous agitation, her face the colour of a rare roast. She was wearing a new bombazine gown that creaked with every breath, and a bonnet of such architectural complexity that it required a system of pins and wires to keep it balanced. ‘Mr. Grudge,’ she cried, as he entered, ‘a terrible discovery! A positive calamity!’

‘Compose yourself, madam,’ he boomed, his voice echoing in the small parlour. ‘A constable is a rock in the tempest. What is the nature of the disturbance?’

‘It is my cousin, Mr. Flinch,’ she wailed. ‘He has been to the bank! He has made enquiries! He says—oh, it is too dreadful to speak!—he says my little nest-egg, my poor hard-won savings, have all been swallowed up by the failure of the Smotherwell & Soot Coal Company! Every penny! I am a ruined woman!’

She watched him through her tears, expecting to see consternation, pity, perhaps even despair. Instead, a strange, slow smile spread across Mr. Grudge’s pudding-like face. The tension seemed to drain from his stiffened shoulders. ‘Ruined, you say?’ he repeated, his voice losing its official timbre and taking on a softer, almost conversational tone. ‘Well. Well, well. That is a change in the proceedings, and no mistake.’

Mrs. Pettifer blinked. ‘A change? My dear sir, it is a catastrophe! I have nothing to bring to our union but a cookshop and a mountain of debt!’

‘On the contrary, madam,’ said Mr. Grudge, drawing himself up, though not quite so high as before. ‘It is a relief. A positive unburdening. For I must confess, that it was not for your capital, but for the comfort of your table and the comfort of your fireside, that I sought your hand. The thought of managing your teapots and your investments… it weighed upon me. It was a responsibility I could ill afford, what with my duties to the parish.’

Mrs. Pettifer stared at him, her bonnet askew. The scales fell from her eyes, and she saw not a pillar of the community, but a man in a very tight coat, looking for a free dinner. ‘Your duties to the parish?’ she said, her voice suddenly sharp as a carving knife. ‘And I suppose it was for the sake of those duties you wished to marry me? For the respectability I might lend to your office?’

‘Well,’ said Mr. Grudge, with a cough, ‘a man in my position must consider appearances. A wife of substance is an asset to the uniform.’

‘An asset to the uniform!’ she shrieked. ‘And here was I, thinking the uniform was an asset to me! I thought I was marrying the Law, and I find I have been courting a rascal in a coat two sizes too small!’

‘And I,’ retorted Mr. Grudge, his face flushing with indignation, ‘thought I was marrying a prudent woman of business, and find I have been paying court to a bankrupt pastry-cook!’

They stood there for a long moment, in the smoky little parlour, two architects of a grand design who had just discovered that each had been building on the other’s sand. The truth, stark and unadorned, lay between them, as ugly and awkward as a dead fish. There was to be no wedding. There was to be no grand entry into society. There was to be no comfortable retirement for a constable.

Finally, Mr. Grudge cleared his throat. ‘Well, madam,’ he said, his voice flat. ‘I believe the matter is concluded. There is no occasion for a ceremony.’

‘None whatsoever,’ agreed Mrs. Pettifer, adjusting her bonnet with a grim finality.

‘I will, however,’ said Mr. Grudge, turning to the door, ‘require a pork pie for the road. A large one. It has been a taxing morning.’

‘Sixpence,’ said Mrs. Pettifer, holding out her hand. ‘And no credit. Not even for the Law.’

Mr. Grudge paid the sixpence, took the pie, and marched out of the cookshop and into the fog, a solitary figure once more. And Mrs. Pettifer, watching him go, began to take down the bonnet, a little wiser, a little poorer, but with the firm and abiding conviction that a good crust was worth more than all the respectability in the parish of St. Buttolph’s.
