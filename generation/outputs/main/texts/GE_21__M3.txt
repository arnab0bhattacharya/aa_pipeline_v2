In the small provincial town of Milby, where the Dissenting interest had long taken root and flourished in modest brick meeting-houses, Mr. Amos Bundy held a position of considerable moral weight. He was a draper of strict probity, a man whose conscience had been woven as closely as the stuffs upon his shelves, and who, having experienced a sharp conversion in his youth under the ministry of the Reverend Matthew Probert, carried through life a heavy sense of obligations both divine and social. It was to Mr. Probert’s early influence that Amos owed not only his spiritual awakening but his subsequent elevation in the chapel, rising from a trembling seat-holder to a respected deacon; and this debt of gratitude had hardened, as such debts will, into a settled deference to the Probert name. Thus, when the Reverend Probert’s son, Theophilus, had been appointed master of the Milby Charity School—a foundation largely supported by the subscriptions of the chapel’s leading adherents—Amos had accepted the young man’s installation with a quiet satisfaction, taking it as a sign of Providence that the seeds of the father’s piety should bear fruit in the instruction of the poor.

Amos was a man who liked to believe that human nature, when watered by grace, produced a uniform crop of virtue; he had little taste for the subtler anatomies of motive which reveal the self-deception lurking beneath a virtuous exterior. He preferred to judge by outward observance, and Theophilus, being punctual at prayers and given to a pale, ascetic gravity of demeanour, presented an exterior which asked no searching questions. But the slow currents of human affairs are not arrested by the timidity of our perceptions, and the truth will out, not always by the dramatic thunderclap of discovery, but by the patient accumulation of small, uneasy observations which the mind cannot forever refuse to coordinate.

It was on a damp Tuesday in November, when the early dusk had already thickened the shadows in the schoolroom yard, that Amos Bundy’s certainties were first shaken. He had called at the school to deliver a parcel of flannel, donated by his own shop for the winter clothing of the foundation boys, and had entered the porch just as a sudden scuffling was heard within. The door was ajar. He stopped, arrested not by any intention to spy, but by that paralysis which seizes a man when his senses encounter what his understanding has no ready category to receive. He heard the sound of a heavy cane striking flesh, not once but repeatedly, and with a rhythm that spoke less of correction than of a fury which had lost its governing hand. Then came a sharp cry, thin and animal, followed by the voice of Theophilus Probert, not raised in the commanding tones of discipline, but hoarse and thick, uttering words that seemed to spring from a dark reservoir of irritation quite at variance with his usual clerical composure: “Hold your tongue, you snivelling brat, or I’ll give you something to cry for!” Amos stood motionless, the flannel parcel damp in his hands, while something within him—that faculty of moral imagination which his rigid theology had never quite succeeded in suppressing—conjured up the small, cowering figure behind the voice, the terror and the incomprehension of a child subjected to a violence it could neither resist nor understand.

He withdrew without making his presence known, walking home through the wet streets with a step that had lost its customary decision. The flannel, he reflected, could be delivered on the morrow; and he was conscious, even as he framed the excuse, of the deeper reason which prompted it: he was not yet ready to confront what he had heard, because to confront it would be to set in motion a train of consequences for which his whole system of loyalties had made no provision. For if Theophilus was a man who beat the children under his care with a savage personal animus, then Amos Bundy, as a member of the school’s governing board, was bound by every duty he recognised to bring the matter forward; and yet to do so would be to strike at the son of the man to whom he owed his very standing in the chapel and the town. The Reverend Probert was old now, and his health infirm; the chapel membership revered him as a patriarch, and any attack upon his son would be felt, with that unreasoning solidarity which characterises religious communities, as an attack upon the father and upon the cause itself.

Amos sat by his fire that evening, his wife moving quietly about the room with the serene unconsciousness of a woman whose husband’s moral struggles belonged to a sphere she trusted him to manage, and he turned the problem over and over with the painful deliberation of a man accustomed to weigh actions by their consequences but unaccustomed to finding two duties in irreconcilable opposition. He thought of the text, “Suffer the little children,” and of the catechism he had learned at the Reverend Probert’s knee; he thought of his shop, his ledger, the neat columns of debit and credit which represented his worldly substance, and he thought of the pale, terrified face he had not seen but had felt with an inward certainty that was more vivid than sight. The contradiction lay not merely between gratitude and justice, but between two conceptions of himself: the loyal chapel-man who honoured the instrument of his salvation, and the man of conscience who could not unknow what he knew. It is in such moments that the deeper self asserts itself, not with the dramatic suddenness of revelation, but with the slow, irresistible pressure of a truth that has been forming beneath the crust of habit; and Amos Bundy, whatever his limitations, was not constitutionally capable of that comfortable self-deception which persuades a man to call his timidity by the name of prudence.

The next morning he rose early, before the pale November light had fully distinguished the shapes of the warehouses across the street, and having dressed with unusual care, he sat down to compose a letter—not to the board, for he felt that public accusation demanded a fuller certainty than one overheard moment could supply—but to Theophilus Probert himself. He wrote with the laborious precision of a man unaccustomed to expressing moral indignation in prose, weighing each phrase as though it were a yard of silk to be measured and not a hair’s breadth over-given. He stated what he had witnessed, he stated his conviction that such severity was contrary both to the statutes of the school and to the spirit of Christian instruction, and he requested a meeting at which the matter might be discussed before any public step was taken. It was a letter which combined the shrewdness of a man who knew that a private warning might spare the school a scandal and save Theophilus from a ruin he had not yet fully earned, with the stubborn rectitude of a man who intended, if private remonstrance failed, to discharge his duty whatever the cost. He sealed it with the heavy wax he used for his commercial correspondence, and walked out into the grey morning to deliver it himself, feeling within his breast that quiet, melancholy resolution which comes to a man when he has decided that the preservation of his own integrity is worth more than the preservation of any relationship, however sacred, which has been built upon it.
