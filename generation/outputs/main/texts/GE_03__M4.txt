The quarrel, when it came at last, had been ten years in the making, though neither party to it could have dated its beginning; for resentment, in natures that are at once conscientious and proud, does not spring up in a night like a mushroom, but rather grows as the lichen grows upon the stone—slowly, imperceptibly, by the mere accumulation of days. It was a Tuesday in the early spring of the year, and the rain had fallen steadily since morning upon the red roofs of Thurlby, so that the pavement before the shop of Messrs. Gedge, Drapers, was a dark and shining mirror, and the few passengers who hurried by held their umbrellas low and gave no greeting. Within, behind the counter, stood Miss Prudence Gedge, folding and refolding a piece of grey stuff with a precision that betrayed an inward agitation, while her brother, Mr. Nathaniel Gedge, leaned against the shelf of drawers in the farther corner, with his hands in his pockets and his eyes fixed upon the floor, in the attitude of a man who has said his say and awaits the issue.

The shop was one of those old-fashioned establishments, with a low ceiling crossed by black beams, and a smell of wool and camphor and brown paper, which seems to belong to an earlier generation, and to promise, with a kind of mute fidelity, that it will survive the changes of the world. Upon the shelves were ranged the stuffs of the trade—the sober broadcloths and the gay printed calicoes, the linseys for the labourer and the figured silks for the farmer's daughter—each in its paper parcel, and each, as it were, a chapter in the history of the town. Behind the shop was the parlour, where the brother and sister took their meals together, and where the portrait of the late Mr. Gedge, in a black coat and a white neckcloth, hung above the chimney-piece, looking down with a countenance of mild and somewhat wooden gravity upon the children who had kept his name and his credit for ten years.

It was of this ten years that Nathaniel was thinking when he broke the silence.

"You speak, Prue," said he, "as if I had never considered you. Do you suppose I have not? Do you suppose I have not lain awake o' nights, and turned it over and over, and asked myself whether I had any right to be happy at your cost? But I say again, as I have said before, that it is not at your cost. Susan is not the woman you take her for."

"I have said nothing against Susan Whitworth," returned Prudence, with a certain stateliness of manner which sat oddly upon her plain and somewhat angular figure. "I have only said that I cannot see what there is in her to justify—" here she paused, and the grey stuff underwent a sharper folding—"to justify the upsetting of everything."

"The upsetting of everything! And what is everything? A shop that barely keeps us, and a house that is too big for two, and a life that is the same from year's end to year's end, without hope or change. Is that everything? Then Heaven help us, Prue, if we are to set such store by it."

"You have no right to speak so," said Prudence, and her voice, in spite of her, trembled a little. "It was our father's shop. It was our father's house. I have given my youth to it, and I do not repent it. I have not asked for change. I have not asked for hope. I have asked only to keep what was ours, and to do my duty by the name that is over the door."

There was a silence, during which the rain beat against the glass, and a cart rattled by in the street without. Nathaniel took his hands from his pockets and walked to the window, and stood with his back to her, looking out at the wet roofs and the grey sky. He was a man of five-and-thirty, with a face that was rather good than handsome, and a manner that was rather gentle than strong; and there was in his eyes, when he turned them upon his sister, a look of perplexed and wounded affection which it was not easy to meet.

"Prue," said he, "do you remember what you said to me when my father died? You said, 'We will keep it together, Nat, you and I.' And I said, 'We will.' And we have kept it together, and we have done our duty, and we have paid every man his own, and we have not been ungrateful to God for His mercies. But we have not lived. Do you understand me? We have not lived. And now there is a chance—a chance for me, at least—and you would have me put it from me because the shop cannot keep two households. But I say the shop cannot keep two households because we have never tried. Susan has a little money of her own—not much, but enough to stock the other side of the shop with haberdashery, which we have never had, and which the town wants. She has a head for it, Prue—a better head than mine, I do not mind saying it. She would bring custom, and she would bring life. And you—you would be mistress in the house, as you have always been. Nothing would be altered. Only there would be—there would be a child, perhaps, and a little more cheerfulness, and a little less of this—this deadness."

"Deadness!" cried Prudence, and she threw down the grey stuff upon the counter with a gesture that was almost passionate. "You call it deadness. I call it peace. I call it the honest quiet of a life that has no shame in it. And you would bring a woman into it who—who laughs when there is nothing to laugh at, and talks of fashion, and thinks of nothing but her ribbons and her bonnets, and who would make our father's house a place of—of—" She could not find the word, and she turned away her face, and put her hand to her eyes.

Nathaniel looked at her with a great pity, and yet with a certain firmness too. "You do not know her, Prue," he said. "You have seen her at church, and at the Misses Thorne's, and you have judged her by her ribbons and her laughing. But I have seen her when her father was ill, and when her sister died, and I know what is in her. She is not shallow. She is only—only bright. And if you would let her, she would love you. She has said as much to me. She has said, 'I am afraid of your sister, Nat, but I would like to be her friend.'"

Prudence turned upon him a face that was pale and set. "And what of me?" she said. "What of my happiness? You speak of yours—of Susan's—of the child that is to be. Have you thought that if you marry, I am alone? Have you thought that I have given my life to this shop, and that if you bring a wife into it, I am a stranger in my own home? You say nothing would be altered. You know that everything would be altered. You know that I should be a dependant in the house I have kept for you. You know it, Nathaniel, and you will not say it."

The words struck home, as words will when they are spoken by those who know us best. Nathaniel's face changed, and he was silent for a moment; and then he said, in a low voice, "And so we are to sacrifice ourselves, you and I, each for the other, and neither for any good. Is that what you would have? Is that the end of all our ten years?"

"It is the end that I have a right to ask," said Prudence, "if I have given my youth for it."

There was a long pause. The rain had ceased, and a pale gleam of sunshine fell through the window upon the grey stuff upon the counter, and upon the two figures that stood facing each other in the little shop—the brother with his hand upon the back of a chair, the sister with her hands clasped before her, and her eyes fixed upon the floor. And in that pause there came to both of them, though neither spoke it, the thought of the years that were gone, and of the years that were to come, and of the strange, sad, patient way in which human beings, who love one another, will yet stand in one another's light, and call it duty, and be unable to see that the shadow they cast is their own.

It was Nathaniel who spoke at last, and his voice was very gentle. "I will not be hasty, Prue," he said. "I will think of what you have said. But I cannot promise you—I cannot promise you to give her up. I have promised her, and I cannot break my word. But I will think, and we will talk again."

And Prudence, without a word, took up the grey stuff and began to fold it once more, with the same precision as before; and Nathaniel, after a moment's hesitation, went out into the street, and walked slowly along the wet pavement, with his hat pulled down over his eyes, and his hands clasped behind him, in the manner of a man who is not at peace with himself. And the sun shone out more brightly, and the town went on with its business, and the shop of Messrs. Gedge, Drapers, stood open as before, with its low ceiling and its black beams and its smell of wool and camphor, waiting, as such places wait, for the slow and inexorable processes of time to decide what the wisdom of man could not determine.
