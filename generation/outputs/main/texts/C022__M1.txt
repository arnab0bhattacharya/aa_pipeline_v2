The letter from England arrived on a Tuesday, on a morning so bright and crisp it felt like a promise. It lay on Miss Albright’s small writing desk, its thick, cream-coloured paper and embossed seal a stark contrast to the spidery Flemish script of the local correspondence. The crest belonged to the headmaster of St. Jude’s Academy for Young Ladies in Hampshire, an institution of considerable reputation and, she knew, considerable means.

Eleanor Albright had been at the Sint-Agneschool voor Meisjes in Ghent for six years. She had arrived a nervous girl of twenty, armed with a good education, a poor command of French, and no knowledge of Dutch at all. Now, at twenty-six, she was the school’s most respected teacher. Her English lessons were legendary, not for their rigour, though they were rigorous, but for the quiet passion she brought to the poets. She made Keats sing and Wordsworth walk, and her pupils, daughters of Flemish merchants and minor nobility, adored her. They had even begun to adopt her soft English cadence when they spoke her language.

The offer was generous. A post as senior mistress, a salary that would allow her to live in comfort, to send money to her widowed mother, to finally stop counting the centimes. It was everything she had worked for, the logical and triumphant next step in her life. She read the letter twice, the crisp English words feeling foreign on her own tongue after so long. She felt a great, hollow space open up inside her, a space where relief and excitement ought to have been.

The news, as such things do, seeped through the school’s ancient stone corridors by afternoon. Her classroom, usually a haven of focused quiet, was a hive of buzzing whispers when she entered for the afternoon session. The girls, aged from fourteen to eighteen, looked at her with wide, tragic eyes. They did not speak of it, not at first. They simply watched her, their expressions a mixture of betrayal and a desperate, childish hope that the rumour was untrue.

The class was translating a passage from Chaucer, a difficult task she had set them. But their hearts were not in it. Little Annelise, the youngest, who had a lisp that made her ‘th’ sounds endearing, let a fat tear roll down her cheek and splash onto the yellowed page of her book.

“Annelise, my dear, what is it?” Eleanor asked, her voice gentle.

The dam broke. “Is it true, Miss Albright?” Annelise whispered, her lip trembling. “Are you going back to England?”

A chorus of soft, pleading voices rose in the quiet room. “Please, Miss Albright, do not leave us!” “We will be perfect, we promise!” “What will we do without you?”

Eleanor felt her own throat tighten. She looked at their earnest, upturned faces, these girls she had watched grow from awkward children into thoughtful young women. She had taught them more than grammar and verse; she had taught them a way of seeing the world, a way of finding beauty in language. How could she explain that the pull of home, of a familiar landscape and a secure future, was a force as real and as powerful as the tide? She could not.

“My dears,” she began, but her voice failed her. She could not make a promise she did not intend to keep, nor could she bear to give them the truth. She raised a hand to quiet them. “We are here to study. Let us return to Chaucer.”

The silence that followed was heavy and bruised. They did their work, but the magic was gone. The words on the page were just words.

After the girls had filed out, their footsteps echoing with unspoken sorrow, Eleanor remained in the classroom. The late afternoon sun slanted through the tall, arched windows, illuminating the dust motes dancing in the air. It was then that she saw him. Mijnheer Vandersteen was standing in the doorway, a silhouette against the brighter light of the hall. He taught history and geography, a quiet, meticulous man with ink-stained fingers and a gaze that seemed to see the past layered over the present. He was perhaps ten years her senior, with a serious face and hair that was just beginning to show threads of grey at the temples.

In six years, they had shared little more than polite greetings in the staffroom. He was a creature of Flemish reserve, and she, of English propriety. They spoke of lesson plans and school administration, and nothing more. Yet, she had always been aware of him. She had seen the care with which he taught, the way he could make the story of the Dukes of Burgundy as compelling as a novel. She had noticed the way he would sometimes look at her when she was speaking in the staffroom, a look of quiet, intense concentration, as if he were studying a complex and fascinating text.

He did not speak. He simply stood there, his hand resting on the doorframe. His eyes met hers, and in that moment, the entire unspoken history of their acquaintance passed between them. There was no need for words. She saw in his gaze not an accusation, but a profound and weary sadness. It was a look that mirrored the hollow feeling in her own chest. It was a look that said, *So, it is true.* It was a look that asked a question she could not answer, a question about the life she might have had, the quiet companionship she might have known in this old city by the river. He had never befriended her, and she had never sought him out. The chasm of language and custom had always been between them. But now, in the silent space of the empty classroom, it felt as though they understood each other perfectly.

He held her gaze for a long moment, his expression unreadable but for the deep-set sorrow in his eyes. Then, he gave a slow, almost imperceptible nod. It was not a gesture of farewell, nor of acceptance. It was simply an acknowledgement of a fact, as immutable as the turning of the earth or the flow of the Leie. He turned and walked away, his footsteps quiet on the worn flagstones.

Eleanor stood alone in the fading light. The letter from England lay on her desk, a symbol of a future she had craved. But now, its promise felt thin, its cream-coloured paper like a shroud for the life she was leaving behind. The adoration of her pupils had been a sweet, bright thing, but the silent sorrow of Mijnheer Vandersteen was a deep, resonant note that struck at the very core of her. She looked out the window at the familiar grey rooftops and the elegant spire of the Belfry against the pale sky. This was her home. Not England, with its green hills and distant promises, but here. Here, in this place of quiet streets and foreign words, where a man she barely knew could make her heart ache with a single, silent look. The choice was not between two posts. It was between two selves. And as the bell in the tower began to toll for Vespers, she knew, with a certainty that was both terrifying and liberating, which self she had to be.
