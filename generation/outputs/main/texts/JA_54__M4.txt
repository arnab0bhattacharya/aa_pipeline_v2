The morning of the proposed expedition to Larkspur Hill had broken with every promise of fine weather, and the party at Delaford Cottage were disposed to think themselves particularly fortunate in the season. Miss Harriet Ellwood, whose suggestion the outing had originally been, was occupied in the pleasant arrangement of cold provisions and the still pleasanter anticipation of Mr. Charles Ashby’s approbation; for that gentleman, lately arrived in the neighbourhood on a visit to the squire, had been heard to profess a great partiality for rural amusements, and it was understood that he would make one of the company. Her mother, a lady of excellent intentions and no very decided opinions, was easily persuaded that the day would be delightful; her father, who valued his own comfort above all things, had already begun to doubt the wisdom of a scheme which required him to walk a mile and a half up a steep incline; and her younger sister, Louisa, was chiefly concerned with the question of whether the strawberry cream would survive the journey.

It was therefore with a general air of satisfaction that the family were assembled in the breakfast-room, and with something approaching complacency that Mrs. Ellwood observed, “I am sure we shall be a very pleasant party. The Ashbys have promised to be with us by eleven, and I have desired cook to pack the hamper with the greatest care.”

“I hope,” said Mr. Ellwood, from behind his newspaper, “that there is to be no parrot.”

This observation, delivered with an emphasis which spoke of long suffering, was met with a moment of conscious silence. It was well known that Miss Prudence Vane, the great-aunt whose eccentricities were the wonder and the burden of the family, had lately acquired a parrot of a singularly accomplished and voluble description, and that she had formed an attachment to the bird which bordered on the maternal.

“My dear,” said Mrs. Ellwood, “your aunt has not been asked.”

“Then she will come,” replied Mr. Ellwood, with the gloomy satisfaction of a prophet. “She always comes when she is not asked. It is her peculiar talent to discover the very occasions on which her presence is least desired, and to grace them with the most punctuality.”

His prediction was verified before the clock had struck eleven. A sound of carriage wheels upon the gravel was followed by the entrance of Miss Vane herself, a small, upright lady of seventy, arrayed in a pelisse of a startling shade of green, and bearing upon her arm a large wicker cage, within which reposed a grey parrot of a contemplative and somewhat cynical expression. Behind her came a footman, staggering beneath the weight of a bandbox and two additional parasols.

“My dear nieces,” said Miss Vane, advancing with great cheerfulness, “I have come to join your little expedition. I hope you are delighted. I have brought Augustus.”

“Augustus?” faltered Mrs. Ellwood.

“The parrot,” said Miss Vane, with a look of mild reproach. “He is the most agreeable creature in the world, and has been in the best spirits all morning. He was a present from my dear friend Captain Hollis, you know, who had him from the Brazils, and who taught him everything he knows. He is quite the man of the world. He has seen things, and he has opinions.”

Mr. Ellwood lowered his newspaper by slow degrees, and regarded the parrot with the air of a man who perceives his worst fears taking shape before him.

“I am sure,” said Harriet, with the desperate civility of one who sees her plans crumbling, “that it is very kind of my great-aunt to join us. But I had understood that the walk to Larkspur Hill was somewhat steep, and I should not wish—”

“Nonsense, my dear,” interrupted Miss Vane. “I am as strong as a horse, and Augustus is accustomed to travel. He sat upon my shoulder all the way from Exeter, and never once complained. He is a capital walker. He will ride upon the top of the hamper, and be the life of the party.”

There was no withstanding this. The carriage was ordered, the hamper was packed, the parrot was installed upon it in a position of considerable dignity, and the party set forth. Harriet, who had hoped for a morning of quiet conversation with Mr. Ashby, found her prospects considerably altered; and when that gentleman arrived at the appointed meeting-place, he found himself confronted not only with the family he had expected, but with an elderly lady in a green pelisse, who greeted him with the familiarity of an old acquaintance.

“So you are the young man of whom I have heard,” said Miss Vane, surveying him through her spectacles. “You have a very sensible face. Augustus, what do you think of him?”

The parrot, who had been regarding the proceedings with a cold and critical eye, inclined his head and remarked, in a voice of great distinctness, “Damned impudence.”

There was a general consternation. Mr. Ashby coloured. Mrs. Ellwood looked as if she wished the earth to open. Mr. Ellwood, for the first time that morning, appeared to be enjoying himself.

“Augustus!” cried Miss Vane, with no appearance of displeasure. “You must not be so free. He learnt it from the captain,” she explained, turning to Mr. Ashby with an air of apology which was not entirely convincing. “The captain was a very fine man, but he had a sailor’s tongue. Augustus is a capital mimic, and he has no notion of propriety. It is his only fault.”

“I am sure,” said Harriet, with an effort at composure, “that it is a very clever bird.”

“He is cleverer than most Christians,” said Miss Vane, complacently. “He can say ‘Pretty Polly’ in three languages, and he knows the difference between a fool and a knave. Do you not, Augustus?”

“Rascal,” said the parrot, with a marked glance at Mr. Ashby.

Mr. Ashby, who had borne the first sally with tolerable grace, began to look a little discomposed. He was a young man of good family and unexceptionable manners, but he had not the gift of easy raillery, and he was conscious of a growing conviction that the bird had taken him in dislike.

The walk proceeded, and the parrot continued. He commented upon the scenery in terms which, if not strictly appropriate, were at least original; he whistled a sea-shanty of a somewhat indelicate character; and when Mr. Ashby, in an attempt to recover his ground, offered his arm to Harriet for the ascent, Augustus observed, in a tone of penetrating scorn, “I’ll be hanged if you do.”

Mr. Ashby dropped his arm. Harriet, who had been prepared to be charmed, found herself instead divided between mortification and an irrepressible inclination to laugh. Louisa had already retired behind a hedge, ostensibly to examine a wildflower, but in reality to conceal her mirth. Even Mrs. Ellwood, who was not given to levity, was observed to press her handkerchief to her lips.

It was Mr. Ellwood who at last broke the silence. “My dear aunt,” said he, with a gravity which was not entirely assumed, “I think your parrot has a great deal to say for himself. But I would suggest that he be permitted to say it at a somewhat greater distance from our young friend here, who, I am sure, has borne the raillery of a sailor’s bird with a patience which does him credit.”

Miss Vane, thus appealed to, consented to remove the cage to the farther side of the hamper, where Augustus, after a few moments of sulky silence, fell into a meditation which lasted until the party reached the summit. There, however, the prospect of the viands revived him; and when Mr. Ashby, in a desperate attempt to recover his dignity, began to carve the ham, the parrot, fixing him with a glittering eye, remarked, with the air of one who has made a discovery, “You don’t know your own mind.”

This was too much. Mr. Ashby laid down the knife, and for the first time in the course of the morning, he laughed. “Upon my word,” said he, “I believe the bird is right. I have been trying all morning to appear what I am not, and he has seen through me at every turn. I hope, Miss Ellwood,” turning to Harriet, “that you will not judge me by the opinion of a parrot who has evidently been brought up in very bad company.”

Harriet, relieved beyond measure, assured him that she would not; and the party, thus restored to good humour, proceeded to enjoy their repast with a cordiality which had been entirely wanting before. The parrot was removed to a respectful distance, where he occupied himself in cracking nuts and muttering imprecations upon the captain, whose memory he appeared to cherish with a singular mixture of affection and resentment.

“I am very glad,” said Miss Vane, when the last of the strawberry cream had been disposed of, “that Augustus has been able to do some good in the world. He is a great judge of character, and he has never yet been mistaken. If he says a young man is a rascal, you may depend upon it that the young man is a rascal; but if he says he does not know his own mind, why, then, you may depend upon it that he is merely a fool, and that is a great deal more hopeful.”

Mr. Ashby, who was seated beside Harriet upon the grass, was pleased to observe that he had never been more grateful for a character reference; and Harriet, who had begun the day in the expectation of a very different sort of pleasure, found herself inclined to agree with her great-aunt that a parrot, properly understood, was not the worst companion in the world.
