It was in the November of ’94, when the fogs had come early and the lamps of Whitehall burned all day like watch-fires in a mist, that I found myself again in the sitting-room at Baker Street, though my friend had quitted it for a season and taken up a small set of chambers in Craven Street, that he might be nearer to certain matters of the Admiralty which were then engaging him. I had come at his summons, and found him seated before a table strewn with blue-books and foolscap, his long fingers pressed together, and his eyes fixed upon the fire with that abstraction which in him betokened the active rather than the idle mind.

“You are come in good time, Watson,” said he, pushing a chair toward me with his foot. “I have a little problem here which may interest you, though it is not one of those gaudy crimes that the public loves. It is a matter of a stolen page, and a young man’s honour, and—what is more curious—a tune.”

“A tune?”

“A whistle, Watson. A few bars of something, heard twice a day through a partition, and never at any other hour. But let me tell it in order, for you have taught me that the reading public prefers a plain sequence to my own disordered methods.”

He took up a slip of paper from the table.

“The young gentleman is one Mr. Arthur Pemberton, a junior clerk in the Hydrographic Department, a post he has held for three years, and one which his father, a retired paymaster, obtained for him with some difficulty. He is engaged to be married to a Miss Eleanor Vane, whose acquaintance I made through my old friend Inspector Lestrade, who is her uncle by marriage. It was Miss Vane who came to me, not the young man—for he was too proud, and too much ashamed, to come himself. She is a sensible young woman, and she stated the case with a clearness that did her credit.”

Holmes drew a folded sheet from among the papers.

“The facts are these. On the fourteenth of October, a confidential report upon the new torpedo trials at Portland was laid before the First Lord. It was a document of some twenty pages, prepared in the office of the Hydrographer, and containing certain figures which, if they reached a foreign power, would be of the highest value. It was copied in the department by three clerks, of whom Pemberton was one, and it was his duty to carry the fair copy to the First Lord’s private room. He did so at half-past eleven. He returned to his desk at a quarter to twelve. At three o’clock the First Lord’s secretary telephoned to the Hydrographer that a page—the seventh—was missing from the report, and that the First Lord was exceedingly vexed.”

“And Pemberton had carried it?”

“He had carried it, and he had carried it alone. He swears that the document never left his hand from the moment he took it from the copying-press to the moment he laid it on the secretary’s table. Yet the page was gone, and it was found—this is the crux of the matter—it was found two days later in the pocket of a waterproof coat belonging to Pemberton, which hung in the clerks’ lobby. He was suspended at once, and but for Miss Vane’s entreaties he would have been dismissed without a character.”

“It looks black enough,” said I.

“It looks black, and it is precisely the sort of blackness that I distrust. A guilty man does not leave the stolen page in his own pocket for two days, to be discovered by the first person who searches. He burns it, or he posts it to his confederate. And there is another point—the seventh page contained no figures at all. It contained the list of the officers who had witnessed the trials, with their comments. Valuable, no doubt, to a foreign agent, but not the page that a traitor would choose if he had but one page to take.”

“Then why was it taken?”

“That is what I propose to discover. I have been to the department twice, in the character of a gentleman interested in the question of office ventilation, and I have made the acquaintance of the copying clerk.”

“The copying clerk?”

“A young man named Joseph Sallow, who came into the department in the same week as the missing page, and who has since been promoted to the copying-press. He is a pale, silent fellow, with a habit of whistling between his teeth. It is a habit I have observed in men who are engaged in work that requires a certain mechanical regularity. But it is not the whistling itself that interests me—it is the tune.”

Holmes rose and went to the window, where the fog pressed against the panes like a grey hand.

“I have made it my business to hear that tune, Watson. I have stood in the corridor outside the copying-room at the hours when Sallow is at his press, and I have heard it. It is a fragment of an old air—‘The British Grenadiers,’ I think—but it is whistled in a peculiar manner, with a pause after the fourth bar, and a little rising note at the end, as though the whistler were listening for something. And I have observed that he whistles it only when the door of the Hydrographer’s room is open, and only when the Hydrographer is at his desk. When the door is shut, he is silent.”

“You think he is signalling?”

“I think he is keeping time. Let me put it to you as a problem of evidence. The page was taken between eleven and twelve. At that hour the copying-room is busy, and the corridor is full of messengers. But there is one place in that corridor where a man may stand unobserved—the alcove by the fire-bucket, where the gas-jet is broken and the light is poor. From that alcove a man can see the door of the Hydrographer’s room, and can see also the door of the clerks’ lobby where the coats are hung. I have stood in that alcove myself, Watson, and I have seen what a man may see from it.”

“And what did you see?”

“I saw Sallow come out of the copying-room at a quarter to twelve, with a slip of paper in his hand—a slip which he did not carry to the Hydrographer, but which he thrust into the pocket of a waterproof coat that hung in the lobby. The coat was Pemberton’s. I saw him do it, and I saw him return to his press and begin to whistle.”

“Then you have your man!”

“I have my man, but I have not my proof. The slip of paper was gone when I searched the coat an hour later—for I had not the right to search it earlier, and Sallow had been beforehand with me. But I have since learned that Sallow has a brother-in-law, a Mr. Tobias Crane, who keeps a small marine store in Wapping, and who is known to have dealings with certain persons who are not particular as to the source of their information. And I have learned something more—that the seventh page, the page of comments, was not the page that Sallow meant to take. He meant to take the page of figures, but he was interrupted, and he took the first page that came to hand. The page of figures is still in the report, and the First Lord has it in his safe.”

“Then the treason failed?”

“The treason failed, but the trap was laid, and it was laid for Pemberton. Sallow had been in the department a fortnight when he was approached by Crane, who had learned from a clerk in the dockyard that Pemberton was in debt—a small matter of a racing wager—and that he was engaged to be married. The plan was simple. Sallow was to take a page from the report, thrust it into Pemberton’s coat, and then, when the loss was discovered, to swear that he had seen Pemberton take it. The whistling was the signal to Crane, who waited in the street below, that the page was in the coat, and that the coast was clear.”

“But why did Sallow whistle at other times?”

“Because he is a nervous man, and the tune had become a habit with him. He whistled it when he was at his work, and he whistled it when he was afraid. It was the whistling that betrayed him—that, and the fact that he whistled only when the Hydrographer’s door was open. A man who whistles a signal does not whistle it when there is no one to hear.”

Holmes turned from the window and took up his hat.

“I have laid the matter before the First Lord’s secretary, and I have laid it before Inspector Lestrade. Sallow was arrested this morning at his press, and the press itself—a double-handled machine of American pattern—was found to have a secret drawer in its frame, in which were three pages of the report, including the seventh, which he had taken back from the coat when he found that he had taken the wrong page. He had meant to return it to the report before the loss was discovered, but he was too late.”

“And Pemberton?”

“Pemberton is reinstated, and Miss Vane is to be congratulated upon a fiancé who has been foolish enough to bet on a horse, but not foolish enough to sell his country. I have written to him this morning, and I have told him that if he must gamble, he should gamble upon the honesty of his friends, for it is a safer investment than the Derby.”

He took up his violin and drew the bow across the strings.

“And the tune, Watson? I have set it down in my index under the letter S—Sallow, Joseph, copying clerk, whistler of ‘The British Grenadiers.’ It is a small thing, but it is the small things that hang men. And now, if you will excuse me, I have a fancy to play something that is not ‘The British Grenadiers.’”
