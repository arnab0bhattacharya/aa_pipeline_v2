The matter of the valuation was bruited abroad in the village of Cold Morton with a rapidity that would have seemed miraculous had it not been so strictly conformable to the known laws of rural propagation; for where there are few events, each event is made to do the work of many, and a piece of news, like a stone dropped into a still pond, sends its circles to the very margin of the pool. It was known, then—known first at the smithy, whence it travelled to the mill, and from the mill to the alehouse, and from the alehouse to every cottage hearth where a pot was boiled—that Mr. Rooke, the steward at the Hall, had ridden over on Tuesday to bid Abel Marston, the wheelwright, attend upon the morrow at Norbury’s farm, to appraise the cart and the tackle there for the satisfaction of the creditors; and it was known, further, that the sum which Abel Marston should set down would be as the seal upon a bond, for that Squire Ellwood had advanced money upon the security of the stock, and must needs be satisfied to the last shilling, since his agent was a man who could not endure to see a ledger crossed in any but the cleanest fashion.

Now, whether the general interest in this transaction arose from any lively sympathy with the debtor, is a question which a philosophic observer might have answered with some qualification. The English peasantry are not an sentimental people, and the misfortunes of a neighbour are apt to be contemplated by them with a resignation not wholly free from complacency, as we contemplate the misfortunes of those remote ages in which the mammoth perished. But there was in this case a peculiarity which touched the common conscience. Norbury was a small farmer, of the class that lives from hand to mouth, and had been unlucky—unlucky in his beasts, unlucky in his weather, and, it was whispered, not over-lucky in his own temper when the ale was in and the reckoning was out; but he had a wife and four children, and the cart was his chief means of getting his few sacks to market, so that the loss of it was not the loss of a luxury but the amputation of a limb. And, on the other hand, Abel Marston was a man of that plain integrity which is not the less honourable because it is unadvertised; he had lived forty years in Cold Morton, and no man had ever said of him that he had given a false weight or a false word. To ask such a man to value his neighbour’s goods for a seizure was to put a knife into the hand of a surgeon who must needs cut deep or prove himself no surgeon.

The morning came with that grey, soft, dripping aspect which an English November wears when it is most English, and Abel Marston, having put on his second-best coat, and taken his stick, walked slowly down the lane towards Norbury’s. He was a stout, ruddy man, with a face that seemed to have been modelled by much honest handling of timber, and eyes that had a habit of looking straight at a thing as if to see the grain of it. As he went, he was aware of faces at the windows and figures at the gates, and he knew well enough what they were waiting for. “There goes Abel,” said one; “he’ll never do it,” said another; “he’ll never set down the truth and ruin the man,” said a third; and a fourth, more knowing, shook his head and said, “Ah, but he’m a just man, Abel is; he’ll set down the truth if it ruin ’em all.” And the general mind was divided between the hope that he would be merciful and the fear that he would be just, which is a division not uncommon in human affairs, where the two virtues are apt to pull in different traces.

At Norbury’s gate he found the steward already arrived, a lean, precise man, with a face like a ruled account-book, who greeted him with the brisk civility of one who has no doubt of the result. “Good-morning, Marston. You will find the cart in the shed yonder. I have the inventory here; we will go over it together, and you will set down the value of each article in the column, and sign at the foot. The squire wishes it done in form.”

The cart stood in the shed, a substantial, well-made cart, though the paint was blistered and the shafts were worn; and Abel Marston walked round it slowly, and laid his hand upon the wheel, and looked at the axle, and lifted the tilt, and examined the iron-work, with the air of a man who was reading a familiar book in a strange language. The steward stood by with his inventory, and the farmer’s wife, a pale, anxious woman, stood at the kitchen door with her apron to her eyes, and the children peeped from behind her, and the farm-labourers lounged at the corner of the rick-yard, and the whole village, in spirit, was gathered there, waiting for the word.

“Well, Marston,” said the steward, “what do you set down for the cart?”

Abel Marston did not answer at once. He stood with his hand on the wheel, and it is to be supposed that he was thinking of many things: of the four children, and of the woman at the door, and of the long years in which he and Norbury had worked side by side in the same fields, and of the hard winter when Norbury had lent him a sack of meal and would take no payment; and, on the other hand, of the squire’s ledger, and of the trust reposed in him, and of that inward witness which no man can silence, and which, if he be honest, is the most exacting of all masters. The sum which the cart was worth in the open market was a sum which would sweep away the last remnant of Norbury’s stock and leave him a beggar; the sum which a merciful man might set down was a sum which would leave the squire’s debt unpaid and the steward’s column false.

“The cart,” said Abel Marston at last, in a slow, deliberate voice, “is a good cart. It was made by Matthew Bond, of Nuneaton, in the year ’ninety-four, and the timber is sound, and the iron-work is but little worn. I cannot set it down at less than fourteen pound.”

The steward wrote it down with a nod of satisfaction. The woman at the door gave a little cry and covered her face. The labourers looked at one another and said nothing. And Abel Marston went on to the plough and the harrows, and set down the value of each with the same slow, deliberate exactness, and signed his name at the foot of the column, and handed the paper to the steward, and took up his stick and walked out of the yard.

But when he had gone a little way down the lane, he stopped and turned and looked back at the farm, and the woman was still standing at the door, and the children were still peeping from behind her, and it seemed to him that he had never seen so sad a sight in his life. And it is to be recorded of him that he did not go home to his dinner, but went to the Hall and asked to see the squire; and what passed between them was never fully known, but it was understood that Abel Marston offered to stand security for Norbury’s debt himself, and that the squire, who was not a hard man when he was not under the eye of his steward, consented to take the cart at a lower figure and to give Norbury a year’s time. And when this was known in Cold Morton, the village was divided in its judgment as villages are, some saying that Abel Marston had been weak, and some that he had been just; but the general voice, which is not always the voice of the majority, gave him the praise of a man who had done what he could.

For it is the fashion of the world to demand of us that we shall be just and merciful at once, and to be surprised when we find the two virtues pulling in different traces; and yet there is no man who has lived long in a village, or in a city either, but has learned that the finest integrity is not that which is merely exact, but that which, having been exact, is willing to bear the burden of its own exactness. Abel Marston set down the truth, and then he took up the cross of it; and if there be any who think that he did too little, or too much, let them consider how they would have acted in his place, and be silent.
