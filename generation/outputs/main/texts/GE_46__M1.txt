When Ruth Marlowe came back to Nether Coppice from her three years’ service at Wrenbury Hall, she brought with her a bonnet of a shape that no woman in the parish had ever seen, and a way of standing quite still, with her hands lightly clasped, that her mother found at once impressive and unaccountable. The cottage, which had seemed to her a sufficient and well-ordered world before she left it, now appeared as a small, dimly-lit stage on which the old, familiar actions were performed with a wearying lack of grace. The low-beamed ceiling pressed down upon her, and the scent of wood-smoke and damp wool, which had once been the very air of comfort, was now merely the smell of a life she had escaped and to which she had, by some incomprehensible mischance, been returned.

Her mother, Mrs. Marlowe, a woman whose perceptions were sharpened by the daily necessity of reading the weather and the tempers of her family, saw at once that the finery was a shell and the happiness a performance. She watched Ruth move about the small kitchen, her hands, which had learned to polish silver and fold linen with an economy of motion that bordered on art, now seeming to fumble with the coarse earthenware of the home table. There was a shadow behind Ruth’s eyes, a kind of perpetual bracing against a blow that had already fallen. Mrs. Marlowe did not ask questions; she knew that a direct assault on such a fortress of silence would only make the walls higher. Instead, she observed, and in the quiet evenings, when the fire had settled to a gentle glow, she would speak of small, safe things—the price of flour, the state of the garden, the gossip of the village—hoping that in the spaces between her words, Ruth might find a place to set down her burden. But Ruth’s answers were brief, her voice flat and colourless, as if she were conserving all her strength for some private, inner trial.

It was Ruth’s father, John Marlowe, who saw the matter with the straightforward, untroubled logic of a man who believes that every problem has a practical solution. His daughter was home; she was of an age to be settled; and young Thomas Pike, the blacksmith, had been faithful as a gundog for the three years she had been gone. To John, the situation presented itself as a simple equation. Thomas was a good man, with a strong back, a thriving trade, and a heart so plainly given over to Ruth that it was visible in every look he cast towards the cottage. What more could a girl want? He did not see the shadow in Ruth’s eyes as a sign of some deep spiritual wound, but as the vapours of a young woman who had had too much luxury and too little to do. He believed, with the conviction of a man who has hammered stubborn metal into submission all his life, that a good husband and a house of her own would soon knock the nonsense out of her.

The proposal, when it came, was not made by Thomas himself—that gentle, ox-like man would sooner have shod a horse backwards than speak of his feelings under pressure—but by her father, across the hearth one Saturday evening. “Thomas Pike was asking after you again today,” John said, not looking up from the boot he was mending. “He’s a steady man, Ruth. A good home, and he’d never look at another. It’s time you were thinking of settling. Your mother and I won’t be here for ever.” He spoke as if offering her a sensible new apron. Ruth felt the words close around her throat like a cord. To marry Thomas Pike would be to enter a life of predictability and honest toil, a life that was the very antithesis of the one she had left behind at Wrenbury Hall. It was a safe harbour, but she felt she could no longer bear the touch of the shore. The secret she carried, a thing of shame and terror that had grown in the silence of her own mind until it possessed the monstrous weight of a physical fact, made such a simple, honest life impossible. She could not bring this corruption into the clean, bright world of Thomas Pike’s affection. It would be a kind of sacrilege.

She stood up, her chair scraping against the stone floor. “I cannot,” she said, and her voice, though quiet, had a finality that silenced the room. She did not look at her father’s bewildered face or her mother’s stricken one. She walked to the door and stepped out into the cool evening air, leaving the warm, suffocating circle of the firelight behind her. The world outside was vast and dark, and for the first time since her return, she felt a sense of terrible freedom in its emptiness. The secret was her own, a solitary island in the middle of a life that everyone else saw as a simple, navigable stream. And she knew, with a certainty that chilled her more than the night air, that she must bear it alone, whatever the cost.
