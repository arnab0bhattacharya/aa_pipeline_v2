The gaslight had not yet been invented to banish the soot-darkened corners of Soho, and the lamp in the small, cramped room at the top of the stairs in Bleeding Heart Lane cast a sickly, yellow pall over everything. It illuminated the dust motes dancing in the stagnant air, a cruel parody of the elegant waltzes that Étienne Vauquelin taught to the daughters of merchants who smelled of cheap perfume and old money. Étienne sat at his writing desk, his fingers stiff and red from the cold that seeped through the single, cracked pane of glass in the window. He was a man who had polished himself into a mirror of European sophistication, a mask of powdered hair and silk cravat that hid the gaunt, hungry face of a refugee beneath.

The knock at the door was not the tentative tap of a student seeking a lesson, but a heavy, rhythmic pounding that vibrated through the floorboards. Étienne froze, his quill hovering over a sheet of paper where he had been attempting to write a letter to the Comte de Rieux, a letter that would never be sent. He opened the door to find a man he did not recognize. He was large, dressed in a coat of coarse wool that looked ill-fitting, his face a map of windburns and worry.

"Mr. Vauquelin?" the man asked. His English was thick, accented, and laced with the particular cadence of the rural south.

"Who are you?" Étienne asked, blocking the doorway.

"Name is Gaspard. From Valmont. We knew your mother. We knew your father before the fever took him."

The name struck Étienne like a physical blow. Valmont. The village of white stone houses and lavender fields, now likely a graveyard of corpses and ash. He stepped aside, his legs trembling slightly, and ushered the stranger in. Gaspard did not look around with the curiosity of a guest; he looked at the floor, at the worn rug, at the single chair, with the pitying scrutiny of a man assessing damage.

"Sit," Étienne said, his voice hollow. "You are far from home, Gaspard."

"I am not from home," Gaspard said, refusing the chair. He remained standing, his hands clasped behind his back. "I have come with news. News that may save the life of your brother, Lucien."

Étienne felt a spike of hope, sharp and painful. Lucien. His younger brother, who had refused to leave France, who had joined the National Guard with such fervent, blind idealism. He had been arrested in Lyon three months ago. The rumors had reached London like a slow poison: executions, deportations to the islands, silence.

"What news?" Étienne whispered.

"He is alive. He is in the prison at the Conciergerie. And he can be released. There is a man, a magistrate, who holds the key. He will take a sum. Not much. Five hundred livres. It is a small price for a life."

Étienne stared at him. Five hundred livres. It was more than he had saved in three years of dancing lessons. It was more than he had in his bank account. It was the price of his freedom, the money he had set aside to leave London for New York, to start over, to be no one.

"Who is this magistrate?" Étienne asked.

"His name is Duvall. He is a friend of a friend. But time is short. The list of names for the guillotine is being finalized. If Lucien is not on it, he is safe. But he must be out by Friday."

Before Étienne could respond, a shadow fell across the doorframe. Mrs. Halloway, his landlady, stood there. She was a small, sharp-featured woman with eyes that missed nothing. She held a tray with a single cup of tea, but she did not offer it.

"I heard voices," she said, her tone dry as parchment. "I heard you speaking of a brother. A French brother."

"Gaspard is a friend," Étienne said, trying to keep his voice steady. "He is a visitor."

"Visitor," Mrs. Halloway repeated, tasting the word as if it were spoiled milk. She stepped into the room, closing the door behind her with a definitive click. "You are a dancing master, Mr. Vauquelin. You teach the minuet to the children of silk merchants. You do not have friends who come from the countryside to ask for money to save prisoners. This is a swindle."

Gaspard turned to her, his face hardening. "Madame, I speak the truth. My name is Gaspard. I can prove it. I knew your husband in the war."

"My husband was a tailor in Covent Garden," Mrs. Halloway said, her voice rising. "He died of consumption. He never saw France. And you smell of the gutter, not the countryside. You are a pickpocket, or a spy, or both. I have seen your kind before. They come in pairs, or with a man who looks like a victim, and they take everything you have."

Étienne looked from his landlady to the stranger. The doubt that had been lurking in the back of his mind suddenly grew teeth. He looked at Gaspard’s hands. They were not the hands of a farmer. They were the hands of a man who picked locks. The coat was too new for a peasant, the buttons too shiny.

"Five hundred livres," Gaspard said, his voice dropping to a whisper. "You have it. I know you do. You save every penny."

"I have nothing," Étienne said, though the lie felt thin.

"Look at you," Gaspard hissed, stepping closer. "You wear a silk cravat in this weather. You eat meat. You have money. Give it to me, and your brother lives. Refuse, and I will tell the authorities you are a Jacobin agent. I will tell them you are plotting to assassinate the King."

Mrs. Halloway stepped between them, her face pale but resolute. "Get out," she said. "Or I will call the watch. I will tell them you tried to steal from a refugee. I will tell them you threatened him. The watch will not be kind to a man who threatens a Frenchman in these times. They will say he is a terrorist. They will hang him from the nearest lamppost."

Gaspard’s eyes flickered. The threat of the watch was a real one. The mood in London had turned sour; every Frenchman was a suspect, every radical a target. He looked at Étienne, then at Mrs. Halloway. The anger drained from his face, replaced by a cold, calculating resignation.

"You will regret this," he said. "Your brother is dead. He has been dead for weeks. I am merely collecting for my own expenses."

"Get out," Étienne said, his voice shaking with a rage he had not felt since the revolution. "Get out of my home."

Gaspard did not argue. He turned and walked out, his heavy boots thumping down the stairs. The door slammed shut, leaving a silence that was louder than the street noise outside.

Étienne sank into his chair, his hands trembling. He looked at Mrs. Halloway, who was still holding the tray, her face tight with worry.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me," she said, setting the cup on the desk. "I did it because I was afraid for you. And because I was right. It was a swindle. A cheap one, at that."

Étienne nodded, unable to speak. He looked at the lamp, at the flickering flame. He thought of Lucien, of the letters that had never come, of the silence that had grown heavy over the past months. He had hoped, foolishly, that the news was true. But Mrs. Halloway’s suspicion had been a blade that had cut away the illusion.

"I have no brother," Étienne said quietly. "I have no family. I am alone."

Mrs. Halloway looked at him for a long moment. Then, she picked up the cup and poured the tea. The steam rose in a thin, white ribbon, curling into the dark air.

"Drink," she said. "It will warm you. And tomorrow, we will burn the letters you have not sent. And you will start anew. No past. No ghosts. Just the next step."

Étienne took the cup. The tea was bitter, but it was warm. He drank, and for the first time in days, he felt the weight of the past begin to lift, not because it was gone, but because he had finally admitted it was gone. The lamp flickered, and the shadows danced, but they were no longer threatening. They were just shadows.
