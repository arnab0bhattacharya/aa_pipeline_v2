It was a truth not universally acknowledged, that a young woman in possession of a good sister must be in want of a brother-in-law; and if the gentleman in question had been paying his addresses, in a manner of speaking, for the better part of two years, the want became a species of civilised torment. Miss Eliza Fielding, a young lady of more spirit than patience, suffered this torment on behalf of her elder sister, Miss Marianne Fielding, with a keenness that Marianne herself seemed entirely to lack.

Mr. Alistair Finch, the gentleman in question, was of a disposition so amiable and a temper so even, that he might have been courted by the whole of Hampshire without once committing himself to any single damsel. His attentions to Marianne were marked by a constancy that was the envy of every mother in the village of Hartley Down, yet they were of such a general and undesigning kind, that one could scarce call them attentions at all. He brought her wildflowers, it is true, but he brought them to her mother also; he admired her singing, but he was no less complimentary to the rector’s wife upon her needlework; and he walked with her in the lane, but the pace was so sober and the conversation so proper, that it could have been the very same walk undertaken with his own aunt.

Eliza, whose seventeen years afforded her a profound and unshakeable certainty on the subject of love, found this state of affairs insufferable. “He is like a will-o'-the-wisp, Marianne!” she declared one morning, as they sat at their embroidery. “He leads you a pretty dance, and when you think you have him, he vanishes into the general landscape.”

Marianne, with a stitch that betrayed no such agitation, replied, “He is the most respectable of men. He is kind to us all. I am quite content with his friendship.”

“Friendship!” cried Eliza, throwing down her silks. “It is a friendship of two years’ standing, and it has not advanced an inch! A friendship does not make a man’s voice soften in a particular way when he speaks to you, nor does it make him forget the price of corn for a full ten minutes. He is in love with you; he is merely too great a coward to say so.”

“It is not cowardice,” Marianne said gently. “It is caution. He is a man of fortune, and must be careful.”

“He is careful not to declare himself! Very well. If he will not speak, then I shall make him.” And with the resolution of a general planning a campaign, Eliza set her mind to the task of forcing Mr. Finch’s hand.

Her first stratagem was one of subtle comparison. At the next assembly, when Mr. Finch came to claim Marianne’s hand for a country dance, Eliza intercepted him with a bright smile. “Oh, Mr. Finch, you must not take Marianne just yet! She has promised the set to Mr. Gable, who has been so very pressing.” Mr. Gable was a young man of twenty, recently come into a small property, and possessed of a loud voice and an alarming tendency to declare his opinions on every subject. He was, in Eliza’s estimation, a perfect instrument of jealousy.

Mr. Finch’s countenance, usually as placid as a summer pond, underwent a faint but perceptible change. “I was not aware,” he said, his voice a degree cooler than before. “Of course, a prior engagement must be honoured.”

Eliza watched with satisfaction as Marianne, blushing with confusion, was led away by the boisterous Mr. Gable, while Mr. Finch stood for a moment in thoughtful silence before asking the rector’s daughter to dance. The plan had worked admirably; he was, at the very least, annoyed.

Emboldened, Eliza proceeded to her second, more ingenious method. She took to praising, in Mr. Finch’s hearing, those qualities in a man which he so conspicuously lacked. “I do so admire a man of decisive action,” she remarked to her mother, as Mr. Finch arrived for tea. “A man who knows his own mind and is not afraid to speak it. Mr. Gable, for instance, told Marianne yesterday that her eyes were the colour of the sea after a storm. It is so romantic, is it not, to be compared to something so powerful and wild?”

Mr. Finch set his teacup down with a quiet precision that belied the sudden tension in his shoulders. He did not speak for some moments, and when he did, it was to ask Mrs. Fielding about the state of the turnips in the home field. Eliza felt a thrill of triumph. She was turning the screw, and he was beginning to feel the pressure.

The final, and most daring, of her designs involved a manufactured misunderstanding. Knowing Mr. Finch was to call on Tuesday, she instructed Marianne to be out, walking in the beech wood with their cousin from Winchester, a sensible but rather plain young woman named Anne. “You must be gone when he arrives,” Eliza insisted. “Absence, you see, must make his heart grow fonder, or at least more urgent.”

Marianne, weary of the entire business, agreed. When Mr. Finch called, he found Eliza alone in the drawing-room, looking unusually grave.

“Mr. Finch,” she began, without preamble, “I am afraid you find us in some distress. Marianne is not at home. She has gone for a walk, and… and I do not think she will be back for some time.”

Mr. Finch’s face paled. “Is she unwell?”

“No, not unwell. But her spirits are much altered. She has received an offer, you see.” Eliza paused, letting the word hang in the air. “A very handsome offer, from a gentleman of considerable standing.”

The colour drained from his face entirely. “An offer?”

“From our cousin, Mr. Devereux,” Eliza continued, inventing a name with astonishing facility. “He is a man of great property in Wiltshire, and is quite determined. He has pressed her for an answer, and I believe she is inclined to accept him. She feels that… that a long acquaintance, without any particular… conclusion… must be taken as a sign that no further interest is to be expected.”

This was the masterstroke. It was a direct, if veiled, accusation of his own inaction. Mr. Finch stood frozen, his hand clenched upon the back of a chair. The even temper, the placid countenance, the air of gentle indifference—all were gone, replaced by an expression of such stark, genuine shock that Eliza felt a sudden, unwelcome pang of alarm.

“I see,” he said, his voice low and strained. “I had… I had not thought… I see I have been mistaken.”

“There is no need to distress yourself, sir,” Eliza said, her own confidence beginning to waver. “Marianne speaks of you only with the greatest respect and friendship.”

“Friendship,” he repeated, the word sounding like a sentence. “Yes. Of course. Forgive me, Miss Eliza. I find I have an urgent matter of business to attend to. Good day.”

He bowed stiffly and left the room without another word. Eliza watched him go, her heart beating a frantic tattoo against her ribs. She had wanted to make him speak, to force a declaration. But this was not a declaration. This was a retreat. For the first time, it occurred to her that she might not have been playing a game of love, but of war, and that she had very nearly driven the enemy from the field for good.

The next two days passed in an agony of suspense. Mr. Finch did not call. Marianne, informed of the deception, was pale and silent. Eliza’s ingenious schemes now seemed the height of foolishness, a cruel and childish prank that might have cost her sister all chance of happiness. She had mistaken caution for indifference, and a quiet nature for a cold heart, and in her youthful arrogance, had sought to correct a fault that did not exist.

On the third afternoon, as a soft rain fell against the windowpanes, the sound of a horse’s hooves was heard in the lane. A moment later, Mr. Finch was shown into the room. He looked tired, and his usual composure was replaced by a solemnity that was far more affecting. He bowed to Mrs. Fielding, and then his eyes sought Marianne’s.

“Mrs. Fielding,” he said, his voice quiet but clear. “Miss Marianne. I have come to apologise for my abrupt departure the other day. I was… discomposed. I have been a fool, and I have wasted too much time in a state of cowardly indecision.”

He turned to Marianne, his gaze unwavering. “Miss Marianne, I have been in love with you since the first moment I saw you. I have been too cautious, too afraid of presumption, and too blind to see that my silence was causing you pain. I have no right to ask you to forget my folly, but I must ask, if it is not too late, if you will do me the honour of becoming my wife.”

The silence that followed was broken only by the gentle patter of the rain. Eliza, who had been holding her breath, saw her sister’s face break into a smile of such radiant happiness that all her own anxiety vanished.

“Oh, Mr. Finch,” Marianne said, her voice thick with emotion. “I thought you would never ask.”

He took her hand, and in that simple gesture was a world of feeling that two years of conversation had never expressed. Eliza, observing them from her seat by the fire, felt a quiet satisfaction. Her methods had been clumsy and almost disastrous, but the result, at last, was a happy one. She resolved, however, that in future, she would leave the business of courtship to those more qualified, and confine her own ingenuity to her embroidery.
