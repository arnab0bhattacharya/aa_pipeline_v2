The bell had rung an hour ago, or perhaps a lifetime; the sound was gone, leaving only the particular hush of a room where voices have been stilled, a hush that is not silence but the echo of noise. Mr. Thwaite sat in the master’s chair, the leather cracked like the skin of a winter apple, and looked at the desks. They were arranged in their rows, those wooden islands, scarred and gouged, holding the sediment of forty years. The light from the high windows, the grey Kentish light of a June afternoon, slanted across the floor, illuminating the dust motes in a slow, golden drift, a galaxy of tiny, drifting worlds.

He leaned forward, his hand, with its liver spots and thickened knuckles, resting on the lid of the nearest desk. He traced the carving there with his forefinger. *E. H. 1912*. Edward Hart. He had been a boy with a shock of red hair and a laugh that was too loud for the classroom, a laugh that had been silenced somewhere in the mud of the Somme. Or was it Ypres? The names of the battles were a geography of ghosts, a map of places he had never seen but which had claimed his boys. He tried to summon Edward’s face, the specific curve of his mouth, the way he chewed his pen when he was thinking about Latin grammar. But the face was a blur, a watercolour washed out by the rain of years. All that remained was the name, and the sound of that laugh, a phantom echo in the empty room.

He moved his hand to the next desk. *J. P. 1913*. James Piggott. A quiet boy, who had sat in the corner and watched the world with eyes that seemed too old for his face. He had been good at mathematics, a mind for figures and logic. Where was James now? Was he lying under a white cross in France, or had he survived, come home to a world that had no place for his quiet logic, and was he now sitting in an office in the City, his mind dulled by columns of numbers? Thwaite did not know. He had lost track of them all, these boys who had passed through his hands like water, like sand. He had been their master, their guide, their keeper of order, and yet he could not keep them from the chaos that had swallowed the world.

He stood up, his joints creaking in protest, and walked slowly along the rows. The floorboards groaned beneath his feet. He ran his hand over the tops of the desks, feeling the grain of the wood, the smooth patches worn by the elbows of generations. He stopped at a desk near the window. *R. M. 1914*. Robert Mallory. Now, Robert he remembered. A boy with a fierce intelligence and a passionate love for poetry. He had been a dreamer, his head always full of verses and stories. He had written an essay on Keats that had been so brilliant, so full of fire, that Thwaite had kept it for years, a testament to the power of the mind. But Robert had not gone to the war. He had died of influenza in the winter of 1918, his body too weak to fight off the fever that had swept through the school like a fire. Thwaite had visited him in the sanatorium, had seen the boy’s face, pale and wasted, his eyes still burning with that fierce light. He had died with a book of poems on his chest. Thwaite had buried him, and he had buried the essay with him, a final, futile offering to the gods of chance.

He sat down on a chair by the window, the cane seat sagging under his weight. He looked out at the school grounds. The cricket pitch was a sweep of green, the wicket a pale scar. The oak tree in the quadrangle was in full leaf, its branches heavy and dark. He could hear the distant sound of a lawnmower, a rhythmic, droning hum that was the sound of summer, the sound of life continuing, indifferent to the passage of time. He thought of the boys he had forgotten, the ones whose names were not carved into the desks, the ones who had passed through his classroom without leaving a mark. They were a faceless crowd, a sea of faces that had merged into one another, a blur of youth and hope and fear. Had he failed them? Had he given them the tools they needed to navigate the world? Or had he simply been a gatekeeper, ushering them out into the darkness?

A bird sang outside, a clear, piercing note that cut through the droning of the lawnmower. He closed his eyes and listened. The sound was so pure, so immediate, that it seemed to wash away the past, to clear the air of the ghosts and the memories. For a moment, he was not an old man sitting in an empty classroom, but a boy himself, sitting under the oak tree, listening to the birdsong, his mind full of the future, a future that was as bright and as clear as the summer sky. But the moment passed, and he opened his eyes. The classroom was still there, the desks still scarred, the light still slanting across the floor. He was an old man, and his future was a short, quiet road that led to a garden and a book and the slow, inevitable fading of the light.

He stood up again and walked to the blackboard. It was clean, wiped smooth by the caretaker, a black mirror reflecting his own tired face. He picked up a piece of chalk from the ledge. His hand trembled slightly as he raised it to the board. He wanted to write something, a final lesson, a last word of wisdom for the boys who would never return. But what could he say? What words could capture the complexity of it all, the beauty and the horror, the love and the loss? He stood there for a long moment, the chalk poised in his hand, the silence of the room pressing in on him. Then, slowly, deliberately, he wrote a single word. *Remember*.

He looked at the word, white and stark against the black board. It was a command, a plea, a prayer. He did not know who he was writing it for. For the boys? For himself? For the world? He put the chalk down and turned away. He walked to the door and paused, his hand on the handle. He took one last look at the classroom, at the desks and the chairs and the light. It was just a room, a collection of wood and glass and stone. But it was also a vessel, a container for the lives of all those boys, for the hopes and the dreams and the sorrows that had been poured into it over the years. He closed the door behind him, the click of the latch a small, final sound in the great, empty school. He walked down the corridor, his footsteps echoing on the stone floor, and out into the bright, indifferent afternoon.
