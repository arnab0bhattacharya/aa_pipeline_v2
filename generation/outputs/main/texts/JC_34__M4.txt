The harbour works at Puerto Almendra had reached, at the time of which I write, that stage of desolate completion when the engineer’s labour ceases to be a struggle with nature and becomes a mere dreary waiting upon the dilatoriness of contractors and the indifference of governments. For eighteen months I had watched the great mole stretch its grey arm into the Pacific, had seen the iron buckets swing and dip, had lived amid the shriek of winches and the monotonous thunder of the tipping-wagons, until the whole enterprise had come to seem the one fixed and rational fact in a world of shifting appearances. And it was in this season of pause—when the sea lay flat and lustrous as a sheet of hammered tin, and the heat pressed down upon the town like the hand of an invisible oppressor—that I first became aware of the Señorita Concepción Vidal.

She was the daughter of Don Anselmo Vidal, a man of consequence in the province, a lawyer, a rhetorician of the old school, whose speeches in the Café de la Constitución were said to be modelled upon the periods of Castelar, and who moved through the shabby splendours of Puerto Almendra with the air of a man who carries within him the destinies of a nation. I had met him at the British consulate, where he had spoken to me, with a grave and melancholy courtesy, of the necessity of progress, of the redeeming mission of capital, and of his own unalterable devotion to the cause of order and constitutional government. He was a tall, spare man, with a white beard cut square, and eyes that seemed to look beyond one at some vision of the republic’s future—or perhaps at some memory of its past. His daughter I saw first at a charity bazaar in the Plaza de Armas, where she presided over a stall of fancy-work with a gravity that sat oddly upon her youth, and where the clear pallor of her face, framed in the black lace of a mantilla, struck me with a kind of wonder, as though I had come upon a rare and fragile flower in a wilderness of dust and sun-baked brick.

I was a young man then, and the harbour was my life; but after that evening I found myself lingering of a Sunday in the shadow of the cathedral, or walking, with a pretence of idleness, beneath the arcades where the ladies of the town took their evening promenade. I was not long in discovering that Doña Concepción—Concha, they called her, with that soft diminutive which seems to rob a name of half its dignity—was as intelligent as she was beautiful. She had read, in a desultory fashion, the books that came to her father’s library, and she spoke of them with a frankness that was sometimes disconcerting. “You English,” she said to me one evening, “you believe that a bridge is a better thing than a poem. But my father believes that a speech is better than either. Which of you is right, I wonder?” I answered, I know not what, some platitude about the necessity of material foundations; and she looked at me with a curious, half-pitying smile, and said: “You are very sure of everything, Señor Grey. It must be a great comfort.”

The comfort, I may say, was not destined to be of long duration. For in the autumn of that year—the autumn of the year 1893, when the republic was already trembling upon the verge of those convulsions which were to shake it to its foundations—Don Anselmo Vidal, the constitutional lawyer, the pillar of order, the man of moderate and enlightened views, vanished suddenly from Puerto Almendra. It was given out, at first, that he had gone to the capital upon business of a legal nature; then, that he was confined to his bed by a sudden illness; and finally, when the truth could no longer be concealed, it was whispered in the cafés, and shouted in the streets, that Don Anselmo had thrown in his lot with the revolution, that he had crossed the mountains to join the rebel forces under General Montero, and that his name was already being spoken of in connexion with the provisional government which was to be established upon the ruins of the old régime.

I cannot describe to you the effect which this intelligence produced upon me. I had been, I think, upon the point of loving Concha Vidal—if, indeed, I had not already passed that point; and the thought that her father was a traitor to the constituted authorities, a man of blood and violence, a conspirator against the peace which I, as an engineer, was labouring to consolidate, filled me with a kind of dismay which was not altogether free from a certain shameful self-interest. I went to the house in the Calle de la Merced, where the Vidals lived, and I asked to see Doña Concepción. I was admitted, after a long delay, into a cool, dim drawing-room, where the shutters were closed against the glare, and where the Señorita received me with a composure that I found almost more disquieting than tears.

“You have come to speak to me of my father,” she said, before I could utter a word. “You are very kind. But I can tell you nothing—nothing, that is, that you do not already know. He has gone to fight for what he believes to be the right. He is with General Montero. He is well, or he was well when I last heard of him. And I—I am here, waiting.”

She spoke in a low, level voice, without any of that agitation which I had expected, and which, perhaps, I had half desired. Her eyes were fixed upon me with a steady, scrutinising gaze, as though she were measuring the depth of my sympathy, or the extent of my disapproval. I murmured something about the dangers of the enterprise, the strength of the government troops, the certainty of failure. She listened in silence, and then she said:

“You speak as an Englishman, Señor Grey—as a man who loves order, and peace, and the building of harbours. I understand you very well. But you must remember that we are not all Englishmen in this country. Some of us—my father, for instance—have other things to think of than harbours. He has thought of liberty, of justice, of the rights of the people. Perhaps he is mistaken. Perhaps the people do not want these things. But he has thought of them, and he has acted. And I—I am proud of him.”

There was a little tremor in her voice as she said the last words, and I saw her hands, clasped in her lap, tighten upon each other. I had a sudden vision of the old man with the white beard, standing in some mountain hut, surrounded by rough soldiers, dreaming his dreams of a regenerated republic; and I confess that the vision moved me strangely, though I told myself that it was all folly, that the man was a disturber of the public peace, and that his daughter—his daughter was a woman, and therefore not to be judged by the ordinary standards of political morality.

The days that followed were days of confusion and alarm. The government had proclaimed a state of siege; arrests were made daily; the streets were patrolled by soldiers, and the evening air was heavy with rumours of battles, of massacres, of the approach of the rebel army. The British consul advised all English residents to leave the port while there was yet time. A steamer, the *Cordillera*, was to sail for Valparaíso on the fifteenth of the month, and I had engaged a passage upon her. But when the fifteenth came—when the last boat was waiting at the mole, and the captain was shouting his imprecations from the bridge—I found myself standing upon the quay, with my portmanteau at my feet, unable to take the final step.

I had seen Concha that morning. She had come to me, for the first time, in my own house, with her mantilla drawn close about her face, and her eyes red with weeping. She had received news of her father. He was alive, but he was ill—wounded, perhaps, she could not tell—and he was in hiding in the mountains, with a price upon his head. She had come to ask me—she, the proud daughter of Don Anselmo Vidal—to help her to reach him. “You have money,” she said, “and you have influence. You are an Englishman; they will not suspect you. I have no one else to turn to. You are the only friend I have in the world.”

I looked at her as she stood there, pale and trembling, with her hands outstretched to me in an agony of supplication; and I knew that I could not refuse her. I knew, too, that in refusing or in accepting I was deciding the whole course of my life. The harbour, the mole, the great works which I had built—they seemed to me, at that moment, things of no more consequence than the toys of a child. I took her hands in mine, and I said:

“I will not leave you. I will stay—whatever happens.”

She raised her eyes to mine, and in that look there was something which I have never forgotten—a kind of wonder, a kind of gratitude, and, beneath it all, a shadow of that irony which I had noticed in her from the first. “You are very good,” she said, “but you do not understand. You are an Englishman. You cannot understand.” And she turned away, and left me standing in the empty room, with the noise of the harbour coming faintly through the closed shutters, and the last whistle of the *Cordillera* sounding across the water like the cry of a departing world.
