The rain in Hampshire did not fall; it hovered, a persistent, grey mist that clung to the stone walls of Elmsworth Manor and seeped into the marrow of anything living within it. Eleanor sat by the window in the drawing room, her fingers tracing the condensation on the glass, watching the village of Oakhaven dissolve into the fog. Two years. That was the duration of her silence, her patience, and her slow, suffocating erosion.

Julian was expected at half-past four. He was always punctual, a trait Eleanor had once admired as a sign of reliability but now found merely mechanical. Julian Ashcroft was a good man. He was kind to the servants, respectful to her father, and possessed of a heart so soft it seemed almost formless. He was also, without a doubt, dull. His conversation was a flat plain with no peaks, no valleys, no surprises. He spoke of the price of turnips, the quality of wool, and the inevitable boredom of the country life with a monotone that could lull a stone to sleep.

The door opened, and the scent of damp wool and earth entered the room. Julian stood there, his hat in his hand, his face flushed from the walk across the wet moors. He looked up, and his eyes, usually so gentle, brightened with a simple, uncomplicated affection that made Eleanor’s chest tighten with a sudden, violent guilt.

"Good evening, my dear," he said, his voice steady and warm. "I hope the journey was not too wet for you."

"Hardly noticed it," Eleanor replied, though her hands were trembling. She stood up, smoothing the front of her muslin dress. "May I have a moment alone with you, Julian? Before my mother sends up tea?"

Julian hesitated, then nodded, setting his hat on the side table. "Of course. Is something wrong? You seem pale."

It was the way he asked—concerned, open, devoid of suspicion. He did not look at her as a woman might look at a man she feared losing, but as a friend who had spilled a drop of ink on a ledger. Eleanor moved to the center of the room, turning away from the window so that the grey light fell behind her.

"Julian," she began, and the word felt heavy in her mouth, like a stone she had been carrying for a long time. "I have been thinking. A great deal. Since my last visit to London."

Julian’s smile faltered, just slightly. "London? You mentioned you were to stay with the Hargreaves. Was it not pleasant? I remember you saying their library was quite extensive."

"It was pleasant," Eleanor said, the lie sitting lightly on her tongue because it was the truth, but not the whole truth. "I met people. I heard music. I saw the world moving, Julian. And when I came back here, to this quiet, to this... safety, I felt as though I had been asleep for two years. And I woke up, and I could not find the dream."

Julian shifted his weight, his hands clasping and unclasping in front of him. "I do not follow, Eleanor. You have never spoken of dreams. You have always preferred the comfort of the hearth to the noise of the city."

"That is the point," she said, her voice rising slightly, then softening. "I thought I preferred it. I thought my comfort was in your steadiness. But I think I have confused safety with happiness. And I think... I think I do not love you, Julian. Or rather, I do love you as a brother, as a dear friend. But the spark, the fire, the thing that makes one’s heart race when a man enters a room—it is not there. It never was, or if it was, it has gone out."

The silence that followed was louder than the rain. Julian did not look angry. He did not look hurt, not yet. He simply looked confused, as if she had spoken in a foreign tongue. He took a step toward her, his expression earnest.

"Eleanor, surely you jest. We have been engaged for two years. Your father and my father have spoken of the wedding for the spring. I have prepared the estate. I have done everything I can to make this a happy union. If you are unhappy, tell me what I can change. I am not a perfect man, but I am devoted to you. I will wait. I will wait as long as you need to find your footing."

His devotion was a cage. That was the terrible realization that struck her now, with the force of a physical blow. He would not fight for her. He would not challenge her. He would simply wait, enduring, patient, good. He would let her stay in this grey, damp prison for another ten years if she asked him to. And that was worse than any cruelty.

"I cannot ask you to wait, Julian," she said, her voice shaking. "And I cannot ask you to change. I cannot ask you to be wild, or risky, or anything other than who you are. But I cannot marry you. I would be doing you a great injustice. You deserve a woman who looks at you with the same depth of feeling you look at me with. I do not have it."

Julian’s face drained of color. For a moment, he looked very young, very small. "But your family," he whispered. "The Ashcrotts and the Whitmores. The alliance. The land. Your father..."

"My father will be displeased," Eleanor said. The fear was a cold knot in her stomach, a sharp, physical pain. She knew what this meant. It meant the loss of the manor, the loss of her status, the loss of the security that had been her life’s foundation. It meant she would be the unmarried daughter, the one who had thrown away a golden opportunity for the sake of a fleeting feeling. It meant the whispers in Oakhaven, the pitying looks from the women at church, the disappointment in her father’s eyes that would never heal.

"Is it worth it?" Julian asked. His voice was barely a breath. "Is whatever you seek in London, or in these other men, worth losing everything you have? Worth losing me?"

Eleanor looked at him, really looked at him, for the last time. She saw the kindness in his eyes, the absolute lack of malice. She saw a man who would never hurt her, a man who would never bore her, a man who would keep her safe from the storms of the world. And she realized, with a clarity that was almost painful, that safety was not enough. To live safely was to not live at all.

"Yes," she said. And the word was a door closing, a lock turning. "It is worth it."

Julian nodded slowly. He reached for his hat, his movements stiff and deliberate. "I will go, then. I will speak to your father in the morning. I hope... I hope you find what you are looking for, Eleanor. I hope it is better than what I could offer."

He walked to the door, paused, and looked back. "You are a strong woman, Eleanor. I always knew that. I just hoped you would never have to use that strength against those you love."

Then he was gone, the door clicking shut behind him, leaving the sound of the rain to fill the void. Eleanor remained standing in the center of the drawing room, alone. The fear was still there, sharp and biting, but beneath it, for the first time in two years, she felt a pulse. It was fast, and it was alive. She turned back to the window, watching the mist swirl around the manor, no longer a grey curtain, but a veil being lifted. The future was uncertain, terrifying, and open. She took a deep breath, the air cold and sharp in her lungs, and for the first time, she did not feel suffocated. She felt awake.
