The brass plate on the door of the surgery in Millbrook had been polished to a fare-thee-well, and the name beneath it—Dr. Elias Thorne—seemed to catch the weak afternoon light with a particular, self-satisfied gleam. It was a small vanity, but then, Thorne had earned it. In the two years since he’d taken over the flagging practice from old Dr. Ainsworth, he had built it into the most sought-after in the district. His secret was not a secret at all, but a pale, bitter-tasting tincture he’d concocted in the cramped dispensary behind the surgery. He called it his Vigor Draught, and he prescribed it, in carefully measured doses, to the overworked mothers, the weary farmhands, and the anxious clerks of the town. It did not cure their ailments, but it banished the exhaustion that made them seem so much worse. It was, he told himself, a humane and modern approach.

His assistant, a quiet, methodical man named Silas Rook, had been the one to refine the formula. It was Rook who first discovered that the active principle, when distilled and concentrated, could suppress the need for sleep entirely in a laboratory mouse. The creature had run its little wheel for three days straight, a frantic, ceaseless motion, before its heart gave out. Rook had noted this with a detached sort of interest, but Thorne had seen the potential. Not for his patients—good heavens, no. For himself.

The demands of a country practice were relentless. There were calvings at midnight, fevers that spiked at dawn, and the endless, draining parade of human worry. Thorne found himself dreaming of his bed, of the simple, blessed oblivion of eight uninterrupted hours. One evening, staring at a vial of the concentrated serum, he succumbed. He measured a dose into a glass of water, drank it down, and waited. The effect was not a jolt, but a gentle, inexorable lifting of a great weight from his mind. The fog of fatigue evaporated. His thoughts, for the first time in years, were crystalline.

That night, he reorganised his entire filing system, balanced the practice ledgers, and read a full volume of medical journals. When the sun rose, he felt no desire for sleep. He saw his morning patients with a clarity and patience that astonished even himself. He was, he realised, finally the doctor he had always meant to be.

The habit took hold. Soon, the small, bitter glass was as much a part of his morning ritual as his shave and his cup of tea. He stopped sleeping altogether. The nights, once a torment of exhaustion, became a gift. He visited the sick in their homes before they had even risen, his black bag swinging at his side as he walked the silent, dew-kissed lanes. He delivered babies at all hours, his hands steady and his mind sharp. He was tireless, efficient, and his reputation grew to mythic proportions. The people of Millbrook began to say that Dr. Thorne was a marvel, a man who seemed to have been carved from granite and animated by some divine purpose. His practice flourished, and the brass plate on his door seemed to shine ever brighter.

But Silas Rook watched. He watched as the shadows under Thorne’s eyes deepened to a permanent, bruised purple, like the faint beginnings of decay. He watched as his employer’s skin took on a greyish, papery cast, stretched taut over his cheekbones. He watched him eat, a mechanical, joyless process, as if food were merely fuel for a furnace that never cooled. Thorne’s conversation, once lively, became clipped and purely functional. He no longer lingered for a cup of tea in a patient’s kitchen, no longer asked after their grandchildren or their harvest. He was a machine for healing, and nothing more.

The patients noticed too, though they could not articulate it. They were grateful, of course. He was always there, always prompt, his diagnoses swift and sure. Yet, there was a growing unease. It was in the way he would stand by a sickbed, his gaze not on the sufferer’s face but on the pulse beating in their wrist, as if he were observing a fascinating natural phenomenon rather than a fellow creature in pain. It was in the cool, dry touch of his fingers. It was in the way he would sometimes stand in the town square at dusk, his head tilted, listening to sounds no one else could hear.

Mrs. Gable, the baker’s wife, was the first to put words to the feeling. “He’s a good doctor, to be sure,” she confided to her neighbour, her voice a low whisper. “But when he looks at you, it’s like he’s looking right through you. Like you’re a clock, and he’s just waiting to see what makes you tick.”

The end came on a Tuesday. A young boy, the son of a local farmer, had been thrown from a hay cart and broken his leg. The bone was a compound fracture, a nasty, splintered thing. Dr. Thorne arrived within minutes, his manner as brisk and efficient as ever. He set the bone with a swift, practiced precision that would have been the envy of a London surgeon. The boy, pale and sweating with shock, was given a dose of the Vigor Draught for the pain and exhaustion.

As Thorne worked, the boy’s mother, a sturdy woman named Martha, watched him. She saw the unnatural stillness of his hands, the way his eyes, devoid of any warmth or sympathy, tracked the boy’s every twitch and moan. The surgery was silent save for the boy’s ragged breathing and the neat, crisp sounds of the splints being applied.

When it was done, Thorne straightened up. He did not offer a word of comfort to the terrified mother. He simply packed his bag, his movements economical and soundless.

“He’ll need to be watched,” Thorne said, his voice flat and without inflection. “The fever may come. I will return in the morning.”

He turned to leave, and as he did, his gaze fell upon the boy’s face. The boy, his eyes wide with a fear that transcended the pain, was staring at the doctor. In the dim light of the farmhouse kitchen, Thorne’s face seemed less like a man’s and more like a mask, a perfect, bloodless replica. His eyes, for a fleeting second, did not seem to focus on the boy at all, but on some point in the middle distance, a point that was nowhere and everywhere. He looked, for all the world, as if he were listening to the silence.

A profound, primal terror seized the boy. He screamed, a raw, piercing sound that shattered the quiet. He screamed not from the pain in his leg, but from the sight of the thing that stood before him, the thing that wore the face of Dr. Thorne.

Thorne did not flinch. He merely observed the screaming boy with a detached, clinical curiosity, as if noting the symptoms of a new and interesting disease. He made a small, mental note, then turned and walked out into the night, leaving behind a terrified family, a freshly set bone, and the lingering, bitter scent of his Vigor Draught. He had a long walk ahead of him, and he was not in the least bit tired.
