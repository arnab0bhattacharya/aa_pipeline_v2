If any one street in the whole of London might be said to have acted as the chief nursery, breeding-ground, and exhausting reservoir of Mud, it was unhesitatingly Puddleby Court, lying in the immediate and unwholesome shadow of the Seven Dials. The mud of Puddleby Court was not the clean, honest, country mud that washes away with a brisk shower, but a thick, tenacious, compound mud, made up of soot, cabbage-water, broken oyster-shells, and the accumulated drift and dregs of a hundred alleyways, all stirred together into a black and bubbling soup by the tread of a thousand weary feet. The houses that hemmed in this dismal slough leaned so lovingly towards one another across the narrow thoroughfare, as if whispering the dark secrets of their rottenness into each other's crumbling ears, that they quite shut out the sky, except for a narrow, sooty ribbon far above, through which a solitary star might occasionally peep, merely to see how wretched humanity could be, and hasten to hide its face again in shame.

In the lowest, dampest, most profoundly dismal of these dwellings—number seven, to be precise, though the number itself was so obscured by grime as to be a matter of pure conjecture—dwelt Mrs. Martha Grimple, a washerwoman. Mrs. Grimple was a widow, and the mother of five small Grimples, who ranged in age from twelve months to eleven years, and who were distinguished by an uniform absence of shoes and a universal prevalence of elbows. Mrs. Grimple’s washing was done in a great, misshapen, battered copper in a shed that leaned against the back-wall of her room, a shed so rickety that it appeared to be supported in its upright position solely by its own astonishment at not having fallen long ago.

It was into this copper, one biting January morning, that Mrs. Grimple, lighting an early candle to begin her week’s labour, thrust her red and chapped hand to test the water, and found not water, but a boy. He was a boy so excessively thin, so utterly and completely devoid of any superfluous flesh, that he had slipped down the flue and lay curled up at the bottom like a skeleton in a nut-shell. His hair was the colour of a neglected sweep’s brush; his eyes, when he opened them in the glare of Mrs. Grimple’s tallow candle, were two enormous, scared, saucer-like pools of blue, staring out of a face of a paleness that rivalled her own best starch; and his garments were so many and so torn, hanging about him in such innumerable tatters, that he resembled less a human boy than an animated bundle of dish-clouts.

"Wot a-going!" cried Mrs. Grimple, starting back so forcibly that she extinguished her candle in the suds. "Speak, ghost, or gally-bagger, or whatever ye be, afore I fetch the parish!"

"I ain't a ghost," whispered a voice from the depths of the copper, tremulously. "I'm only a boy. Please, mum, I'm most partic'ly starved, and the copper was warm."

Now, Mrs. Grimple was a woman of a remarkably soft heart, which she had found, in the rough schooling of Puddleby Court, to be a very great nuisance indeed. She was, besides, the mother of five. She looked at the starved boy; she thought of her own five small Grimples, and how they would look if they had no crust to gnaw and no hole to creep into; and the foolish tears started to her eyes.

"Come out of that, then, thou morsel of a misery," said she, lifting him out with a tenderness that would have astonished her neighbours, and setting him down upon a three-legged stool before the expiring fire. "Here, take and eat."

She gave him a crust of bread, hard as the heart of a parish beadle, and a cup of cold water. He ate the bread as if it were the most delicious plum-cake, and drank the water as if it were the richest wine, and the blue eyes lost something of their terrified stare, and the pale face began to look a shade less like a ghost's.

It was in this posture of blissful feeding that Mrs. Grimple's next-door neighbour, the irascible and deeply suspicious Mr. Squeege, burst into the room. Mr. Squeege was a dealer in second-hand door-knockers, a profession that had imbued him with a profound contempt for humanity. He was a little, dry, crisp, crackling man, who snapped his words out as if they were so many dry biscuits.

"Ho!" said Mr. Squeege, staring at the boy. "Ho! A boy! A stray boy, Mrs. Grimple! A copperful of boy! And what, pray, is the meaning of this, Mrs. Grimple?"

"It's my nevvy," said Mrs. Grimple, with a promptitude and a hardihood that surprised even herself. "My nevvy, come from the country."

"Your nevvy!" snorted Mr. Squeege. "A pretty nevvy! A copper-boiling nevvy! I know your nevvy, Mrs. Grimple! I know your five Grimples, and I know your nevvy! He’s a workhouse brat, and I'll lay a dozen of my best brass knockers to a broken bell-pull that the beadle's a-looking for him this very minute. You're a fool, Mrs. Grimple!"

"I may be a fool, Mr. Squeege," returned Mrs. Grimple, drawing herself up to her full, though somewhat stunted, height, "but I'm not such a fool as to turn a starved rat out of a warm hole. And if you say nevvy agin, I'll chuck this here dipper at your head."

Mr. Squeege retreated, crackling with indignation, and Mrs. Grimple knew that her secret would be public property in Puddleby Court within a quarter of an hour. She looked at the boy. The boy looked at her.

"Look ye here, my lad," said she, "they'll be coming to take ye. The parish beadle, Mr. Bumble—for such is the nature of beadles—will be here. And if he finds ye, back ye go to the stone jug, to be fed on skilly and driven to the treadmill. Will ye be my nevvy?"

"I'll be anything, mum," said the boy, fervently, "if so be as I may stay here."

"Then a nevvy ye shall be," said Mrs. Grimple. "And mind, Grimples," she added, turning to her five, who had gathered round in a wondering semicircle, "this is your nevvy, come from the country. His name is—his name is—" she cast about for a name, and her eye falling upon an old, torn, and much-stained ballad sheet pasted on the wall, representing the heroic end of a certain legendary personage, she caught it up—"his name is Jack. And if any one of you says he ain't, I'll skin ye alive."

The five Grimples, deeply impressed by this threat, nodded their five heads with one accord, and Jack was formally installed as a member of the family.

But the danger was not yet over. Even as Mrs. Grimple was hiding Jack under a heap of damp linen, the heavy, measured, and pompous tramp of authority was heard in the court. It was Mr. Grumple, the parish beadle, a man of a most portly and imposing presence, who carried his head as if it were a particularly large and valuable pumpkin, and who looked upon the inhabitants of Puddleby Court as so many noxious weeds, to be rooted up and transplanted to the workhouse whenever he pleased.

He stopped at the door of number seven, and knocked with his cane—a knock that was a perfect compound of authority, suspicion, and contempt.

"Mrs. Grimple!" he cried, in a voice that vibrated through the damp shed like the tolling of a muffled bell. "Mrs. Grimple, I have information. A boy, a stray boy, a workhouse boy, is concealed on these premises!"

"Law, sir!" cried Mrs. Grimple, opening the door with a face of innocent wonder. "A boy! In this 'ouse? Why, I've got five of my own, and that's enough and to spare, without taking in boys."

"Ay, ay," said Mr. Grumple, pushing past her and glaring round the room with his small, sharp eyes. "But there is a sixth. Squeege has informed me. Where is he? Where is the boy?"

"There ain't no boy," said Mrs. Grimple, with a firmness that would have done credit to the Bench. "There's me, and the five Grimples, and my nevvy."

"Your nevvy!" repeated Mr. Grumple, with a withering sneer. "A likely story! And pray, Mrs. Grimple, where may this precious nevvy be?"

"He's a-washing his face," said Mrs. Grimple, with a sudden inspiration, as a splashing was heard from the direction of the copper. "Jack, come out and speak to the gentleman."

And from the copper, dripping like a drowned rat, but with a face scrubbed to a shining redness, emerged Jack, the stray boy.

Mr. Grumple glared at him. Jack stared at Mr. Grumple, with his blue eyes wide open, and a look of such profound and artless innocence that it would have melted a heart of stone.

"His name is Jack, sir," said Mrs. Grimple, "come from the country, to larn the washing trade."

Mr. Grumple looked at Jack; he looked at Mrs. Grimple; he looked at the five Grimples, who were nodding their heads with a persistence that was almost fiendish. He was baffled. He could not prove that Jack was not Mrs. Grimple's nevvy. The law, which he represented, was powerful, but it could not compel a boy to be a stranger to his own aunt.

"Humph!" said Mr. Grumple, turning to go. "Take care, Mrs. Grimple. Take care. The parish has a long arm, and a sharp eye. If this nevvy of yours turns out to be a workhouse brat—"

"Then the parish will be wrong, sir," said Mrs. Grimple, "as it often is."

Mr. Grumple retired, baffled and majestic, and Mrs. Grimple shut the door behind him with a bang that shook the crazy shed to its foundations. Then she turned to Jack, and Jack turned to her, and the five Grimples turned to both of them, and a great, foolish, tearful, triumphant laugh went up from the whole six, which was the best and only music that Puddleby Court had ever heard.
