If any lonely ghost, drifting upon the ebb-tide of that murky estuary, had peered into the counting-house of Mr. Pumblechook in the town of Saltcomb, it might have wondered why a small boy stood trembling before a vast mahogany desk, rather than sitting comfortably at home. But the small boy—whose name was Timothy Pipsqueak, and who had lately been rendered an orphan by a damp grave in Saltcomb churchyard—had no comfortable home to sit in. His father, a modest clerk of tally-whisker proportions, had departed this life owing precisely seven pounds, four shillings, and sixpence to Mr. Pumblechook, a creditor of elephantine memory and corresponding bulk. And Mr. Pumblechook, having surveyed the deceased's effects and found them wanting, had fixed upon the living son as the most promising asset.

"He is twelve," said Mr. Pumblechook, speaking to the shipping agent as though Timothy were a sack of coals, "and small for his age, which is an economy of space. The *Greedy Gull* wants a cabin boy. Let him be a cabin boy. The sea makes men, and what it does not make, it drowns, which is a solution either way."

The shipping agent, a man who smelled of tar and looked as though he had been marinated in it, agreed. And so Timothy Pipsqueak found himself conveyed, in a state of considerable terror, to the quayside, where the collier brig *Greedy Gull* lay wallowing at her moorings like a fat black sow asleep in a puddle.

She was a broad-beamed, heavy-arsed vessel, painted the colour of sin, and her deck was so thoroughly caked with coal-dust that one might have believed her cargo had been not merely carried but ground into her very bones. The rigging was stiff with it; the ropes were black; the sails, when they were hoisted, went up not white but grey, like the shirts of the very poor hung out to dry across a London rooftop. Her crew moved about her decks in a perpetual twilight of soot, their faces so begrimed that only the whites of their eyes distinguished them from the deck itself, and even those whites were yellowed.

Timothy was conducted aboard by the mate, a stooped and bilious man named Mr. Grubb, who communicated entirely in grunts and expectorations, and who seemed to regard the boy as an inconvenience roughly equivalent to a leak. He was shown a hole in the bows which he was told was his berth, and which smelled of bilge, old fish, and the despair of previous cabin boys. He was told that his duties included sweeping, holystoning, fetching, carrying, and not dying. He was not told how he was to manage the last.

The *Greedy Gull* carried coal from Newcastle to London, a trade which she conducted with all the urgency of a funeral procession and all the grace of a drunkard crossing a dance floor. She rolled, she pitched, she lurched; she groaned in her timbers as though lamenting every ton of black cargo in her hold. Timothy, who had never been to sea, was ill before the Tyne was behind them, and remained ill for three days in a convulsion of misery that made him wish, with genuine fervour, for the damp grave in Saltcomb churchyard.

No one aboard the *Greedy Gull* asked after Timothy's welfare. The crew were men of few words and those mostly unrepeatable. They spoke in a dialect compounded of Northumbrian guttural and sailors' profanity, and their conversation was conducted at a volume that suggested each man believed his shipmate to be not merely deaf but in another county. When they noticed Timothy at all, it was to send him sprawling with a casual boot, or to roar for him to fetch something he could not name and could not find. He became, in the economy of the vessel, a sort of animated mop—a thing that fetched and carried and was kicked when it was slow.

The cook was another matter.

He was called Mr. Trencher, and he was the largest man Timothy had ever seen, and the most terrible. He stood six feet and four inches in his stockings, and his stockings were black, because everything aboard the *Greedy Gull* was black. He had one eye—the left—which was a small, bright, ferret-like organ that gleamed from the depths of a face so broad and so encrusted with soot and sweat that it resembled a vast scorched loaf. Where his right eye had been, there was a patch of black cloth, and above the patch, a scar ran up into his hair like a ploughed furrow crossing a dark field. His arms were like the limbs of oak trees, and his hands were like the shovels with which the coal was loaded. He presided over his galley—a iron box no bigger than a confessional, in which he produced meals of a darkness and density that matched the cargo—with a temperament that was, to put it gently, volcanic.

Mr. Trencher threw things. He threw pots, he threw pans, he threw ladles, he threw language. When the fire was too low, he swore at the coal. When the fire was too high, he swore at the coal. When the fire was exactly right, he swore at the coal for not having the decency to be inconsistent, so that he might have something to complain about that was novel. His voice was a deep bass roar that vibrated in the timbers, and when he was angry—which was the condition in which he existed from dawn to dusk—the galley chimney appeared to tremble, and the pots to cower on their hooks.

Timothy was terrified of Mr. Trencher. He approached the galley as a mouse approaches a cat's den, which is to say with his heart in his mouth and his feet prepared for flight. He was sent to collect the crew's meals—messes of salt pork and pease-pudding and hard bread that had the consistency of geological specimens—and each time he reached for the pot, he expected Mr. Trencher's vast hand to descend upon his skull and crush it like an egg.

But on the fourth evening, as Timothy stood shaking at the galley threshold, Mr. Trencher paused in the middle of a tirade against the ship's boy who had failed to bring him fresh water, and turned his single bright eye upon the child.

"What's the matter with you?" he said, in a voice like the rumble of stones in a barrel.

Timothy, who had been asked no question by any soul since his father died, could not answer. His mouth opened, and his chin wobbled, and a great salt tear rolled down his cheek, cutting a clean white channel through the coal-dust on his face.

Mr. Trencher stared at him. The galley fire crackled. A pot bubbled. The ship groaned.

"Sit down," said Mr. Trencher, pushing a stool towards him with his foot—a stool that skidded across the deck like a hockey puck.

Timothy sat. Mr. Trencher, with movements that were surprisingly careful for so vast a man, ladled something from a pot into a bowl and set it before the boy. It was a stew, black as the cargo and thick as mortar, and it smelled, beneath the smoke, of something that was almost, in a certain light, meat.

"Eat that," said Mr. Trencher.

Timothy ate. It was the first thing he had eaten in four days that did not immediately wish to return the way it had come. He ate, and the warmth of it spread through him like a small, brave fire lit in a vast, cold house.

Mr. Trencher watched him with his one eye, and said nothing for a long while. Then he said: "Who's dead, then?"

"My father," whispered Timothy.

Mr. Trencher grunted. He turned back to his galley, and picked up a pot, and did not throw it. He stirred something, and swore at it, and then, over his shoulder, said:

"You'll do."

He said nothing more that night. But the next morning, when Timothy came to collect the crew's breakfast, Mr. Trencher looked at him with his single ferret eye and said: "How are you, then?"

And Timothy, bewildered and trembling in the black galley of the *Greedy Gull*, found that he could answer.
