The first thing that struck me upon stepping off the train at the station, which the clerk in the ticket office called Paddington but which I suspect is merely a name for a place of great confusion, was the sheer volume of the air. In the valley of the Kethri, the air is thin, cold, and still, smelling perpetually of dried dung and pine resin. Here, the air is thick, warm, and tastes of coal smoke and wet mud. It presses against the ears with a physical weight, a constant, roaring hum that seems to come from the ground itself, as if the earth were a great engine. I stood there for a full minute, clutching my straw hat, waiting for the sound to cease, but it only intensified. A cab, a black box on four wheels pulled by a horse that looked half-starved and half-feral, swept past with a squeal of iron on iron that made my teeth ache. Behind it came another, and another, until the street was a river of black and noise. I felt then, as I had felt when I first saw the glacier calve into the lake, that I was standing at the edge of a precipice, and that the ground beneath me was not solid at all, but merely a thin sheet of ice over a chasm of fire.

I had been in London for three days. My uncle, Mr. Alistair Thorne, who is a solicitor and a man of very precise habits, had taken me to a lodging house in Kensington. The rooms were small, carpeted in a red that seemed to pulse in the gaslight, and smelled of lavender and old paper. But it was the city that held me in its jaws. I went out each morning to walk, for I am told that walking is the primary occupation of the English, and I wished to observe them with the same detachment I once applied to the yaks grazing on the high pastures.

They are a strange people, these Londoners. They move with a hurried, shuffling gait, their heads down, their eyes fixed on the pavement or on the next person, as if they are guarding themselves against a sudden attack. They do not look at one another. In the valley, a man’s face is his book; here, they wear masks of indifference. I saw a woman in a bonnet who looked as if she had not eaten for a week, yet she carried a basket of fruit and spoke to a child with a voice of steel. I saw a man in a top hat who stumbled over a puddle, and instead of helping him, the crowd parted around him like water around a stone, and continued on their way. It is a savage custom, this indifference. We do not do so in the Kethri. If a man falls, the whole village gathers. Here, if a man falls, the city steps over him.

And then there are the machines. This is what truly frightens me, though I confess it also fascinates me. The underground railway, which they call the Tube, is a subterranean pit from which emerge trains of iron that roar like dragons. I went down once, with my uncle, and the experience was so violent that I nearly fainted. The tunnel was dark and hot, and the air was stale. The train came in with a screech that seemed to tear the fabric of the air, and the doors opened to reveal a press of bodies, men and women standing shoulder to shoulder, their faces pale and dead. There was no space, no air, only the smell of sweat and oil. I climbed out with my heart hammering against my ribs, and I have not gone down since. It is a machine for moving people, but it seems to me a machine for erasing them.

But the surface machines are no less terrible. The tramways, with their electric sparks and clattering wheels, the omnibuses with their heavy bells, the locomotives that spew black smoke into the sky—everywhere there is a sense of force, of power harnessed and unleashed. I watched a steamroller one day, a great iron beast that ground the road into submission. It moved with a slow, inevitable purpose, and the horses avoided it, and the men stood back. It was not a living thing, yet it had the look of a predator. I began to wonder if the English are not masters of these machines, but their servants. They seem to worship them. They bow to the clock, they rush to the train, they are slaves to the speed of the city. In the valley, we follow the sun. Here, they follow the whistle.

I wrote to my father yesterday, telling him of these things. I told him that the English eat their food in silence, that they speak in low tones, and that they are afraid of the dark, for they light their streets with gas until dawn. I told him that they have no respect for the land, for they build upon it, they dig into it, they cover it with stone. I told him that they are a people of the indoors, that they live in boxes and move in boxes, and that they are strange, pale things, like moles that have come up into the light.

My father’s reply came last week. It was short, as his letters always are. He said that I must study hard, that I must learn the language well, and that I must not forget where I came from. He said that the mountain is the only true home, and that the city is a trap. I know he is right. I know that I am out of place here. But I find that I cannot leave. There is something in the noise, something in the speed, that holds me. It is like a fever. I dream of the valley, of the white peaks and the silence, but when I wake, I hear the roar of the city, and it is so loud that I cannot sleep.

Last night, I went to the park. It was late, and the gas lamps were burning. The park is a green space in the middle of the stone, a place where the city tries to pretend it is not a city. I sat on a bench and watched the people. They walked in pairs, in threes, in fives. They did not speak much. They looked at the trees, but they did not see them. They looked at the sky, but they did not see the stars. They were here, but they were not there. I felt a great loneliness, a loneliness that is not of the body, but of the spirit. I am a man of the mountain, and the mountain is far away. I am a stranger in my own world.

I think of the missionary who came to our valley, who told us of God and of heaven. He spoke of order, of law, of the divine. But here, I see a different kind of order. It is the order of the machine. It is the order of the clock. It is a cold, hard order, without mercy, without grace. And yet, it is powerful. It moves millions of people, it feeds them, it houses them. It is a great beast, and we are its food.

I must study. My uncle expects me to sit for my examinations next month. I must learn the history of England, the laws of England, the science of England. I must learn to think as they think. But I am afraid that I will lose myself in the process. I am afraid that I will become one of them, a pale, silent thing, moving through the noise, wearing the mask of indifference. I am afraid that I will forget the smell of the pine, the sound of the wind, the face of my mother.

I will write to my father again. I will tell him that I am well, that I am studying, that I am adapting. I will not tell him of the fear, of the noise, of the machines. I will tell him that the city is beautiful, that the people are kind, that the air is fresh. It is a lie, but it is the lie that he wants to hear. And perhaps, in time, it will become true. Perhaps, in time, I will be able to forget the mountain. But for now, I am here, in the roar, in the smoke, in the dark. And I am afraid.
