It was with a heart exhausted by its own anxieties, and a frame already half-subdued by the long mutiny of the ocean, that I drew near to the shores of Lisbon. The pestilence, that silent and invisible conqueror, had for three long years held our desolate England in its thrall; it had emptied our villages, it had silenced our universities, and had filled the very air we breathed with a funereal quietude. It was the silence of a graveyard, spread over a whole nation. When, therefore, the rumour reached our few remaining scholars—a rumour as faint and as precious as a whisper of hope in the ear of the dying—that in the far southern city of Lisbon, a remedy had at last been wrested from the grasp of the malady, I felt within my soul an impulse I could not resist. I, Arthur Penhaligon, a physician whose art had been made a mockery by this great unmaking, resolved to abandon the grey shores of my dying country and sail upon that desperate quest.

The voyage itself was a passage through a world remade; we saw no other sail upon the vast expanse of the Atlantic, only the great, melancholy procession of the waves, and the clouds, which seemed themselves to carry the weight of the world’s sorrow. Yet as we entered the Tagus, and the city rose before me, I was struck by an astonishment that for a moment overrode my grief. Lisbon was not as I had imagined it—not a city of ruin, or of desperate, feverish activity, but a place of an almost supernatural calm. The ancient towers stood serene against the blue sky; the terracotta roofs were whole and glowing in the sun; and the streets, though not crowded, were inhabited by people whose placid countenances and orderly movements spoke of a life untroubled by the shadow that lay upon the rest of Europe. A profound and chilling tranquillity reigned here, a peace so absolute it felt like an affront to the memory of all that had been lost.

My inquiries, made with the earnest haste of a man who has staked his last hope upon a single venture, were met with a courteous but impenetrable politeness. I sought out the great infirmaries, the university, the halls of the city’s magistrates; I presented my credentials, I spoke of the dying children in the north, of the silent libraries of Oxford, of the fields that lay fallow for want of hands to till them. I pleaded, I argued, I laid bare the whole of my nation’s anguish before them. And always, the answer was the same, delivered in a voice as smooth and as unyielding as the polished marble of their public squares: that the matter was one for the Council of Sanitation, and that their processes were their own. They spoke of order, of the preservation of their society, of the delicate balance they had achieved, as if the salvation of the rest of mankind were a secondary consideration to the neatness of their own arrangements.

It was in the vast, echoing nave of the Sé Cathedral that I at last found an audience with a man whose station appeared to grant him some authority: one Senhor Valerius, a physician of the Council. He was a man of advanced years, whose face was a mask of serene wisdom, and whose eyes, though kind, held the distant calm of one who has long contemplated abysses and chosen to build a wall around them. He listened to my impassioned appeal with a patience that was more terrible than any interruption. When I had finished, my voice hoarse with emotion, he remained silent for a long moment, the only sound the faint whisper of the wind through the high, stained-glass windows.

“Doctor Penhaligon,” he said at last, his voice low and measured, “your grief is a noble thing. It speaks of a heart that has not been hardened by the calamity. But you mistake the nature of what we have found here. It is not a remedy in the sense you imagine—a simple draught to be bottled and shipped across the sea. What we have discovered is a principle, a way of living in harmony with the forces that have unmade your world. It is a fragile state, a complex equilibrium of diet, of habit, of the very air we allow to circulate within our walls. To attempt to export it would be to shatter it utterly, like a crystal vase. It would be to bring the contagion of your despair into the heart of our salvation. We cannot share what we have, for to do so would be to lose it.”

I stood aghast, his words striking my soul with the force of a physical blow. It was not malice in his voice, but a terrible, unassailable logic—the logic of a man who had saved his own family by barring the door to his starving neighbours. “Then you condemn us!” I cried, my voice ringing in the sacred space. “You condemn millions to the grave while you hoard your secret in this gilded cage! Is this your harmony? A peace built upon the corpses of all others? I beg of you, in the name of the humanity that yet survives in the world, let me see your laboratories, let me speak to your savants! Surely there is some fragment, some knowledge, that might be spared!”

Senhor Valerius rose, and placed a hand upon my shoulder, a gesture of pity that felt colder than any rebuke. “You are a physician,” he said. “You know that a single fevered patient can poison a whole ward. We are that ward, Doctor. And the world is the fever. We have chosen health. We have chosen life. It is a terrible choice, but it is ours to have made. Go back to your England. Tend to your dying as best you can. There is no remedy for you here.”

He turned and walked away, his footsteps echoing and then fading, leaving me alone in the vast, indifferent silence of the cathedral. I stumbled out into the bright, terrible sunlight of the Lisbon afternoon. The city lay before me, beautiful and serene, its people moving with the placid grace of the saved. But I saw it now for what it was: not a sanctuary, but a tomb, as orderly and as final as any plague pit. The calm was not peace; it was the quiet of a heart that has ceased to beat. And I, who had crossed an ocean for a cure, found myself marooned in a city of the living dead, the sole bearer of the world’s sorrow in a place that had renounced it. My despair, which had before been a dull and heavy ache, now became a wild and shrieking thing within me, for I had seen the promised land, and knew that its gates were barred against all the suffering of mankind.
