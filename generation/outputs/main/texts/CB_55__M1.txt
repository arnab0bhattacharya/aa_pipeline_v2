I had preached a sermon that morning which I knew would bring me into collision with the powers of this world. The text was from the prophet Isaiah: “Woe unto them that decree unrighteous decrees, and that write grievousness which they have prescribed.” I spoke of the children in the mills, of their twelve-hour days, their little backs bent over the looms, their lungs choked with cotton-dust. I spoke of the wages withheld and the fines levied for a moment’s rest. My voice, I felt, was but a reed in the wind, yet I endeavoured to make it a trumpet of the Lord’s justice. And as I spoke, my eyes, against my will, sought one face in the congregation—the pale, serious face of Miss Eliza Thorne, seated beside her father, the very man whose decrees I denounced.

Mr. Thorne sat immovable, a block of granite in a fine broadcloth coat. His face was a mask of cold displeasure, but his daughter’s was flushed, her dark eyes fixed upon me with an intensity that both thrilled and tortured my spirit. She was the mill owner’s daughter, the rose that had grown up amidst the thorns of this manufacturing inferno of Cragg Vale. That she should possess a soul so attuned to the cries of the oppressed seemed a miracle, and a cruel one; for in loving her, I was but loving a star I could never reach.

After the service, as the congregation filed out into the damp November air, I stood by the porch, my heart a battlefield of hope and dread. Mr. Thorne passed me with a curt nod, his lips a thin, hard line. But Eliza lingered. She came to me, her gloved hands clasped before her.

“Mr. Vance,” she said, and her voice was low and earnest, “your words today were not of man’s devising. They were a flame.”

A flame! My own heart felt consumed by one. “Miss Thorne,” I stammered, my natural shyness rising like a flood, “I could not be silent. The sight of such suffering is an affront to Heaven.”

“It is an affront to humanity,” she replied, her gaze unwavering. “My father believes it is the price of progress. I believe it is the price of damnation.”

Here was a declaration that struck me to the core. To hear such sentiments from her lips was to find an oasis in a desert. I dared to take a step closer. “And what is to be done?” I asked, my voice barely a whisper. “When the law is with the oppressor, and the purse-strings are in his hand?”

“One must bear witness,” she said. “As you do.” Then, a shadow crossed her face, a look of profound sorrow. “But it is a hard duty. It creates… divisions.”

I knew what she meant. The division between her and her father; the chasm between her world and mine. She was heiress to Cragg Vale Mill, and I was a curate with a stipend that would scarcely keep a wife in bread and coal. Yet, in that moment, the worldly chasm seemed to vanish, leaving only the two souls, exposed and trembling before each other.

“It is a division I would cross, if I could,” I said, the words torn from me by a passion I could no longer contain. “Eliza, you must know my heart.”

Her eyes filled with a sudden, brilliant light, but it was extinguished as quickly. “Arthur,” she whispered, using my Christian name for the first and, I feared, the last time. “Do not. Do not speak of what cannot be.”

“Cannot?” I cried, all restraint gone. “Is it a crime to love you? Is my affection, though poor, to be counted as an injury to you?”

“It is an injury to my father,” she said, her voice firm with a painful resolution. “And I will not be the cause of a rupture so final. He is my father. The commandment is plain: ‘Honour thy father and thy mother.’ I cannot marry against his will. I cannot.”

Her refusal was not born of coldness, but of a fierce, unyielding sense of duty—a duty that warred with the love I saw shining in her eyes. It was this that maddened me. She felt as I did, yet she would bind herself to a principle that would make us both wretched.

“His will is unjust!” I declared, my hands clenched. “He would have you sacrifice your happiness on the altar of his pride and his profit!”

“And you would have me sacrifice my conscience on the altar of my own desires!” she retorted, her spirit flaring up. “Do not ask it of me, Arthur. It is a burden I cannot bear. To love you is my cross; to defy my father would be my sin.”

We stood thus, locked in a struggle of the heart, while the thin Yorkshire rain began to fall, misting the grey stone of the church and the smoking chimneys of the mill that loomed over the village like a tyrant’s castle. I saw the whole of my future in that moment: a life of lonely service, my love a constant, aching wound. And she, I saw, would be no less afflicted.

“Then we are condemned,” I said, my voice hollow with despair.

“We are tried,” she corrected me, a single tear tracing a path down her cheek. “God does not give us more than we can bear. You must go on with your work. And I… I must go on with mine.” She looked towards the mill, its dark windows like vacant eyes. “Perhaps my position allows me to help in ways you cannot. A quiet word. A small kindness. It is not the sermon, but it is something.”

It was a noble thought, a desperate attempt to find purpose in our shared misery. But it offered me no comfort. I wanted her beside me, not as a silent partner in a distant struggle, but as my wife, my companion, my other half.

With a gesture of finality, she pulled her shawl tighter about her. “Goodbye, Mr. Vance.”

She did not wait for my reply, but turned and walked away, her small, determined figure swallowed up by the grey, weeping afternoon. I remained by the porch, the rain cold on my face, feeling as one who has stood in the presence of an angel only to be told he is unworthy of paradise. The sermon was over; the real trial had just begun.
