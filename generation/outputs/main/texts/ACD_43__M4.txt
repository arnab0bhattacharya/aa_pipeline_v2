The fog had lifted somewhat by the time I reached the corner of Vauxhall Walk, but the air was still heavy with the smell of the river and the raw chill of an English October. Inspector Lathom was waiting for me beneath the lamp, his broad shoulders hunched against the damp, and his face wore that particular expression of vexation which I had come to associate with a problem that refused to fit itself into the neat categories of Scotland Yard.

“You have come, Doctor,” said he, extending a hand. “I confess I am glad of it. This affair has a queer look about it, and though I am no believer in ghosts and such nonsense, I cannot make head or tail of the matter.”

“You spoke of an empty house,” I replied, as we turned into a narrow street of mean and neglected dwellings. “And a dead man.”

“Aye, and that is the puzzle of it. The house has stood empty these six months, the furniture removed, the windows boarded, and yet a man lies dead within it, and not a sign of how he came there or who he was.”

He led me up a short flight of steps and through a door which had been forced open. Within, the place was as he had described—bare boards, stripped walls, and the melancholy aspect of a dwelling abandoned to dust and shadows. In the front room upon the ground floor, a constable stood guard beside a figure stretched upon the floor.

The dead man was of middle years, stoutly built, and dressed in a suit of good cloth, though much rumpled and stained with the grime of the streets. His face was purple and swollen, and about his throat there were the unmistakable marks of strangulation. Beside him, as though it had fallen from his hand, lay a folded newspaper.

“An American paper,” observed Lathom, as I knelt to examine the body. “The *Rochester Sentinel*, if you please. And look you here, Doctor—a date is ringed in ink upon the margin. The fourteenth of September.”

I took up the paper and examined it. It was some weeks old, and the marked date referred to a column of shipping intelligence. Of the man himself there was nothing to identify him—no letters, no cards, no mark upon his linen.

“He has been dead,” said I, after a careful examination, “some twelve hours, perhaps less. The marks upon the throat are consistent with strangulation by a cord or a thin rope, applied from behind. There is no sign of a struggle.”

“And that is what puzzles me,” said Lathom. “For how did he come here? The door was locked—I had to force it. The windows are boarded. There is no other entrance save the cellar, and that is choked with rubbish. It is as though the man had been set down here by the devil himself.”

At that moment there came a step upon the stair, and a voice which I knew well.

“Not the devil, Lathom,” said Sherlock Holmes, entering the room with his quick, noiseless tread. “Though I daresay the devil had a hand in it. Good evening, Watson. You are in time to see the solution of a very pretty little problem.”

He was wrapped in his long grey travelling-cloak, and his face was flushed with the keenness of the chase. He had been absent from London, as I knew, upon a matter connected with the Duke of Holdernesse, and I had not expected to see him for some days.

“You have heard of this, then?” I asked.

“I have heard of nothing,” said he, “but I have observed a great deal. Lathom, be so good as to tell me what you have discovered.”

The inspector recited the facts as he knew them—the empty house, the locked door, the body, the newspaper. Holmes listened with his eyes half closed, and when Lathom had finished, he stepped forward and took the paper from my hand.

“The *Rochester Sentinel*,” said he. “A provincial journal of no great distinction, but of considerable interest to the student of crime. You have marked the date, I see. The fourteenth of September. Does that suggest nothing to you?”

“It is a date,” said Lathom, “and that is all.”

“It is the anniversary,” said Holmes, “of the great robbery of the Merchants’ Bank of Rochester, which occurred twenty years ago this very month. Five men were concerned in it, and of those five, three were taken, one died in prison, and one—the most cunning of them all—escaped with the bulk of the spoil. His name was Ezra Kemble, and he was never heard of more.”

Lathom stared. “And you think this dead man is Kemble?”

“I think nothing of the kind,” said Holmes. “I observe that the dead man is not Kemble, for Kemble was a small, slight man with a cast in one eye, whereas this person is stout and heavy. But I observe also that the date is ringed in ink, and that the ring is not a circle but an oval, drawn with a peculiar turn of the pen which I have seen before. It is the mark of a man who was once a clerk in the service of the bank, and who was suspected of complicity in the robbery, though nothing was proved against him. His name was Tobias Crane, and he was afterwards employed as a private inquiry agent in the city of Rochester.”

“But what has Crane to do with this?” I asked.

“Everything,” said Holmes, “if my reading of the matter is correct. Crane was a man of method. He kept a record of every case he undertook, and he was in the habit of marking the date of a crime upon any document connected with it. This paper was found upon the body, and the date is marked in his hand. It is therefore probable that the dead man came by it from Crane, and that Crane is either the murderer or the next victim.”

He turned to the body and examined the hands with great care. “Look at the palms, Watson. They are calloused, and there is a stain of printer’s ink upon the forefinger of the right hand. This man was a compositor, and he had been employed upon a newspaper. The *Rochester Sentinel*, I fancy, is printed in a shop where such men are employed. And see here—upon his collar there is a faint mark, as of a number. It is the number of a lodging-house in Lambeth, and I have no doubt that if we inquire there we shall learn that he came to London three days ago, and that he was seen last night in the company of a man answering to the description of Tobias Crane.”

Lathom’s face had grown long. “Then you think Crane is the murderer?”

“I think,” said Holmes, “that Crane is the man who wrote the letter which this poor fellow was carrying when he died. It was taken from him, no doubt, by the murderer, who feared what it might contain. But the murderer was careless, for he left the newspaper, and the newspaper has told me all I need to know.”

He stepped to the window and looked out into the gathering dusk. “The man we want,” said he, “is Ezra Kemble. He has been hiding these twenty years in America, and he has come back to England to recover the spoil which he buried before he fled. Crane knew of the hiding-place, and he has been blackmailing Kemble. The compositor was a tool of Crane’s, employed to carry messages. Last night Kemble met him here, under the pretence of receiving a letter, and strangled him to silence. But Kemble is not a clever man, and he has left a trail which even Lathom could follow.”

“And where is Kemble now?” asked the inspector.

“He is at the Bull’s Head in Westminster, where he has taken a room under the name of Mr. Henry Blackwood. I have had a man watching the place since noon, and I have no doubt that we shall find him there before morning.”

Lathom needed no further urging. He was out of the house in an instant, and Holmes turned to me with a smile.

“A very simple matter, Watson,” said he, “though I confess that the newspaper puzzled me for a moment. It is not every day that a dead man carries his own death-warrant in his pocket.”

“And the date?” I asked.

“The date was the clue,” said he. “The fourteenth of September was the day of the robbery, and it was also the day upon which Kemble was to have met his confederates at the Bull’s Head. Crane knew it, and he marked the paper to remind himself. The compositor was to have carried it to Kemble as a warning. But Kemble was too quick for him, and the warning was never delivered.”

He took up his hat and cloak. “Come, Watson,” said he, “we have still an hour before the arrest. Let us see if we can find a cab, for I have a fancy to be present when Lathom takes his man.”

And so it proved. Before midnight, Ezra Kemble was in custody, and the stolen money—some twelve thousand pounds in gold and notes—was recovered from a vault beneath the cellar floor of the empty house in Lambeth. The compositor was identified as one William Dodd, of Rochester, and the newspaper which had betrayed his murderer was preserved by Lathom as a curiosity, though I fancy he never quite understood how it had told its tale.
