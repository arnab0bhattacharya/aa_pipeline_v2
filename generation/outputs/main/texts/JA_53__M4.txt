The morning room at Ashcombe Priory was generally allowed to be the pleasantest apartment in the house, and it was here that Miss Harriet Ellwood had established herself, with her work-basket and her suspicions, for the better part of the fortnight since her brother’s marriage. The windows faced south over the park; the fire burned with a steadiness that spoke well of the under-gardener’s diligence; and everything within was calculated to compose the mind to gratitude and good humour. Yet Harriet’s mind was composed to neither. It dwelt, with an industry all its own, upon the character of her new sister.

Mrs. Ellwood—for so the family were now obliged to call her—sat at the writing-desk by the window, her head inclined over a letter, and her countenance wearing that expression of gentle application which Harriet had already learnt to distrust. She was a small woman, not handsome in the fashionable way, but with a face that recommended itself on first acquaintance, and a manner so uniformly correct that it seemed to Harriet less the product of feeling than of rehearsal. She had been three years at Mrs. Ashby’s school in Bath, it was understood, and had come out of it with accomplishments enough to justify her in seeking a situation as governess, and with a set of connexions so obscure that Harriet had spent an entire evening in endeavouring to disentangle them, and had failed. That such a woman should have married Frederick Ellwood, of Ashcombe Priory, with fifteen hundred a year and expectations from his uncle, was a circumstance which Harriet could explain only by the most painful of all explanations—that her brother had been caught.

“You write a great many letters, ma’am,” said Harriet, after a silence of some minutes, in which she had observed the pen move steadily across three sides of paper.

“I have a great many friends to thank,” replied Mrs. Ellwood, without raising her eyes, “and it is a duty I am glad to perform while my husband is from home. He does not like to see me idle.”

“My brother is very particular in his notions,” said Harriet, with an air of meaning much. “He was always so. He cannot endure anything that looks like pretension.”

“I hope I am not likely to offend him there,” said Mrs. Ellwood, smiling. “I have no pretensions of any kind, except to be thought fond of him, which I hope is not a very criminal one.”

Harriet felt the smile to be an evasion. It was the smile of a woman who had an answer ready for everything, and who knew that her answers would be reported. She applied herself to her needle with renewed vigour, and resolved to watch more narrowly.

The opportunity was not long in coming. Lady Ashby, the widow of the late baronet, and Frederick’s godmother, had sent an invitation to the Priory for the last week in the month, and Mrs. Ellwood, upon being informed of it, had received the intelligence with a composure that Harriet considered highly suspicious. A woman who had never in her life sat down to dinner with a baronet’s lady might reasonably have betrayed some flutter; Mrs. Ellwood merely enquired whether the invitation included the whole party, and observed that she must see about having her grey silk made up.

“You will find Lady Ashby very grand,” said Harriet, with a satisfaction she did not attempt to conceal. “She keeps a great deal of state. Her servants are all in livery, and she has a way of looking at one—I cannot describe it—which makes one feel one’s insignificance.”

“I have been looked at before,” said Mrs. Ellwood, mildly.

“Oh, but not in that way. She is very particular in her acquaintance, too. She has refused to receive people—people of very good family, I assure you—merely because she did not like the cut of their coat, or the colour of their wife’s pelisse. Frederick will tell you the same.”

“Then I hope she will like my grey silk.”

Harriet was provoked. The woman was impenetrable. She had a way of answering everything with a pleasantness that was worse than contradiction, and of looking at one with a steady blue eye that seemed to say she knew precisely how little was meant. Harriet began to suspect that she was being laughed at; and to suspect it was to be certain of it, for Harriet had never in her life been able to conceive that any one could be amused at her expense without intending the grossest impertinence.

The visit to Lady Ashby took place, and Mrs. Ellwood’s conduct throughout it was of a piece with everything else. She was not fluttered; she was not forward; she paid her compliments with a readiness that Harriet thought too easy, and listened to Lady Ashby’s long account of her grandson’s measles with an attention that Harriet thought too well sustained. When Lady Ashby, at the close of the evening, was pleased to observe that Mrs. Ellwood was “a very pretty-behaved young woman, and did Frederick great credit,” Harriet felt the commendation as a personal injury.

“You seem to have got on very well with Lady Ashby,” she said, as they drove home.

“She was very kind to me.”

“She is kind to every one who is brought before her. It costs her nothing. But she does not ask people to her house twice, I assure you, unless she is very much pleased.”

“Then I shall hope to be asked twice.”

“I wonder you are not afraid of her. She has a great deal of penetration. She sees through people in a moment.”

“Then she will have seen through me, and that is a comfort,” said Mrs. Ellwood. “I have nothing to conceal.”

“Nothing!” said Harriet, with an emphasis that she intended to be killing.

Mrs. Ellwood turned her head and looked at her sister-in-law for a moment with an expression that Harriet could not read; then she said, quietly, “Nothing, Harriet. I hope you will one day believe it.”

This was the nearest approach to a direct remonstrance that had yet passed between them, and Harriet felt it as an impertinence. To be answered with such calmness, and to be called by her Christian name in that tone of gentle expostulation, was to be treated like a child. She resolved that she would not be so treated. She would have it out with her brother.

But Frederick Ellwood was not a man to be “had out” with anything. He listened to his sister’s hints and circumlocutions with a patience that was itself a rebuke, and at length said, “Harriet, I am going to speak to you plainly, because I think you will thank me for it hereafter. You have been imagining that my wife married me for my fortune. I know it. I have seen it in your looks and heard it in your silences. Now I will tell you what I know of the matter, and then I will never speak of it again. She refused me twice before she accepted me. She refused me when she believed me to be a younger son with nothing but my profession, and she accepted me when she found that my uncle’s death had made me what I am. If you can make a fortune-hunter of that, you are cleverer than I am.”

Harriet was silenced, but not convinced. She had a theory, and theories are not to be destroyed by evidence. She reflected that a woman might refuse a man twice for the sake of appearances, and that the discovery of an inheritance was exactly the sort of accident that a designing woman would know how to turn to account. She watched, therefore, with the more vigilance, and was rewarded—as she thought—by a circumstance that confirmed every suspicion.

It was the matter of the locket. Mrs. Ellwood wore, on a chain about her neck, a small gold locket, which she was observed to open and look into with great frequency, and always with an air of peculiar tenderness. Harriet, who had never yet been able to see the inside of it, set herself to discover what it contained. She imagined a miniature of some former lover; she imagined letters; she imagined, in her wilder moments, a first husband. At last, on a morning when Mrs. Ellwood had left the room to speak to the housekeeper, Harriet, with a beating heart, took up the locket from the table where it had been laid aside, and opened it.

It contained a lock of very fine light hair, tied with a thread of blue silk, and on the inner side of the lid, in a small hand, the words: “My mother’s.”

Harriet stood with the locket in her hand for a full minute. She was not a bad woman, only a foolish and a proud one, and the sight of that little inscription did what no argument of her brother’s could have done. She remembered that Mrs. Ellwood had lost her mother at fourteen; she remembered the grey silk, and the steady blue eye, and the quiet voice that had said, “I hope you will one day believe it.” She closed the locket, and laid it down, and went to the window, and stood looking out at the park for a long time without seeing it.

When Mrs. Ellwood returned, Harriet was still at the window. There was a silence, and then Harriet said, without turning round, “I have been thinking, sister, that you have never told me about your mother.”

Mrs. Ellwood was silent for a moment. Then she said, in a voice that was not quite steady, “I should like to tell you, if you would care to hear it.”

And Harriet, turning at last, found that she did care very much indeed.
