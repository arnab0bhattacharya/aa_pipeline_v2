It is a common error in the arithmetic of benevolence to suppose that the quotient of human happiness is increased merely by multiplying the physical conveniences we ourselves desire. The philanthropist, possessed of a surplus fortune and an energetic conscience, is perpetually tempted to construct a domestic Utopia according to the blueprints of his own imagination, forgetting that the foundations of the heart are not laid in Portland cement but in the obscure, tenacious loam of habit and inherited association. Such an error was now operating with considerable force upon the mind of Mr. Henry Leighton, of Thorncroft Hall in Buckinghamshire, a man whose large fortune had been accumulated in the manufacture of railway iron, but whose later years were entirely devoted to the fashioning of what he called, with a somewhat oppressive emphasis, "the moral and sanitary regeneration of the rural poor."

To this end, Mr. Leighton had commissioned Mr. Arthur Davenant, a young architect of advanced principles and a recent medallist of the Royal Institute, to design a terrace of model cottages upon a breezy, unwooded slope at the eastern extremity of the Thorncroft estate. Davenant was a youth of five-and-twenty, possessed of a broad, candid brow and a mind fervently occupied with the theories of Ruskin and the structural efficiencies of modern hygiene. He brought to his task not merely the skill of a draughtsman, but the enthusiastic humanity of a reformer. He believed thoroughly that the misery of the agricultural labourer was solely a question of defective drainage and insufficient cubic feet of air; and he had drawn his plans with a meticulous attention to back-yards, ash-pits, and the prevention of damp, which would have done honor to a Board of Health.

The difficulty lay, however, not in the drawing of the plans, but in their translation into the stubborn medium of human life. The old cottages at Thorncroft, a row of weather-beaten, tumble-down structures reared in the reign of George the Second, were undoubtedly rotten to the core. They leaned against each other for support like aged topers; their roofs were a landscape of patchwork, and their floors were a perpetual humidity. Yet to their inhabitants they possessed the supreme merit of being home—not a model home, but a place where the grandmother had died in the corner by the chimney, where the children had been born upon a straw pallet, and where the very smuts on the low ceiling seemed to belong to one’s being as much as the hair on one’s head.

When it was announced that the old cottages were to be razed and the new ones occupied, a profound and sullen dismay settled upon the village. The labourers did not articulate their objections in the language of political economy or sentimental attachment; they merely looked at the rising red-brick walls of the new terrace with a suspicion as instinctive as that of a horse for a strange dog. 

"I bean't a-goin' into they newfangled pens," said Jacob Bostock, an aged labourer who had been born in the old row, and whose rheumatic knees were wrapped in fragments of sacking. "The bricks be red, red as blood, an' the floors be main too hard for a body's feet. The old un had a bit o' dirt, aye, but 'twas warm dirt. An' as for they winders that open at the top—what's a man to do but break his neck a-pokin' at 'em? Give me the old place, wi' the smoke a-comin' down the chimbley friendly-like, to keep the cough from the chest."

This sentiment, though less audaciously expressed, was shared by his neighbors. The new cottages were too high; the windows commanded a prospect of a bare hillside instead of the cozy village midden; the gardens were laid out with a geometrical precision that rebuked the random growth of hollyhocks and cabbages. To the rural mind, a house that was aggressively clean and mathematically regular was a house devoid of comfort; it was a public institution, not a sanctuary.

Davenant, standing in the crisp October air beside the half-completed terrace, felt the full weight of this unpopularity. He was a man capable of deep sympathy, but his sympathy was at present fighting a losing battle with his theories. He had come to Thorncroft filled with a glowing vision of regenerated peasantry, dwelling in sanitary health and aesthetic order; and he found himself confronted with a stubborn preference for damp and dirt. It was a crisis of the spirit. He was beginning to perceive, with a painful clarity, that the human soul is not a sheet of white paper upon which the reformer may inscribe his improvements, but a palimpsest of inherited habits and obscure affections, which it is perilous to erase.

The situation was further complicated by the attitude of Mrs. Leighton. That lady, a woman of acute perceptions and a somewhat caustic tongue, had watched her husband’s benevolent activities with an admiration that was frequently alloyed by a critical skepticism. She was not deficient in charity, but she possessed that rare and uncomfortable faculty of seeing her husband’s motives in their component parts, and of distinguishing the alloy of vanity in the pure gold of benevolence.

"You are building a monument to your own ideas, Henry," she had said to him that morning, as they stood in the library, the plans of the new cottages spread upon the table before them. "You call it philanthropy, and I do not deny that you wish to do good; but you wish to do it in your own way, and to have the laborers express their gratitude in the terms you have dictated. You have not consulted their preferences; you have only consulted your own notions of what their preferences ought to be. Mr. Davenant is a clever young man, but he builds for an ideal labourer, not for Jacob Bostock."

Mr. Leighton had received this observation with a patience that was itself a form of majestic self-confidence. "My dear Eleanor," he had replied, "the preferences of the poor are formed by their ignorance. It is our duty to elevate, not to pander. The cottages are a model of sanitary science, and the laborers will be grateful when they have experienced the benefit."

"I doubt it," she had answered, quietly. "They will feel themselves removed from their old lives, and they will resent the cleanliness as a reproach. You cannot wash a man’s soul by putting him in a clean house."

It was this conversation that Davenant recalled as he stood alone beside his work, watching the masons laying the courses of red brick with a precision that seemed to mock the irregular lives of those who were to inhabit them. He had sought out Mrs. Leighton, drawn by that instinct which leads the perplexed and generous mind toward the counsel of a clearer intelligence; and he had found in her a sympathy that was the more valuable because it was unaccompanied by any illusion.

"You must not be discouraged, Mr. Davenant," she had said to him, in her low, even voice, as they walked together toward the old cottages. "My husband’s aim is excellent, and your work is beautiful in its intention. But you must remember that the poor are not statistical abstractions; they are men and women with memories and prejudices, which are as much a part of them as their bones. If you wish to make them happy, you must not give them a perfect house, but a house that they can make imperfect in their own way. You must leave some corner for the old habits to cling to. It is a hard lesson for a reformer, but it is the lesson of life."

Davenant had listened in silence, feeling the justice of her words, and yet reluctant to abandon the fair structure of his theories. He had gone back to his plans, and had made some modifications—not in the drainage, which he held sacred, but in the arrangement of the interiors, lowering the ceilings a little, and leaving a space for a settle by the chimney, and a corner where a pot of herbs might stand in the window. He had even, with a pang of architectural conscience, allowed the garden walls to be built of a rougher stone, that they might look less new and more as if they had grown out of the soil.

It was a compromise, and compromises are often the most enduring of human works. The cottages were finished, and the laborers, after some grumbling, took possession. They did not love them; the old affection for the tumble-down hovels still lingered, a stubborn ache in their hearts. But they found that the new chimneys did not smoke, and that the floors, though hard, were dry, and that the windows, though strange, let in the sun. And gradually, as the weeks passed, the newness wore off, and the red bricks gathered a film of dust, and the gardens began to show the random growth of cabbages and hollyhocks, the model cottages of Thorncroft ceased to be a monument of reform and became, in some small degree, a home.
