Of all the boarding houses in Camden Town—and there were many, huddled together in sooty rows like a congregation of weary old men—Mrs. Smethwick’s establishment on Pilgrim Row was the most crowded, the most clamorous, and, in its peculiar way, the most honest. It was a house that had given up all pretence of gentility long ago; its walls, stained with the ghosts of a thousand boiled dinners, seemed to have absorbed the very arguments and anxieties of its inhabitants, and its floorboards groaned not merely under the weight of feet, but under the crushing burden of unpaid bills and blighted ambitions. Within its narrow confines, a microcosm of London’s struggling souls jostled for space: Mr. Crump, the perpetually indignant clerk who was convinced the entire Board of Excise was a personal conspiracy against his advancement; the Misses Prattle, two faded sisters who communicated entirely in a shared language of sighs and allusions to a lost country seat; and young Mr. Penfold, a poet of seventeen, whose verses were as thin and pale as his face, and who subsisted almost entirely on hope and weak tea.

And at the very bottom of this human heap, both in station and in spirits, was Peg. She was not a lodger, for a lodger pays. She was a fixture, a utility, a creature of the household as much as the pestilent cat or the groaning water-pump in the yard. Orphaned, friendless, and possessing nothing in the world but the threadbare grey dress on her back and a capacity for silent endurance, she was the girl for all work. She blacked the grates until her hands were perpetually grimy, she hauled the coal-scuttles that seemed to grow heavier with every step, she scrubbed the floors with a ferocity that suggested she was trying to scour away not just the dirt, but her own dreary existence. Mrs. Smethwick, a woman whose face seemed permanently compressed by some great, unseen grievance, referred to her as ‘the girl’ as one might refer to ‘the coal-hole’ or ‘the damp patch in the cellar’—a thing to be used, and complained of, and generally taken for granted.

Then came the day of the Great Interruption, a day so unlike any other that the very sunlight, filtering grimly through the smudged panes of the parlour, seemed an audacious novelty. The arrival of Miss Perpetua Fotheringay was heralded not by a knock, but by a prolonged and furious ringing of the bell, a summons so commanding it rattled the very teacups in their saucers. And when Mrs. Smethwick, with the air of a martyr preparing for the stake, opened the door, there stood a vision. Miss Fotheringay was a spinster of considerable means and even more considerable eccentricity, a tall, angular woman dressed in a cascade of black lace and purple ribbons, with a bonnet so vast and befeathered it seemed to have a life of its own. She swept into the parlour, her gaze raking over the shabby furniture and the startled faces of the lodgers as if they were exhibits in a museum of the mundane.

“I have come,” she announced, her voice a rich contralto that filled the room, “in search of a specimen. A human specimen, mind you, of the rawest, most unformed material. I have a project.” Her eyes, sharp as needles, landed upon Peg, who was frozen in the act of carrying a tray of used cups. “Ah! What is this delightful little smudge? This creature of the dustpan and the brush? She will do. She will do famously!”

Mrs. Smethwick, whose mind worked with the slow, grinding certainty of a sausage machine, could only stammer about wages and board and the inconvenience. But Miss Fotheringay simply waved a gloved hand, dismissing such sordid details as one might dismiss a fly. A sum of money, prodigious and unheard-of, was placed upon the mantelpiece, and before Peg could fully comprehend that her life was being altered as casually as one might move a chair, she was bundled into a waiting cab, leaving the clamour and the grime of Pilgrim Row behind her in a bewildering cloud of coal-smoke.

The world of Miss Fotheringay was one of bewildering splendour and bewildering loneliness. Her great house in Belgravia was a palace of velvet and gilt, where every clock ticked with an expensive precision and every footstep echoed in cavernous halls. Peg was not to be a servant, but a ‘companion’ and a ‘project’. She was washed, and brushed, and dressed in fine clothes that felt as strange and restrictive as a cage. She was taught to sip her tea without a sound, to hold her fork as if it were a delicate bird, and to make conversation not about the price of coal, but about the virtues of the Italian masters. Miss Fotheringay, with the passion of a sculptor and the impatience of a child with a new toy, moulded and polished her, delighting in the transformation of the ‘little smudge’ into a porcelain doll. For a time, Peg felt a dizzying sense of elevation, a belief that she was ascending from the pit of her former life to some sunlit plateau.

But a project, once completed, soon loses its charm. The novelty of Peg’s flawless curtsy began to wear thin. Her quiet, well-mannered presence, which had at first been a triumph of Miss Fotheringay’s art, soon became merely… quiet. The great house, once a stage for their drama, now felt like a tomb. Miss Fotheringay’s attention drifted to other whims: a collection of rare orchids, a plan to translate a difficult Greek text, a sudden, overwhelming desire to learn the concertina. Peg, with her fine manners and her empty days, was an ornament that no longer matched the decor. The dismissal, when it came, was as swift and casual as the adoption. “My dear,” Miss Fotheringay said, not looking up from a book of botanical illustrations, “our little experiment has, I think, run its course. You are a great success, but I find I have no further need of you. You may go back to where you came from.”

And so, Peg found herself once more upon the doorstep of Mrs. Smethwick’s boarding house. But she was not the same girl who had left. She wore a dress of good grey wool, and her hands were clean and white. She stood straight, and her speech, when she managed to utter a word, was soft and measured. Mrs. Smethwick, who had spent the windfall from Miss Fotheringay on a new set of blinds and a case of questionable port, looked her up and down with profound suspicion. “So, the grand lady is done with you, is she?” she snorted. “Well, I daresay you’ve forgotten how to do an honest day’s work. You’ll have to prove yourself again.”

The world of Pilgrim Row had not changed, but Peg’s place in it had evaporated. The Misses Prattle eyed her clean frock with a mixture of envy and disdain, whispering that she was now ‘neither fish nor fowl’. Mr. Crump complained that she no longer flinched when he shouted, which robbed his tirades of their satisfaction. She was an anomaly, a creature polished by a foreign hand and then returned to the mud, her fine manners an affront to the comfortable squalor of her surroundings. She could no longer be the silent, unthinking drudge, for she had seen another way of living. Yet she was no longer the lady’s companion, for she had no prospects, no fortune, no name but ‘the girl’.

One evening, as she sat mending one of her old grey dresses—the fine wool one having been deemed too conspicuous for a girl in her station—she felt a strange resolve settle upon her. She looked at her clean, capable hands, then at the grimy faces of the lodgers arguing over a farthing’s worth of sugar. She belonged to neither world. She was a bridge between them, and a bridge, she realised with a sudden, startling clarity, is meant to be walked upon, not to stand still. The question was not which world she belonged to, but what she would build for herself in the desolate country that lay between. And in the flickering candlelight of that crowded, clamorous house, for the first time in her life, Peg began to make a plan.
