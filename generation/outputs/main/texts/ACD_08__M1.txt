The rain of a London November had set in with that dogged persistence which speaks of no early abatement, and the streets of Bloomsbury shone with the oily blackness of wet macadam. It was upon such an evening that I found myself accompanying my friend, Mr. Sherwood Locke, to the lodging house of Mrs. Agatha Finch at number fourteen, Bedford Square. Her letter, a singular document written in a cramped, agitated hand, had summoned us with an urgency that even Locke, a man of sedentary habits and ironical humour, had found compelling.

Mrs. Finch met us in the narrow hall, a woman whose face was a roadmap of anxieties. She was of that age where the bloom of youth has long faded, but the vigour of a lifetime of worry has not. Her cap was askew, and her hands, chapped and red, twisted a damp apron in a constant, fretful motion.

“Oh, Mr. Locke, sir, and Dr. Vance,” she began, her voice a reedy whisper. “Thank the heavens you’ve come. The police… they would only laugh at me. They call it a simple case of a man who has done a flit. But I know better. I have a feeling in my bones.”

“Feelings, Mrs. Finch, are the province of poets and gouty invalids,” said Locke, shaking the rain from his hat. “I deal in facts. Pray, state them plainly. You have a lodger who has vanished.”

We were shown into a small, stuffy parlour that smelled of stale tea and beeswax. A portrait of a stern, whiskered gentleman glowered from the wall.

“It is Mr. Elias Thorne,” she said, sinking into a chair. “He came here four months ago. Paid three months in advance, in sovereigns, sir, clean and new from the bank. A quiet gentleman. Read a great deal. Never a letter, never a visitor. But the candle, Mr. Locke! The candle in his window.”

“Ah, the candle,” Locke murmured, his grey eyes taking in the room with a swift, comprehensive glance. “Pray, describe it.”

“Every night,” she continued, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial tone, “from dusk until two in the morning, he would light a single candle in the window of his room. Not for reading, mind you, for he had his own lamp. This was a signal, sir. I’m sure of it. A signal to someone in the square opposite.”

“And last night?” I inquired, feeling it my duty to play the straight man to my friend’s deductions.

“Last night, there was no candle. And this morning, there was no Mr. Thorne. His door was locked. I had to use my master key. The room is empty, save for his trunk. He’s gone, and he’s left his entire wardrobe behind him.”

Locke steepled his long, delicate fingers beneath his chin. “A man who pays in gold, receives no correspondence, and signals from his window with a candle. Then he vanishes, leaving behind the very thing a fugitive would be most anxious to possess. Most instructive. Take us to his room, Mrs. Finch. And do not touch anything.”

The room was at the top of the house, small and neat. The bed was made with military precision. On a small table stood a candlestick, the wax still pooled around a blackened wick. As Mrs. Finch had said, a large steamer trunk, of dark leather and brass bindings, stood squarely in the centre of the floor. It was locked.

“His bed is made,” I observed.

“A man who intends to return or to be found does not leave his bed in a state of disarray,” Locke replied, his eyes scanning the floorboards. “He is a methodical man. Observe, Watson, the faint scuff marks near the window. He stood there often. And the rug is turned up at that corner. He was anxious, perhaps, pacing. Now, the trunk.”

He bent to examine the lock. “A standard Bramah. But look here.” He pointed a slender forefinger at the keyhole. “A fine, silvery dust. Graphite, unless I am mistaken. Someone has been lubricating the lock. Not Mr. Thorne, I think. He would have used oil. This is the work of a professional, or a very careful amateur.”

He produced a small leather case from his pocket, from which he extracted a set of slender steel probes. With surprising dexterity, he worked at the lock for a moment. There was a soft click.

“Mrs. Finch,” said Locke, without looking up. “You were right to send for me before the police. The constabulary would have burst this lock and trampled over the evidence. The story is in the details, and they have a lamentable habit of destroying them.”

He lifted the heavy lid. The scent of cedar and mothballs filled the small room. Inside, neatly folded, were a dozen shirts, several suits of tweed and wool, a collection of ties, and a pair of highly polished boots. It was the wardrobe of a gentleman of modest but comfortable means.

“Nothing is missing,” I remarked. “Even his hairbrush and shaving kit are here.”

“Precisely,” said Locke, his voice sharp with excitement. “Which is the most telling fact of all. A man who leaves in such a hurry that he forgets his entire collection of clothing would surely have taken his toilet articles. No, no. This trunk was not abandoned. It was left. It is a message, or a decoy. He has gone to ground, and he has done so with a premeditation that is almost artistic.”

He began to lift the clothes out, item by item, his touch as delicate as a surgeon’s. Beneath the shirts, he found a cardboard box containing collar-studs and cufflinks. Beneath the suits, a pair of carpet slippers. It was all so painfully ordinary. Then, as he lifted the final pair of trousers, his hand stopped.

“Ah,” he breathed. “The first discordant note.”

He drew out a small, leather-bound diary. It was slim, with no name upon the cover. He opened it. The pages were filled with neat, spidery script, but they were not entries of a personal nature. They were columns of figures, dates, and what appeared to be shipping manifests.

“Port of London,” I read over his shoulder. “S.S. *Orion*. Cargo: uncut diamonds. Consignee: Mr. J. Abernathy.” There were dozens of such entries. “Good heavens, Locke. This is a smuggler’s ledger.”

“Or an investigator’s,” Locke corrected softly. He turned the diary over in his hands. “Consider, Watson. Mr. Thorne, our quiet lodger, arrives and pays in cash. He signals to a confederate with a candle. He then vanishes, leaving a trunk full of clothes as a red herring to suggest a panicked flight. But he leaves behind this diary. Why? Because it is not his.”

He looked at me, his eyes alight. “He is not a criminal. He is a detective. A private agent, perhaps, hired to watch Mr. J. Abernathy. The candle was to signal ‘all is well’ to his employers. Last night, the signal was not given. That was the warning. His quarry knew he was being watched. Thorne did not flee; he was taken. Or he has gone to earth to escape the very men he was observing. The trunk was left to create confusion, to buy him time.”

Mrs. Finch was wringing her hands. “Taken? Oh, the poor man!”

“Not so poor, perhaps,” said Locke, closing the diary. “He was playing a dangerous game. The question is, who is the second player? This Mr. Abernathy is our man. The diary gives us the name. The game is afoot, Mrs. Finch, and Mr. Thorne has just made his first move. It is now up to us to make the next.” He slipped the diary into his pocket, his face set in that mask of cool concentration I knew so well. The rain continued to beat against the windowpane, a frantic drumming that seemed to echo the sudden, racing pulse of the affair.
