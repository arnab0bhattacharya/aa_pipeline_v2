Mr. Arthur Pendrell, walking homeward through the damp, gas-lit obscurity of Euston Square, felt the pressure of a choice that was rapidly hardening into a destiny. He was a young man of seven-and-twenty, possessed of a countenance that bespoke more of uneasy aspiration than of comfortable equilibrium; his eyes were of that searching grey which seems perpetually to look for the moral significance of facts, and his brow, already slightly furrowed with the effort to reconcile ecclesiastical theory with social reality, gave him an air of premature gravity. He had left the presence of Sir Hugo Mallinger, a baronet of ample fortune and boundless indifference to everything but his own digestion and the social tone of his dinner-parties, with an offer in his pocket that most young clergymen would have accounted an unmingled blessing. The living of St. George’s, Hanover Square, was vacant, and Sir Hugo, who held the advowson, had intimated that it was at Mr. Pendrell’s disposal.

Sir Hugo was a man who transacted all his affairs, spiritual and temporal, with the same urbane, impersonal ease. He had looked at Arthur over the capacious bulwark of his waistcoat, twirling his eyeglass, and had said, in that deliberate, slightly husky bass which seemed to issue from a cavern of profound self-satisfaction, “You’re a clever fellow, Pendrell—uncommon clever; read a lot of German metaphysics, I suppose, and all that sort of thing. But you’ll find it won’t do in the pulpit. We want a man who can give us a good, sensible, wholesome discourse; nothing about the atonement in a way to make people uncomfortable, you know; nothing about the poor. The poor are always with us, and we subscribe handsomely to the hospitals. What we want is a tone—tone, sir; a little quiet scholarship, a graceful appeal to the higher sentiments. I shouldn’t like to think my wife was going to be made melancholy on a Sunday morning. She’s delicate, and she requires cheering up.”

Arthur, in the presence of this large, bland, perfectly-attired worldliness, had felt himself singularly small and ineffectual. He had murmured something about the mission of the Church, and Sir Hugo had waved his white, plump hand with a gesture that seemed to brush aside all such cobwebs of enthusiasm. “The Church is a very excellent institution,” said the baronet; “it keeps up the connection between the classes. I don’t want to hear politics from the pulpit, and I don’t want to hear economics. I want to hear a gentleman speak like a gentleman, and to feel that I’ve been to church.” And with this, and a vague, hospitable allusion to dining at the house when Arthur should be settled, the interview had ended.

And now, as he walked through the fog, Arthur felt the temptation closing round him like a damp, warm cloak. The income was twelve hundred a year; the society was that of the most refined and intellectual circles in London; there would be leisure for study, for the pursuit of those historical and theological investigations which were the true food of his mind. He saw himself in a pleasant study, surrounded by books, occasionally issuing forth to preach an elegant sermon to a congregation of peers and dowagers, and to dine afterward at tables where he would hear the best talk of the day. It was a prospect that might have allured a stronger man; and Arthur, with his slender frame, his fastidious tastes, his shrinking from the rougher aspects of life, felt the allure with an intensity that alarmed him. He knew that he was not by nature a fighter; he was a student, a dreamer, a man who loved harmony and hated friction, and to be told that he was to be the spiritual ornament of a worldly society was to touch the very spring of his weakness.

But against this soft, alluring vision there rose another, sharp and rugged as a northern crag. In his pocket, folded and refolded, was a letter, written in a cramped, laborious hand, with ink that showed pale and watery on the rough paper. It was from Mr. Bartholomew Jowett, the churchwarden of St. Aidan’s, Whitfield, in the north country, a place that clung to the side of a bare hill, with a few rows of soot-grimed cottages, a ruined mill, and a population of weavers and colliers who were at once poverty-stricken, independent, and suspicious. The living of St. Aidan’s was in the gift of a society, not of an individual; it was worth a hundred and fifty pounds a year, and the parsonage was damp. Mr. Jowett wrote with a directness that had in it no trace of London polish:

“Reverend Sir,—Having seen your name in the list of them as has passed, we at Whitfield are desirous of knowing if you’d be willing to come among us. We want a man as is not above his work, and as will stand by us in trouble. The old mill’s stopped, and there’s many families here as is sore bested. We don’t want no fine preaching; we want a man as can speak plain to us, and as will be a friend to the sick and the lads as is growing up wild. The pay is small, and the house is cold, but the people is willing to do their part if the parson will do his. We are, Reverend Sir, your obedient servants, Bartholomew Jowett, Churchwarden, and others.”

Arthur had read this letter many times, and each time it had stirred in him a deeper emotion. It was not merely the appeal of suffering—though that appealed strongly to his tender, susceptible heart—but it was the recognition of a need which he felt himself peculiarly fitted to supply. He had dreams of reform, of education, of social regeneration; he had read much of the new science, of the conditions of the laboring classes, and he had formed in his own mind a scheme of parish work that should be at once Christian and scientific. He wanted to try the experiment; he wanted to test his theories; he wanted, in short, to live the life he preached. And here was the opportunity.

But it was a hard opportunity. It meant exile from all he loved: from books, from music, from the society of refined minds, from the pleasant, easy, cultivated world to which he belonged by birth and training. It meant cold, dirt, discomfort, the daily contact with roughness and ignorance, the wearisome round of petty duties, the consciousness of powers wasted on an unresponsive medium. He knew himself well enough to feel that the very qualities which made him long to serve the poor would make their service peculiarly painful to him; he was fastidious, shy, easily wounded, and the thought of living among people who would probably misunderstand him, perhaps despise him, was a keen humiliation. He saw himself, in fancy, standing in a bleak, bare church, preaching to a handful of listless hearers, who would stare at his pale face and thin, refined hands with a dull wonder, and would care nothing for the message he brought them. And then, in the reaction of his mood, he saw himself at St. George’s, in a surplice of fine linen, ascending the steps of a carved pulpit, and looking down on a sea of aristocratic faces, and he felt that he could preach a sermon that would be a masterpiece of polished rhetoric, and that it would leave every soul untouched.

The fog thickened; the lamps gleamed like blurred moons; the footsteps of the passers-by sounded muffled and remote. Arthur paused at a crossing, and stood still, trying to see his way. The two lives lay before him: the life of ease, of culture, of refined self-culture, with its certain rewards and its certain nullity; and the life of hardship, of self-sacrifice, of obscure and perhaps futile effort. He thought of the young men of his acquaintance, who were accepting livings in fashionable parishes, and who spoke of their work as a “profession,” and of their congregations as their “patrons.” He thought of the bishops, who were politicians and men of the world, and who would smile at his scruples as the amiable eccentricities of an inexperienced youth. He thought of his mother, who had pinched herself to give him an education, and who would be proud to see him in a London pulpit, and who would be grieved and bewildered if he chose instead a dreary northern village. The claims of love, of duty, of ambition, of sympathy, all pulled him different ways, and he felt himself, in the phrase of the philosopher he had been reading, the helpless sport of motives whose very complexity was a proof of their strength.

And yet, beneath all this turmoil of argument and desire, there was a deeper voice, not to be silenced—a voice that spoke not from the intellect, but from the very core of his moral being, and that said, simply and inexorably, that he was meant for the north. He knew that if he accepted St. George’s, he would be bought; that his fine sermons would be a price paid for his acquiescence in a system he despised; that his culture would be a sedative, not a stimulus; and that, in the end, he would sink into a comfortable, urbane, well-fed pluralist, who would wonder, in his old age, at the fervent ideals of his youth, and would account them the illusions of inexperience. And he knew, too, that if he went to Whitfield, he would suffer; that he would often regret his choice; that he would be lonely, discouraged, and perhaps defeated. But he would be a man, and not a mere ornament; he would be fighting on the side of the weak, and not of the strong; and in the great, dim, unknown future, where all our actions bear their fruit, he would have the consciousness of having tried to do the work that was given him to do, in the place where it most needed doing.

He crossed the street, and walked on, with a quicker step. The fog seemed less oppressive; he breathed more freely. He had not decided, he told himself; he would think it over, weigh the arguments, consult his friends. But in his heart, beneath all the plausible delays that habit and inclination would suggest, he knew that the decision was already made. The slow, difficult, painful growth of a moral purpose, which is the true history of every noble life, had been going on in him for months, perhaps for years, and the moment of crisis had only revealed to him the work that had been accomplished in silence. He was going to Whitfield.
