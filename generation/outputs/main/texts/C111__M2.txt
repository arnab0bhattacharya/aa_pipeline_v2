The water in the courtyard of Solara was not merely cold; it was a mirror of polished obsidian, reflecting the high, arched windows of the palace that loomed over it like the ribs of a giant stone beast. In the center of this basin, a single goldfish, named Ory, drifted with the languid grace of a creature that had long ago stopped caring about the world above. He was not a pretty fish. His scales were the color of tarnished brass, and his tail fin was torn at the edge, a souvenir from a battle with a heron three seasons past. To the courtiers who strolled the gravel paths, he was an inconvenience, a smudge in the reflection of their own vanity.

It was on a Tuesday, during the midday heat when the air shimmered and warped, that the change occurred. A fairy, no larger than a thimble and dressed in a gown of woven spider-silk, dropped from the eaves. She did not speak in words, for she had none, but she pressed her thumb against Ory’s gill plate. A spark, blue and cold, shot through his veins. Ory gasped, not with water, but with a sudden, terrifying clarity. He felt the weight of the air on his skin, the sting of the sun on his scales, and the overwhelming, crushing pressure of knowing exactly what was being said as he opened his mouth.

The first to notice was Lady Vela, the King’s chief advisor, who was leaning over the railing, adjusting her pearl choker. She frowned, expecting the usual bubbles. Instead, a voice, thin and reedy like a reed pipe, rasped out of the water.

"You are lying to the King about the grain harvest, Vela. You have embezzled the surplus to pay for your son’s gambling debts in the lower city."

Lady Vela froze. Her hand hovered over the railing. The courtyard, usually a hum of polite gossip and the clatter of servants’ trays, fell into a dead silence. The birds in the cypress trees stopped singing. Ory’s eyes, wide and black, stared up at her. He did not blink.

"Impossible," Vela whispered, her voice trembling. "A fish cannot speak."

"I can do many things now," Ory said, his voice bubbling slightly as he turned in a slow circle. "It is a terrible gift. It tastes like ash."

King Aelric, who had been resting in the shade of a pergola nearby, rose from his chaise lounge. He was a man of broad shoulders and a weary face, accustomed to the heavy burden of a kingdom that stretched from the salt flats to the pine forests. He walked toward the fountain, his sandals crunching on the gravel. The courtiers parted for him, a sea of silk and steel, their eyes darting between the King and the small, golden creature in the basin.

"Is that you, Ory?" Aelric asked, his voice calm.

"It is I, Your Majesty," Ory replied. "And I am sorry for the disruption. I did not ask for this. But I find I cannot stop."

Aelric looked at his advisors. Lord Kaelen, the Master of Coin, was pale. He shifted his weight, trying to hide behind a servant. "Your Majesty, this is a trick. A mimic. Or perhaps a curse."

Ory turned his head toward Kaelen. "You are not a trick, Lord Kaelen. You are a coward. You stole the funds for the northern fortification and buried them in the cellar of the tavern where you keep your mistress. You tell the King the walls are weak because you fear the King will send soldiers to inspect them, and they will find the empty coffers."

Kaelen gasped, his face draining of color. "I... I never..."

"You did," Ory said simply. "And you are afraid of the King because you believe he knows."

Aelric stared at his Master of Coin. For a decade, Kaelen had been his right hand, a steady, if dull, presence. The betrayal cut deep, a sharp pain in the center of the King’s chest. He looked back at the fish. "Why? Why are you doing this?"

"I do not know why I started," Ory admitted, his voice dropping. "But I feel the truth in the water. It is heavy. It is like swallowing stones. I cannot hold it back. It hurts to keep it inside."

The court was in an uproar. Whispers erupted like sparks. Lady Vela was weeping silently, her pearls slipping from her neck. Kaelen was trying to back away, but the servants, sensing the shift in power, had formed a loose circle around the fountain. The King raised a hand, and the noise died.

"Listen," Aelric commanded.

Ory looked up. "There is more," he said. "Prince Lorian, your son, does not care for the throne. He only cares for his poetry and his vineyards. He is not plotting against you, as the spies say. He is plotting to escape. He is in love with a baker’s daughter in the market square, and he is terrified you will force him to marry the Duke’s daughter."

Aelric closed his eyes. He had suspected it. The spies had reported shadows and secret meetings, but they had never reported the name of the baker’s daughter. The fish had spoken the private truth, the thing that lived in the heart and not the ledger.

"Enough," Aelric said. His voice was harder than it had been in years. He looked at Ory. "You are a creature of the water. You belong to the court, but you are not of it. You have broken the order of things."

Ory’s gills flared. "I am only telling the truth, Sire. Is that not what you require of your court?"

"I require loyalty," Aelric said. "I require discretion. I require silence."

The King reached into his pocket and pulled out a small, silver coin. He held it over the water. "If you speak again, I will throw this coin into the basin. The clang will shatter the magic. You will be silent forever, or dead. Choose."

The courtiers held their breath. Lady Vela looked at the King with a mixture of hatred and relief. Kaelen was on his knees, begging for mercy, though it was too late for him. The truth was out, and it could not be put back in the bottle.

Ory looked at the coin. He looked at the King. He saw the exhaustion in Aelric’s eyes, the burden of a man who had to know everything but could trust no one. The fish realized that his gift was not a blessing, but a wound. It had exposed the rot in the bones of the kingdom, but it had also destroyed the illusion that held them together.

"I will be silent," Ory said, his voice barely a ripple.

He sank to the bottom of the fountain, his brass-colored scales settling into the dark sand. The water above him was still, reflecting the blue sky and the faces of the courtiers.

Aelric stood for a long moment, the coin still in his hand. He looked at his son, who was watching from the shadows of the pergola, and at his advisors, who were no longer looking at him with reverence, but with fear. The court was not confused; it was shattered. The truth was a weapon that cut both ways, and now the King held it.

He dropped the coin into the water. It sank, making a small, dull *plip* that sounded final.

"Go," Aelric said to the court. "There is much to do."

The courtiers turned and fled, the gravel crunching under their feet. Aelric remained alone by the fountain. He looked down into the water. Ory was gone, hidden in the depths. The water was clear again, but it no longer looked like a mirror. It looked like a lie. The King turned and walked back into the palace, the weight of the truth settling on his shoulders, heavier than any crown.
