The locomotive announced itself from afar, not as a sound but as a vibration in the soles of the feet, a tremor that ran up through the iron rails and into the timbers of the platform where I stood, and I remember thinking—with that curious detachment which comes to a man at the end of a long labour—that the earth itself had begun to breathe. For seven years I had driven that breath into the mountains. I had surveyed the grades, sunk the shafts, cursed the engineers and been cursed by them in turn, buried three men in a single season and written to their families in a hand I could scarcely hold steady. And now the thing was done. The rails ran from the sea into the cordillera like a vein opened in the body of the continent, and I stood on the platform of Puerto Aldana with my hat in my hand and the salt wind on my face, waiting for the first train to come down out of the hills.

The morning was very clear. The light lay hard and white upon the water, and the harbour—such as it was, a shallow basin of greenish swell behind a mole of rubble—showed every pebble of its bottom, so that the fishing boats seemed to hang suspended in glass. Behind me the town climbed in a disorder of white walls and red tiles toward the first spurs of the range, and beyond them the mountains themselves stood up in a long, serrated wall of shadow, blue at the base and brown at the summit, where the sun had already begun to burn away the last of the night. There was no cloud. There was, indeed, nothing in all that vast and glittering prospect to suggest the least uncertainty in the order of the world; and yet I was conscious of a certain tightness in the chest, a premonitory constriction, as though the morning itself were holding its breath.

I had been assured—by the President himself, in the long, dim room in the capital where the portraits of liberators hung in their tarnished frames—that the first train would carry a delegation of engineers, a company of the railway's shareholders, and a band. The band, I confess, had been my own suggestion, for I had a notion—a young man's notion, perhaps—that the arrival of the first train should be a thing heard as well as seen, that the villages along the line should lift their heads at the sound of brass and know that the world had come to them at last. I had imagined it a hundred times in the sleepless nights of the survey camps: the locomotive rounding the last curve, the smoke streaming back over the cane, the brown faces of the peons turning up from the fields, and the great, foolish, glorious noise of it all.

What came instead was silence. The vibration deepened; the rails began to sing; and then, far up the cutting where the line emerged from the first fold of the hills, a plume of smoke stood up against the brown flank of the mountain—not the soft grey of wood smoke, but a hard, black, mineral smear, such as a warship makes. And after the smoke came the engine, and behind the engine the cars, and I saw at once that they were not the passenger cars I had ordered from the works at Barracas. They were boxcars, high-sided and iron-shuttered, and from the open doors of them there protruded, row upon row, the barrels of rifles.

I do not know how long I stood there. It was not long—a minute, perhaps, while the train slowed and the brakes took hold and the whole immense weight of it came to rest at the platform's edge with a shudder and a long expiring sigh of steam. A soldier dropped from the leading car, then another, then a dozen, and they formed up on the platform with the neatness of men who have been drilled in the dark. Their uniforms were the colour of dust. Their faces were the faces of the villages—the same high cheekbones, the same patient, heavy-lidded eyes—and they looked at me, or rather through me, with that perfect and terrible incuriosity which is the gift of the man who has been told nothing and expects nothing.

An officer descended last. He was a small man, very upright, with a thin black moustache and spectacles that caught the light so that one could not see his eyes. He approached me and saluted with a courtesy that was somehow more chilling than any discourtesy, and he said, in a voice of great refinement: "Señor Ingeniero. We are come, as you see. The President sends his compliments. The line is very fine. Very fine indeed." He turned and looked back along the track, at the mountains, at the smoke still drifting from the stack. "It will be most useful," he said.

I found my voice—or something that passed for it—and asked after the shareholders, the delegation, the band. The officer regarded me with a mild surprise, as a man might regard a child who has asked after the fairies at the bottom of the garden. "There has been a change of plan," he said. "The President is of the opinion that the interior is not yet—how shall I say—prepared for the shareholders. There are elements, you understand. Agitators. The railway, señor, is a great instrument. It carries many things." He smiled, and the light slid from his spectacles, and for an instant I saw his eyes, and they were not cruel, nor even hard, but merely tired—tired with the particular weariness of a man who has long since ceased to be surprised by anything that men will do. "You have given us the road," he said. "We will take care of the traffic."

I looked past him at the soldiers, at the rifles, at the boxcars that had been built to carry ore and timber and the produce of the fields, and I understood—with a clarity that was like a physical blow, a pain beneath the breastbone—what it was I had built. The villages would lift their heads at the sound of the train, yes. They would lift their heads and see the smoke, and they would know that the world had come to them at last; and the world, it appeared, had come in the shape of a small man with spectacles and a company of dusty soldiers and a great many rifles. The prosperity I had imagined—the schools, the markets, the slow sweet commerce of peaceful things—that had been my own invention, my own innocent and foolish dream, and it had no more to do with the iron road than the song of a bird has to do with the axe.

The officer was speaking again, something about billets and water for the men, and I heard myself answering with the correct and mechanical civility of a man in a dream. The sun stood higher. The heat came down upon the platform, and the smell of hot iron and salt water and the sweat of soldiers rose about us, and far off in the town a church bell began to ring the hour, and the sound of it fell across the harbour and the hills with a terrible and indifferent sweetness. I put on my hat. I remember that my hands were quite steady, and that this steadiness seemed to me the most shameful thing about me. For seven years I had driven that road into the mountains, and now I stood at the end of it, at the beginning of it, and watched the soldiers unload.
