The cottage at Lark’s Meadow was small, but it was their own; and if the annuity which accompanied it was not large, it was at least fixed. To Miss Arabella Thorne, the elder sister, this twofold blessing appeared in the light of a haven after a long and unprosperous voyage; to Miss Cordelia Thorne, it seemed rather a sum of money lying idle, and a house inconveniently situated for the enjoyment of it. They had received both from their godmother, Mrs. Everard, whose partiality had been the more gratifying because it had been so little expected; and they had received them, as sisters will receive unequal portions of the same good fortune, with very different degrees of thankfulness.

Arabella, who was nine-and-twenty, and had known what it was to be poor without the consolation of being interesting, asked nothing more of the world than permission to keep a few rose-trees, a cat, and her own opinions in peace. Cordelia, at three-and-twenty, with a complexion which she considered her capital, and a temper which she considered her charm, thought the world had been very slow in discovering her, and that the annuity had come at last only to make discovery possible. London, she said, was the only place in which a woman of any spirit could be said to live at all; and to London, therefore, the annuity ought properly to be devoted.

“Two hundred a year,” said Cordelia, “is not a fortune; but it is exactly the sort of sum which, laid out in the right manner, may be made to look like one. One season, Arabella—only one—and who can tell what may come of it?”

“Nothing can come of it,” replied Arabella, “but a great many bills, and a great deal of disappointment, and the necessity of living afterwards in a cottage smaller than this, with the additional mortification of remembering that we once had a carriage.”

“You always see the end of everything,” said Cordelia, with an air of injury. “It is a great pity that you were not born a man, for you would have made an excellent actuary.”

This was not the first conversation of the kind, nor was it likely to be the last; for Cordelia had the happy faculty of forgetting a refusal as soon as it was pronounced, and Arabella had the unfortunate one of remembering her own reasons. The affair might have languished in this manner for months, had not their brother, Mr. Henry Thorne, thought fit to interfere.

Mr. Henry Thorne was a solicitor in a country town, and esteemed himself, on that account, a man of the world. He had expected the whole of Mrs. Everard’s property, having been given to understand, at various periods of his boyhood, that he was the favourite; and he had borne the disappointment with that species of resignation which consists in talking a great deal about the uncertainty of human expectations. He now wrote to propose a visit, expressing himself anxious to be of use in arranging his sisters’ affairs; and he arrived on a Tuesday, with a portmanteau, a quantity of good advice, and a strong sense of his own disinterestedness.

“The truth is, my dear Arabella,” said he, when they were seated at dinner, “that you are both of you wrong, and I am the only person in the family who sees the matter as it ought to be seen. Cordelia wishes to spend the money; you wish to hoard it. Neither of you has considered that money is a means, not an end.”

“I have never considered it an end,” said Arabella, quietly. “I consider it a roof, and a fire, and the power of paying one’s debts.”

“Very good—very good, in its way. But a roof may be too low, and a fire too small, and debts—well, debts are the natural consequence of spirit. Now, I propose a compromise. Let Cordelia go to London for the season, under the care of my wife’s cousin, Mrs. Ashby, who is a very respectable woman, and lives in a very respectable street; and let her go with a fixed allowance, which she shall not exceed. And do you, Arabella, remain here, and keep the house; and if the season answers—why, so much the better; and if it does not, why, she will have had her experience, and we shall all be the wiser.”

Cordelia clapped her hands. “Dear Henry! You are the only one of us who has any sense.”

“I hope I am not without sense,” said Arabella, with a smile which was not entirely one of pleasure. “But I confess I do not see the compromise. It is all on one side. If Cordelia goes to London, the annuity goes with her; and I am to remain here, and be thankful.”

“You would not wish to go to London yourself?” said Henry, with an air of surprise which was meant to be flattering.

“I have no wish to go to London. I have a wish to be secure. There is a difference.”

“My dear sister, security is the virtue of a strong-box, not of a rational being. You have a little fortune, and you must make the most of it. Cordelia has beauty—or, at least, she has not a bad face—and she has a right to try her fortune. It is what our poor godmother would have wished.”

“Our poor godmother,” returned Arabella, “was a woman of excellent understanding, and I believe she wished us both to be happy in our own way. But she did not, I think, intend that one of us should be happy at the other’s expense.”

“Expense!” cried Cordelia. “You talk as if I were going to ruin you. It is only for a few months. And I shall write to you every week, and tell you everything.”

“I have no doubt of it,” said Arabella. “You will tell me of the balls you have been to, and the partners you have danced with, and the ladies who were not so well dressed as yourself; and I shall read your letters in this little parlour, with the rain against the window, and think how much better it would have been if you had stayed at home and been contented.”

There was a pause. Henry looked at his plate, and Cordelia looked at the window, and Arabella looked at neither of them. It was the sort of pause which in a well-regulated family precedes a general reconciliation; but in this family it preceded nothing but a general discomfort.

“You are very hard,” said Cordelia at last, in a voice which was intended to be pathetic. “You have no feeling for a girl who has never had any pleasure.”

“I have a great deal of feeling,” said Arabella, “for a woman who has never had any security. But it seems we are not to agree; and I am sure I have no wish to quarrel. Let Henry do as he pleases. He is the man of business.”

Henry, thus appealed to, became instantly important. He drew his chair closer, and began to talk of settlements, and allowances, and the propriety of keeping accounts; and Cordelia listened with a face of radiant attention, and Arabella with a face of patient resignation. It was settled, at length, that Cordelia should go to London in the spring; that she should have a hundred pounds for her expenses, and no more; that she should write once a fortnight; and that if, at the end of the season, she had not received an offer of marriage, she should return to Lark’s Meadow without a murmur.

“And if she has?” said Arabella.

“Then,” said Henry, with a smile of great complacency, “we shall have done our duty by her, and she will have done hers by herself.”

“I hope so,” said Arabella; and she said it in a tone which her brother thought unaccountably cold.

The evening passed away in plans and projects, in which Arabella had no share, and in which she was too wise to wish for one. She sat by the fire with her knitting, and listened to her sister’s raptures, and thought of the many women who had gone to London with a hundred pounds and a good complexion, and had come back with neither. She thought, too, of the annuity, which was now to be diminished by half for the gratification of a single season; and she wondered whether Cordelia would ever understand that the pleasure of a ball is a very transient thing, and the comfort of a fixed income a very lasting one. But she said nothing; for she had long since learned that there are some truths which are never so unwelcome as when they are spoken by an elder sister.

When Henry had gone, and the house was quiet again, Cordelia came and sat down on a stool at Arabella’s feet, and looked up in her face with an air of penitence which was not entirely assumed.

“You are not angry with me, Arabella?”

“I am not angry with you. I am only sorry.”

“Sorry! Why should you be sorry? I am going to be happy.”

“I hope you will be. But I am sorry that you think happiness is to be found in a place where you will be obliged to be agreeable to people you do not like, and to spend money you cannot afford, and to pretend to be what you are not.”

“You are always so grave,” said Cordelia, pouting. “You never allow for a little folly.”

“I allow for a great deal of folly,” said Arabella, “but I do not allow for it at the expense of a hundred pounds.”

Cordelia laughed, and kissed her, and ran away to look over her dresses; and Arabella sat alone by the fire, and thought how strange it was that the same inheritance should have made one sister so happy and the other so anxious. But she was not a woman to repine long; and when the cat came and rubbed against her hand, she smiled, and said, “Well, puss, we shall have the house to ourselves, at any rate.”
