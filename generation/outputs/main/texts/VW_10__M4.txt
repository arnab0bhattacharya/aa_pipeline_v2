The sea was a great, restless animal at the bottom of the garden, and she—small, in a pinafore stiff with salt—stood at the very edge of the cliff path where the thrift grew, watching it breathe. Up, and the whole world was water, a green glassy wall that would surely never break; down, and there was the shock of white, the long withdrawing sigh, the rattle of pebbles dragged under. Her brother had told her once that if you stared long enough the sea would come up and take you, and she had believed him for a season, until the day she saw their father, a dark upright figure, walk down the sands and stand with his boots in the foam, and the sea did nothing at all but fret about his ankles like a dog. That was the summer at Trewellan, the summer of the red bucket and the smell of mackerel and the lamp lit early in the low white house, and it seemed to her afterwards, when she was old, that it had lasted a hundred years and also no time at all, like a note held on a piano while someone in the next room moves the furniture.

Then there was the garden, and the garden was Mr. Ellery. She had not expected him; he came at the end of a long afternoon in June, when the lupins were going over and the air was thick with the smell of box and warm stone, and her mother had said, *Go and walk with him, Beatrice, he has something to say to you*, and she had known at once, from the particular carefulness of her mother's voice, what it was. He was a tall man with a thin, kind face and hands that did not know what to do with themselves. He spoke of the delphiniums. He spoke of the rain they had needed and now had had. And then, by the sundial, where the shadow lay across the face and made the hours illegible, he said it: that he had loved her since the first evening he had seen her at the Aldercombes', and that he could not go on without knowing. She looked at the sundial. She thought, *I shall remember this always*, and even as she thought it she felt the moment begin to slip, the way a dream slips when you name it, and she heard herself saying yes—saying it because the lupins were going over and he was kind and the evening was so still—and she was not sure, then or ever, whether the yes had come from her or from the garden, from the heavy scent of the box and the shadow on the stone. He took her hand. His was trembling. And the rooks went up from the elms all together, a black flurry of them against the pale sky, and that, she decided, was the sound of a life beginning.

The years after were not a story either. They were a house in Ledbury Terrace with a dark hall and a clock that struck the quarters; they were children, two of them, and then a third who did not stay; they were the war, and the letters, and the long grey afternoons when she sat with her sewing and heard the guns across the Channel like distant furniture being moved. Her husband died in the second year of the peace, quietly, in his chair, with the paper on his knee, and she had looked at him and thought, with a kind of shame, *I never did know what you were thinking*, and had been surprised to find that this was not a grief but a fact, heavy and smooth as a stone in the hand.

And then the nursing home, where her mother lay. The room was high and white and smelled of eau-de-Cologne and something older underneath. The window looked on a plane tree, and the leaves moved against the glass all afternoon, so that the light in the room was never still. Her mother's hands on the counterpane were like something washed up, small and pale and curiously finished. She talked, that last day, of Trewellan—of the red bucket, of the mackerel, of the lamp lit early—and Beatrice, sitting beside her, understood that her mother was not remembering but living there, and that the white room and the plane tree and the daughter by the bed had all receded into a distance from which they could not be called back. *The sea*, her mother said, *the sea, Beatrice*, and then was quiet. And Beatrice sat on, listening to the leaves, and thought how strange it was that a life should end as it began, in water, and that the great restless animal at the bottom of the garden should have been waiting all along.

Now she was old, and the park was full of April. She came here on Thursdays with her granddaughter, a child of seven called Norah, who ran ahead and came back, ran ahead and came back, like a dog off the lead, and asked questions that had no answers. *Why is the grass green? Why is that man's hat like that?* And Beatrice said, *Because it is, because it is*, and watched the light come through the new leaves, that particular green of April, thin and gold and trembling, as if the whole world were being seen through a glass of white wine. Norah stopped at the pond and stood very still, and Beatrice stood beside her, and they looked at the ducks, and at the sky in the water, and at the clouds moving slowly under the ducks, and the child's hand came up and found hers, small and warm and sticky with something, and held it.

*Is that all?* she thought. *Is that a life?* The sea, and the sundial, and the clock in the dark hall, and the leaves against the glass, and now this—an old woman and a child and a pond in April, the ducks paddling through the clouds. It did not make a story. It made a series of moments, each one complete and shining and separate, like the beads her grandmother had worn, which she had once, as a child, let run through her fingers and off the string, and which had scattered across the floor and rolled into corners and been found, some of them, for years afterwards, in the strangest places—under a rug, behind a bookcase, in the toe of a shoe. She had never restrung them. She had kept them in a tin, and sometimes she took them out and held them, and they were beautiful and useless and hers.

Norah pulled at her hand. *Come on*, she said, *come on, come and see*, and Beatrice, who was old, who was slow, who felt the April cold in her bones, let herself be pulled along the path, past the deck-chairs and the men with their papers and the nursemaids with their prams, towards the gate where the chestnut trees were in flower, white and stiff and candelabra-like against the blue. And she thought, with a clarity that came to her less and less often now, and always with the same surprise: *It is enough. It is not enough. It is what there is.* And the child ran on ahead, and the light came through the leaves, and the moment—this one, now, this one—opened like a flower and closed again, and was gone.
