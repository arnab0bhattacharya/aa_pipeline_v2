Lady Imogen Vane had discovered that the most exquisite jewels are often the most dangerous. Her husband’s generosity, she learned, was a necklace of such gems, and she now found it was choking her. The news had come not in a letter or a confession, but in the cruel, polished form of a banker’s polite inquiry, slipped to her by a friend with more malice than sense. Lord Alistair Vane, her husband of two years, had settled the considerable gaming debts of a certain Mrs. Delphine de Courcy. Mrs. de Courcy was a woman whose reputation was a work of art in itself, constructed from whispers, ruined men, and the audacious glitter of her salon. To Imogen, the meaning was as clear and as cold as a diamond: her husband was paying for another’s pleasures.

When she confronted him in the library, a room of sober leather and hushed gravity, he did not deny it. He stood by the fireplace, a silhouette against the flames, and his silence was a more potent admission than any words.

“Alistair,” she had said, her voice a fragile thing in the vast room, “the sum is… considerable.”

“The debts of a friend are a tax upon friendship,” he replied, not turning. His tone was as smooth and impenetrable as polished agate. “And one must always pay one’s taxes.”

“Is she a friend? I had not thought Mrs. de Courcy cultivated friendship. She is said to prefer… patrons.”

Now he turned, and his face, usually so full of a gentle, weary affection, was a mask of stone. “Some women are a public necessity. They provide a service to society by drawing all its vices into a single, brilliant room. To support them is a form of civic duty.”

The paradox was so Wildean, so utterly his own brand of elegant absurdity, that for a moment Imogen was disarmed. But the wound was too fresh. “And what service, pray, does she provide to you?”

“That,” he said, his voice dropping to a note of finality, “is a matter for the ledger of my own conscience, and I do not permit auditors.”

He would say no more. His refusal was a wall of polished marble, beautiful and cold. In that moment, Imogen resolved that if she could not scale the wall, she would go around it. She would attend Mrs. de Courcy’s infamous Midwinter Masquerade. She would see this creature for herself, would stand in the very heart of her rival’s kingdom, and demand the truth not from her husband, but from the source of the corruption itself.

The night of the ball was a study in decadence. Mrs. de Courcy’s house in Mayfair blazed with a thousand gaslights that turned the falling snow to a storm of silver dust. Inside, the air was thick with the scent of tuberoses and expensive champagne. A river of silk and satin flowed through the halls, and behind jewelled masks, the aristocracy of London indulged in the delicious pleasure of being scandalised while appearing to be the cause of the scandal. Imogen, her face hidden by a simple black velvet mask, felt a profound sense of detachment, as if she were observing a curious and rather vulgar painting. She was a study in white and silver, a lily in a hothouse of orchids.

She found her quarry not holding court in the centre of the room, but observing it from a velvet-cushioned alcove, like a queen watching her gladiators. Mrs. Delphine de Courcy was not beautiful in the conventional sense. Her beauty was an argument, a sophisticated thesis on the power of artifice. Her hair was the colour of dark honey, her gown a cascade of peacock-blue silk that shimmered with every breath, and her eyes, visible above her gilded mask, held an expression of amused, intelligent boredom.

Imogen approached, her heart a frantic bird in her chest. “Mrs. de Courcy?”

The woman’s gaze swept over her, taking in every detail of her simple elegance. “My dear,” she purred, her voice like warm honey, “one does not use names at a masquerade. It is the one rule that keeps the evening from becoming as tedious as real life.”

“I am Lady Vane,” Imogen said, ignoring the pleasantry. The name hung in the air between them.

A slow smile spread across Mrs. de Courcy’s lips. “Ah, the lily. I wondered when I should have the pleasure. Your husband speaks of you.”

The casual cruelty of it was breathtaking. “My husband,” Imogen said, her voice tight, “is a man of expensive charities.”

“Charity is the most fashionable of sins,” Mrs. de Courcy replied, accepting a glass of champagne from a passing footman. “It allows one to feel wicked and virtuous in the same breath. A delicious sensation.”

“He has paid your debts. I wish to know why.”

Mrs. de Courcy took a delicate sip, her eyes never leaving Imogen’s. “My dear Lady Vane, you are labouring under the most charming of misunderstandings. You believe your husband is in love with me. How wonderfully conventional. How utterly middle-class.”

“Then what is the explanation?” Imogen demanded, her composure beginning to fray.

“The explanation is that Lord Vane is, above all else, a connoisseur of aesthetics. He does not collect women, my dear; he collects situations. He found my impending ruin to be in poor taste. A blemish on the social canvas. He paid my debts for the same reason one restores a Tiepolo ceiling or polishes a flawed emerald: not for love of the object, but for the preservation of a certain standard of beauty in the world. My ruin would have been a vulgar smudge, and your husband cannot abide vulgarity.”

Imogen stared, her certainty dissolving into a mist of confusion. “He did it for… aesthetics?”

“He did it to prevent a masterpiece of scandal from being spoiled by a clumsy, cheap denouement,” Mrs. de Courcy clarified, a flicker of genuine admiration in her eyes. “He is not my lover, Lady Vane. He is my patron. There is a difference, though I grant you it is a subtle one. He finds my life a more interesting work of art than any he could own. He keeps me from ruin so that he may continue to be entertained by my survival. It is the most selfish, and therefore the most honest, form of generosity.”

Just then, a familiar figure detached himself from the crowd and moved towards them. Lord Alistair Vane, his mask removed, his face pale and drawn. He had followed her. He looked from his wife’s stunned expression to the triumphant smile of Mrs. de Courcy.

“Imogen,” he said, his voice low and urgent. “You should not have come here.”

“On the contrary,” said Mrs. de Courcy, rising gracefully. “It was inevitable. She is the moral centre of your world, Alistair. She was bound to be drawn to its most immoral point. It is a matter of gravity.” She gave a little bow and melted back into the glittering throng, leaving them alone in the alcove.

“You heard her,” Imogen whispered.

“I did,” Alistair said, his eyes filled with a strange, weary pain. “And now you understand. I could not explain it to you because the truth is not a matter of the heart. It is a matter of taste. To have an affair would have been a sordid, predictable thing. But to keep a beautiful, dangerous creature from being destroyed by her own debts… that is a work of art. I am guilty of nothing but a desire to keep the world from being any duller than it already is.”

He looked at her, and for the first time, she saw not a guilty husband, but a man trapped in the gilded cage of his own philosophy. He had not been unfaithful in the flesh, but in spirit he had been living in another world altogether, a world where morality was merely a colour on a palette. Imogen looked past him at the swirling, masked dancers, a whirlwind of colour and light, and felt a profound chill. She had come seeking a simple truth, a villain and a victim. Instead, she found a paradox, and a husband who loved beauty more than he loved her. And as she stood there in her white silk gown, she felt that she was the most scandalous thing in the room: a wife who suddenly realised she was merely part of the décor.
