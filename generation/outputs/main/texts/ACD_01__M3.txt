It was upon a raw, foggy morning in November of 1895 that I first found myself in the cramped, gas-lit chambers of Mr. Silas Grier, a private inquiry agent whose remarkable success in unraveling the more obscure crimes of the City had become a matter of some quiet notoriety among the clerks of the Bank of England. I had served as a junior clerk at Halloway & Thorne’s, a respectable private banking house in Lothbury, for nearly three years, and it was there that the singular circumstance which drove me to Grier’s door had presented itself.

Grier was a tall, gaunt man, with a sharp, pale face and a pair of penetrating grey eyes that seemed to extract information from a client before a word was spoken. He sat at his desk, enveloped in a cloud of strong shag tobacco, and motioned me to a chair.

“You are in some trouble connected with your employment, Mr. Phipps,” he remarked, by way of greeting. “A bank clerk’s anxiety is usually written in his right hand, which has been clutching a pen, and in his left, which has been counting coin. But you carry a deposit book in your breast pocket, and your left cuff bears the distinct impression of a brass counter-rail. What is the matter?”

I was so startled by the accuracy of his observation that I momentarily forgot my errand. Then I drew a long breath and stated my case. For the past four months, an elderly gentleman of most respectable appearance had called at our bank every morning at precisely a quarter past ten, and again every evening at five minutes to three. His name was Mr. Josiah Amberley, and his transactions were of a nature so extraordinary that they had become the talk of the counting-house.

“Every morning,” I explained, “he deposits exactly one gold sovereign in a new account he opened at the end of July. Every afternoon, without fail, he withdraws that same sovereign, closing the day’s balance to zero.”

Grier’s eyebrows rose a fraction of an inch. “One sovereign in, one sovereign out. A singular ritual. And you say he has done this daily?”

“Without exception, sir. He is a man of perhaps sixty, very neatly dressed in a black frock-coat, carrying an oiled-leather satchel. He is most particular. Should the teller attempt to engage him in conversation, he would stare as though offended. He counts the coin, signs the slip, and departs. But yesterday morning he did not come. And this morning, too, he was absent. The non-appearance of a man so bound by routine struck me as ominous. I took the liberty of looking up his references. He gave his address as Number 14, Culloden Street, a quiet cul-de-sac off Finsbury Circus.”

Grier leaned forward and knocked out the dottle of his pipe into the grate. “You did well to come to me, Mr. Phipps. The regularity of the eccentric is a thing of nature, but the interruption of that regularity is a thing of art, and often of malice. Let us walk to Culldon Street.”

The fog had thickened by the time we reached Finsbury Circus, lending the city a yellow, suffocating pallor. Number 14 was a tall, narrow house of yellow brick, with heavy green blinds drawn down over every window. Grier paused at the foot of the stone steps, his keen eyes taking in the pavement, the knocker, and the brass plate beside the door, which bore the name of Amberley in faded lettering.

He rapped sharply. After a long interval, the door was opened by a slatternly woman with red eyes and a soiled apron, who proved to be the landlady.

“Mr. Amberley?” she repeated, in a voice thick with a cold. “He’s gone, sir. Left yesterday morning before the light. Took a cab. I was to give this letter to anyone who asked for him.”

She produced a sealed envelope, which Grier took, examined closely, and opened with a penknife he drew from his waistcoat. It contained a single sheet of paper, bearing a few lines written in a shaky, elderly hand. Grier read it, thrust it into his pocket, and turned to the woman.

“Did Mr. Amberley receive many visitors?” he asked.

“None, sir. He was a quiet gentleman. But on Tuesday evening, a man came. A rough-looking man, in a grey ulster and a billycock hat pulled low over his eyes. I heard them talking loud in the parlour—about money, I think. Then the gentleman left in a great hurry.”

“Was this visitor perhaps a man of middle height, with a dark beard and a scar upon his left cheek?”

The woman’s eyes widened. “Yes, sir! How did you know?”

Grier did not answer, but turned on his heel and walked briskly down the street, motioning for me to follow. “We have no time to lose, Mr. Phipps,” he said, as we turned into Finsbury Circus. “The matter is more serious than you imagined. Mr. Amberley has been the victim of a very old and very clever device. But for your sharp eyes, he might have lost not only his fortune, but his life.”

We took a hansom to a public house in Wapping, where Grier inquired of the barman for a man named Blackmore. The barman, after some hesitation, directed us to a back room. There, seated at a table covered with papers and empty glasses, was the very man the landlady had described—middle height, dark beard, a livid scar upon his left cheek. He looked up with a start as Grier entered.

“Well, Mr. Blackmore,” said Grier, in a quiet, steely voice, “I think you have something belonging to Mr. Amberley. A satchel, perhaps, containing some thirty thousand pounds in negotiable bonds.”

The man sprang to his feet, his hand going to his hip. But Grier had produced a small, serviceable revolver, and held it with a steadiness that was somehow more menacing than a shout.

“Sit down,” said Grier. “The game is up. I have your letter, Mr. Phipps, in which you confess to the whole affair.”

I stared at him in astonishment. “His letter, Mr. Grier? The letter was from Mr. Amberley!”

Grier smiled. “It was from Mr. Amberley, but it was dictated by this gentleman. The writing was shaky, you will recall, because it was written by a man under the influence of chloral hydrate. Mr. Amberley was a retired wholesale chemist, and he kept a quantity of this drug in his house. You will find it in his parlour, Mr. Phipps, if you care to return. But the key to the whole affair lies in that morning and evening ritual.

“You see, Mr. Phipps,” he continued, turning to me, “a man who deposits a sovereign every morning and withdraws it every evening is not a lunatic. He is a man who has made an appointment. The sovereign is a token. The morning deposit says, ‘I am here, and I have the funds.’ The evening withdrawal says, ‘I am still here, and I am ready for the exchange.’ It is a system used by certain continental criminals to signal to a confederate that a ransom or a payoff is prepared. The bank, with its regular hours and its respectable facade, serves as a dead-letter office.

“Mr. Amberley, as it happens, had been persuaded by this fellow Blackmore that his son, who is abroad in South America, was being held for ransom. The thirty thousand pounds was to be paid over at a meeting arranged for this very day. Blackmore drugged Amberley yesterday, forged the letter to cover his absence, and intended to collect the bonds himself. He would have succeeded, too, had it not been for the watchfulness of a bank clerk.”

Grier turned to Blackmore, who had slumped back into his chair, his face livid with fear. “You will accompany us to the nearest police station, Mr. Blackmore. And Mr. Phipps, I shall need you to make a statement. Mr. Amberley, who is at this moment lying insensible in his house in Culloden Street, will be deeply in your debt.”

It was not until late that evening, after we had given Blackmore into the custody of Inspector Marlow of the City police, and after a doctor had been summoned to attend the unfortunate Amberley, that I found myself once more in Grier’s chambers. The fire burned brightly, and the fog had lifted from the streets below.

“You did well to come to me, Mr. Phipps,” said Grier, pouring out two glasses of port. “The bank clerk’s eye is the first line of defence against the criminal, and yours has been singularly acute. But for your observation, Mr. Amberley would have been a poorer man, and the City of London a poorer place.”

I raised my glass. “I have to thank you, Mr. Grier, for unraveling what seemed to me an impenetrable mystery.”

Grier shook his head. “There is no mystery, my dear sir, where there is a sovereign deposited and withdrawn with the regularity of a clock. The unusual is always the gateway to the hidden. Remember that, and you will not go far wrong in the City of London.”
