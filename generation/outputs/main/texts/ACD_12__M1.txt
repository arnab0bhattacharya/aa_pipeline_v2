It was upon a raw November evening, with the fog lying thick and yellow in the streets of the Strand, that I found myself seated opposite my friend, Mr. Jabez Murchison, the secretary of the Ludgate Chess Club. The club’s rooms, situated upon the second floor of a narrow house near the Savoy, were warmed by a coal fire, but the atmosphere was heavy with the scent of stale tobacco and the peculiar, metallic odour of anxiety.

“You must understand, Dr. Watson,” said Murchison, nervously adjusting his gold-rimmed pince-nez, “that we are men of calculation. We deal in the sixty-four squares. We are not given to hysteria. Yet this matter has reduced our senior members to a state of bewilderment that borders on the superstitious.”

He pushed a sheet of paper across the green baize table. It was a record of games, meticulously kept in a copperplate hand.

“For the past month,” he continued, his voice dropping to a whisper, “a gentleman named Alistair Vane has won every single game. Not merely won, sir, but conquered. He has defeated the champion of the Thames Valley League and old Mr. Pritchard, who has not been beaten on home ground since the seventies. And the manner of the victories! The moves are impossible. They are like the strokes of a fencer who knows his opponent will lunge before the lunge is made.”

“Perhaps he is simply a player of genius,” I suggested.

“Genius has a rhythm, Doctor. It has a logic. Mr. Vane plays with a cold, mechanical precision that defies the spirit of the game. But it is the incident of last Tuesday that has prompted us to seek your advice. Or rather, the advice of your illustrious colleague.”

Murchison looked at me with an imploring gaze. I saw that the affair had struck deep.

“Tell me of the incident,” said I.

“Mr. Vane was playing young Hartington. A fine player, though hot-headed. The game had lasted two hours. Vane had maneuvered his Queen into a position that seemed suicidal, yet left Hartington with no escape. Hartington, in a great state of agitation, reached for his teacup. We drink tea during the adjournments, you see. He took one sip, coughed violently, and fell forward upon the board, scattering the pieces. He was quite insensible.”

“Dead?” I asked sharply.

“Near it. He was taken to St. Thomas’s, where he remains. The doctors speak of a sudden cardiac failure induced by shock, but there was a peculiar taste to the tea, they said. Yet they found no poison, or at least, none they could name. The police have been called, but they are baffled. We cannot have a scandal, Watson. It would ruin the club. We need Holmes.”

I promised to lay the matter before him, and within the hour we were in a hansom, rattling over the cobbles towards the rooms of the Ludgate Chess Club. Holmes sat in silence, his sharp features illuminated by the intermittent flare of the street lamps. He had listened to my recital with his eyes closed, but I knew he had missed no syllable.

The club was in a state of suppressed commotion when we arrived. Murchison led us through a small anteroom into the main hall. It was a long, low room, lined with bookshelves and occupied by some twenty tables. The air was thick with the smoke of cigars. At the far end, a tournament was in progress.

“There,” Murchison whispered, pointing. “He is at the third table from the window.”

I looked. The man known as Alistair Vane was of medium height, dressed in a severe black frock coat. His hands, resting upon the table, were long and white, the fingers tapering. He sat with a posture of absolute stillness, his eyes fixed upon the board. Opposite him sat a portly gentleman with mutton-chop whiskers, who was mopping his brow with a handkerchief.

Holmes did not approach the table. Instead, he drifted to the side of the room, his eyes darting from the player to the spectators, to the floor, and to the window. He observed the tea service upon a sideboard—a silver urn and a row of china cups. He noted the rug beneath Vane’s chair. He watched the clock upon the mantelpiece.

For half an hour, we stood in the shadows. The only sound was the click of chessmen and the heavy breathing of the players. Suddenly, the portly gentleman groaned and leaned back. Vane extended a hand and tipped over his own King.

“Checkmate,” Vane said. His voice was soft, yet it carried across the room with an eerie clarity.

The defeated man stared at the board, then at Vane. “I don’t see it,” he stammered. “I don’t see how you forced it.”

“You missed the Knight’s retreat on the fourteenth move,” Vane replied, his tone devoid of emotion. “It was inevitable.”

Holmes touched my arm. “Come, Watson. Let us examine the scene of the tragedy.”

We moved to the table where the incident had occurred. It was currently unoccupied. Holmes dropped to his knees and examined the carpet with his lens. He sniffed the air near the floorboards. Then he rose and walked to the window.

“Murchison,” Holmes called softly. The secretary hurried over. “Does that window overlook a courtyard?”

“Yes, sir. It is a light well for the kitchens below.”

“And the sill? It appears rather dusty, yet there is a clean patch here, roughly the size of a man’s hand.”

Murchison looked bewildered. “The charwoman cleans the sills on Mondays.”

“Precisely,” said Holmes. He turned his gaze back to the room. His eyes settled upon Alistair Vane, who was now sipping a glass of water, his face a mask of pale indifference.

Holmes walked briskly across the room. He did not introduce himself. He stood before Vane’s table and looked down at the board.

“A curious opening,” Holmes remarked. “The Sicilian Defense, but played with a variation I have not seen in London.”

Vane looked up. His eyes were grey and very cold. “It is a line of my own devising, sir.”

“So I perceive. It has a certain mechanical symmetry. Tell me, do you find the game of chess a stimulating mental exercise, or merely a mathematical problem?”

“It is a contest of wills,” Vane said.

“Is it?” Holmes smiled, a thin, mirthless smile. “I should have said it was a contest of information. But we shall not detain you.”

Holmes turned away abruptly and walked to the sideboard. He picked up one of the teacups left from the previous week, which Murchison had preserved. He held it to the light. Then he did a strange thing. He walked to the window, opened it, and leaned out into the cold, foggy air of the light well. He remained there for a moment, his hand resting on the sill.

When he returned, his face was grim.

“Watson,” he said, “pray ask Mr. Murchison if there is a ladder available in the building. A short one will suffice.”

Murchison, looking terrified, hurried away. Holmes turned to me.

“The game is up, Watson. But not for Mr. Vane. Not yet.”

Murchison returned with a step-ladder. Holmes took it from him and placed it beneath the window he had just examined. He climbed up and reached towards the upper part of the window frame, hidden by the heavy velvet curtain.

“I thought as much,” he muttered. He reached into the darkness and withdrew a small, black object. It looked like a cigarette case, but it was attached to a wire.

He descended and placed the object upon the table. It was a small, battery-operated device, crudely made but ingenious. Attached to it was a tiny, needle-like projection.

“Mr. Vane,” Holmes called out, his voice ringing through the hall. “I believe you have lost something.”

The room fell silent. Vane looked up. For the first time, a flicker of emotion crossed his face—surprise, quickly suppressed.

“What is that?” he asked.

“It is a receiver,” said Holmes. “And a transmitter. The wire runs up the wall, behind the curtain, and out through the window. On the roof of the adjacent building, or perhaps in the room above, sits a confederate. He watches the board through a telescope or a periscope—perhaps even through a hole in the ceiling, which I shall find presently. He analyzes the game with the aid of a powerful engine or a master player, and transmits the correct move to you via a series of electrical impulses, which you feel as a tingling in your fingers or hear as a faint code through a hidden earpiece.”

Vane stood up. “This is madness. You are accusing me of cheating?”

“I am accusing you of murder, Mr. Vane,” Holmes said, his voice dropping to a deadly hush. “Or at least, attempted murder. The device is not merely for the game. The wire also controls a small mechanism near the tea service. When young Hartington was winning—when he had beaten your mechanical system—you activated the device. A small quantity of concentrated digitalin was released into his cup. It is a poison that mimics heart failure and dissipates quickly, leaving little trace.”

“You cannot prove it,” Vane snarled. His hand moved towards his pocket.

Holmes was faster. He lunged forward and seized Vane’s wrist. With a twist, he forced the man’s hand open. In the palm lay a small, glass pellet.

“Proof enough,” said Holmes. “And I have no doubt that the police will find your accomplice upon the roof, freezing to death with his telescope.”

The silence in the room was absolute. Then, from the doorway, came the heavy tread of boots. Inspector Lestrade, summoned by a telegram Holmes had sent an hour prior, stepped into the light.

“I think we can take it from here, Mr. Holmes,” Lestrade said, producing a pair of handcuffs.

As Vane was led away, his face ashen and his shoulders slumped, Murchison collapsed into a chair.

“I cannot believe it,” he whispered. “A machine? To win at chess?”

“It is the modern spirit, Murchison,” Holmes said, lighting a cigarette. “The desire to win without effort, to conquer without risk. But the human element, I am glad to say, remains unpredictable.”

He turned to me as the police van rattled away into the fog.

“A cold night, Watson. I think a glass of brandy at the Diogenes Club is indicated. The game, as they say, is over.”
