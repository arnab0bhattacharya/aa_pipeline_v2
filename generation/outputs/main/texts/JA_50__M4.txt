The Assembly Rooms at Weymouth were, on the evening in question, as brilliant and as crowded as the most sanguine visitor could desire. The season was at its height, the band was in excellent practice, and every family of consequence within twenty miles had come to see and to be seen. Among those who entered with the modest bustle of first attendance were two young ladies from the village of Ashcombe, Miss Harriet Ellery and her particular friend Miss Catherine Pemberton, who had been suffered to make the journey under the protection of the former's aunt, a widow of small fortune and large confidence in her own judgement.

Miss Ellery was pretty, and knew it; she was lively, and knew that too. She had no sooner crossed the threshold than her eyes began their work of agreeable calculation, and by the third dance she was engaged for every one that remained upon the card. Her figure was good, her spirits unfailing, and she smiled upon her partners with a liberality that ensured her a partner for the evening entire. She returned to her aunt between the dances with her colour heightened and her fan in constant motion, and declared that she had never been so happy in her life.

Miss Pemberton, who was two years her senior, and possessed of a countenance rather sensible than striking, stood by Mrs. Ellery's chair through the whole of it. She was not without admirers, had she chosen to encourage them; but she had early determined that a ball was a thing to be observed before it was enjoyed, and that to dance with a stranger merely because he asked her was to purchase a fleeting pleasure at the price of a lasting mortification. She therefore declined the two gentlemen who applied to her, with a civility that left them no cause of complaint and no hope of success, and employed herself in attending to the music, to the dresses of the company, and to the behaviour of her friend, which last afforded her a good deal of quiet amusement.

"You are very cruel," said Harriet, returning flushed and breathless from a country dance. "Poor Mr. Selby has been watching you this half hour."

"Mr. Selby is very good-natured to watch me," replied Catherine, "but I am persuaded he will recover."

Mrs. Ellery, who considered dancing the great end of a young woman's education, was disposed to be severe upon such singularity; but as Catherine bore it with perfect good humour, and never once complained of the headache which she had been at some pains to feel, the aunt contented herself with observing that she hoped Miss Pemberton would not regret her resolution when it was too late.

Among the company was a gentleman of about eight-and-twenty, of a serious aspect and a very good air, who had been introduced to the party early in the evening by a common acquaintance. His name was Mr. Delafield, and he was understood to be lately come from the north, with a fortune sufficient to make his opinions worth attending to. He had danced once with Harriet, and had afterwards stood a considerable time near the chairs of the Ashcombe ladies, saying little, but observing a great deal. Catherine had remarked that his manner, though reserved, was perfectly unassuming; and that when Harriet, in the gaiety of her heart, had rallied him upon his gravity, he had answered with a good-humoured readiness that did him credit.

On the following morning, the two young ladies were sitting with Mrs. Ellery in the parlour of their lodgings in the Crescent, when the servant brought in a card. Mrs. Ellery took it, and read aloud, with an accent of some surprise, "Mr. Delafield."

"Mr. Delafield!" cried Harriet, starting up. "La! I danced with him but once. What can he want?"

"Perhaps," said Catherine, "he wishes to dance with you again."

"With me? No, no, he can have no such thought. It is you he comes to see, I dare say; he looked at you a great deal last night. You know you would not dance with anybody, and so he is come to be introduced to you properly."

"I think it much more likely," said Catherine, "that he is come to pay his respects to your aunt."

Mrs. Ellery, who was not insensible to the honour of a morning visit from a gentleman of fortune, composed her countenance and her cap, and desired that the gentleman might be shown up.

Mr. Delafield entered with the ease of a man who had no apologies to make for coming. He spoke to Mrs. Ellery of the weather and the rooms, to Harriet of the dance she had done him the honour to dance with him, and then, turning to Catherine, he said, "I believe I am not mistaken in supposing that you are no great lover of dancing, Miss Pemberton."

"I am not fond of it," she replied, "but I hope I am not so singular as to condemn it in others."

"You did not dance at all last night, I think."

"No, I did not."

"And yet," said he, "I should have thought you might have been tempted. The band was good, and the company cheerful."

"A cheerful company is a very pleasant thing to look at," said Catherine, "and I had the pleasure of seeing my friend enjoy herself, which was quite as agreeable to me as if I had been dancing myself."

Mr. Delafield smiled, and said that he had observed as much, and that he had been struck by the singularity of a young lady who could be happy in another's happiness without any share in it herself.

"There is no great merit in that," said Catherine. "Harriet is so happy that it is impossible not to catch something of it."

"You speak as if you thought it a duty to be pleased."

"I think it is a duty to be pleased when we can," said she, "and a very easy one, when there is so much to please us."

Mr. Delafield looked at her with an attention that was not lost upon Mrs. Ellery, who began to think that her niece's friend was a young woman of more understanding than she had given her credit for. He then turned the conversation to books, and it appeared that they had read many of the same, and differed only in the degree of their admiration; and from books he passed to the country about Weymouth, and from that to the country about Ashcombe, of which he had heard, he said, a very good account.

Harriet, who had expected to be the object of the visit, and who had prepared a great many lively things to say, found herself, for the first time in her life, a little at a loss. She was not accustomed to be overlooked, and she could not help thinking it very odd that a gentleman should come to a house to see one young lady and talk to another. She said as much to Catherine when he was gone, and Catherine, who was too honest to pretend not to understand her, replied, "He came to see us all, my dear, and he talked to me because I happened to sit next him."

"But he might have talked to me. I was not afraid of him."

"No, indeed, you were not," said Catherine, laughing; "and that is perhaps the very reason he did not."

Mrs. Ellery, who had been silent for some time, now observed that Mr. Delafield was a very agreeable man, and that she hoped they should see more of him; and Harriet, who was too good-natured to be long out of humour, declared that she did not care if she never danced again, if it was to bring such a visitor as that to the house. Catherine said nothing, but she thought that a gentleman who could prefer a quiet conversation to a ballroom was a novelty worth observing; and she was not sorry that she had declined to dance.
