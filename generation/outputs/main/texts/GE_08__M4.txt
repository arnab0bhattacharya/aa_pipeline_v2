Mr. Lucian Ferrers, of the Middle Temple, was a young man whom fortune had treated with a tenderness that bordered on indulgence. His father, a manufacturer of agricultural implements in the county town of Marstonbury, had amassed a competence by the steady application of a shrewd though narrow intelligence, and had expended a portion of it in bestowing upon his son the education of a gentleman—an education which Lucian had received with the languid grace of one who accepts a gift without inquiring into its cost. He had been sent to Rugby, thence to Oxford, and finally to the Bar, not because he had any intention of practising, but because the Bar was understood to be the proper destination of a young man of means who had no distinct vocation, and because his father, who had a reverent faith in the mystery of the legal profession, wished to see his son's name upon the boards of an Inn of Court. Lucian had eaten his dinners with exemplary punctuality, had been called, and had since occupied a set of chambers in the Temple which were chiefly remarkable for the number of unopened law books upon the shelves and the excellence of the cigars upon the table.

It was his habit, once or twice in the year, to visit Marstonbury, where his mother still lived in the substantial brick house with the laburnum in the front garden, and where he was received with that mixture of pride and solicitude which provincial parents commonly exhibit towards a son who has risen above them in the social scale. On these occasions he was conscious of a pleasant condescension, a gracious willingness to be pleased with the old place and the old faces, which he mistook for affection, and which was in truth the self-satisfaction of a man who feels that he has escaped. He walked through the streets of the town with the air of a traveller in a foreign city, observing the quaintness of the inhabitants with a tolerant amusement, and was wont to say afterwards, in the smoking-room of his club, that Marstonbury was a place where one might study the manners of the last generation, if one had the patience.

It chanced that in the autumn of the year 1864, being at Marstonbury on one of these periodical visits, he was applied to by the town clerk, an old friend of his father's, to undertake the defence of a poor glove-maker, one Matthew Gedge, who stood committed for trial at the ensuing assizes upon a charge of stealing a silver watch and a sum of money from the house of a farmer named Harlow, in the neighbouring parish of Beckworth. The town clerk, who was a man of easy conscience and a great regard for the Ferrers family, explained that the prisoner was a fellow of no education, who had got himself into trouble through his own obstinacy, and that the case was perfectly clear against him, but that as the poor wretch had no friends and no money, and as the assizes would be held in a fortnight, it would be a charitable thing if Mr. Ferrers would consent to appear for him. Lucian, who had never in his life addressed a jury, and whose knowledge of criminal law was confined to a vague recollection of the statutes of his student days, was at first inclined to refuse; but the novelty of the proposal, the flattery of being asked, and a certain romantic notion of doing a good action in a humble way, combined to overcome his hesitation. He consented, with a smile which the town clerk interpreted as a mark of condescension, but which was in reality the smile of a man who is pleased with his own generosity.

The trial came on at the Michaelmas assizes. Lucian, who had spent the intervening fortnight in a diligent perusal of such authorities as he could find in the Marstonbury subscription library, and in a conscientious though somewhat bewildered attempt to master the facts of the case, presented himself at the court-house on the morning of the hearing with a sensation of nervous excitement which was entirely new to him. The court-house was a small, dingy building, smelling of damp plaster and stale tobacco, and crowded with a motley assemblage of farmers, tradesmen, and labourers, who had come to see the sport. The judge was a testy old gentleman with a gouty foot, who had no patience with the follies of mankind, and who was understood to be particularly severe upon offences against property. The counsel for the prosecution was a sharp young barrister from London, who had already made his mark upon the circuit, and who conducted his case with a brisk assurance that made Lucian's heart sink within him.

The facts, as they were unfolded, were these. On the night of the 14th of September, the farmer Harlow's house had been entered by a window which was found open in the morning, and the watch and money, which had been left upon the kitchen table, were missing. The prisoner, Matthew Gedge, had been seen in the neighbourhood of the house on the evening in question, and had been heard to say, in the public-house of the village, that he "shouldn't mind havin' a silver watch like that o' Harlow's"—a remark which, in the mouth of a poor man, was construed by the prosecution as a criminal intention. He had been arrested two days afterwards, and though nothing was found upon him, and though he protested his innocence with a vehemence that was perhaps too loud to be convincing, the evidence was considered sufficient to justify his committal.

Lucian, in his opening speech, made a well-meaning but somewhat rambling attempt to suggest that the evidence was circumstantial, and that the prisoner's remark at the public-house was capable of an innocent interpretation. But he was conscious that he was speaking to deaf ears. The jury, composed of farmers and tradesmen, who had already formed their opinion, looked at him with a stolid indifference which was more discouraging than hostility. The judge interrupted him twice with sharp questions, and once with a sarcasm which made him colour to the roots of his hair. When the prisoner was called upon for his defence, he made a statement which, in its confused earnestness, seemed to Lucian to possess a certain pathos, but which produced no effect upon the court. He said that he had been at the public-house on the evening in question, but that he had left it early, and had gone home to his wife, who was ill; that he had never been near Harlow's house; and that the watch, which he had admired in the window of a jeweller's shop in Marstonbury, had been the subject of his remark, and not the farmer's property. He spoke in the broad dialect of the county, with a faltering tongue and a bewildered eye, and Lucian, listening to him, was struck for the first time with the reflection that he had never before heard that dialect spoken by any one who was in earnest. It had always seemed to him a picturesque survival, a quaint relic of a simpler age, fit for the mouths of stage peasants and the pages of pastoral poetry. Now, as he heard it proceeding from the lips of a man who was fighting for his liberty, it sounded in his ears like the voice of a stranger, a voice from another world, a world of which he knew nothing, and which had never spoken to him before.

The jury, without leaving the box, returned a verdict of guilty. The judge, after a brief and severe allocution, in which he dwelt upon the prevalence of dishonesty among the labouring classes, and the necessity of a salutary example, sentenced the prisoner to twelve months' imprisonment with hard labour. As the man was led away, he turned and looked at Lucian with an expression which was not anger, nor reproach, but a kind of dull wonder, as if he were trying to understand by what mysterious process a gentleman in a black coat and a white tie had come to be his advocate, and why that advocacy had been so unavailing. Lucian, gathering up his papers with a trembling hand, felt that he had never in his life been so thoroughly ashamed of himself.

He walked back to his mother's house through the streets of Marstonbury, which seemed to him to have changed in some indefinable way since the morning. The familiar shops, the familiar faces, the familiar smells, had all at once become strange to him. He looked at the passers-by with a new curiosity, and it seemed to him that they returned his look with a new suspicion. He had lived in Marstonbury for the first eighteen years of his life, and he had never before realised that it was a place where men and women suffered, and struggled, and went to prison, and that he himself, in his comfortable ignorance, had been as remote from them as if he had lived in another planet. He thought of the glove-maker's wife, lying ill in her cottage, and of the glove-maker himself, in his prison cell, and he felt a sudden and overwhelming sense of the injustice of a world in which such things could happen, and in which he, Lucian Ferrers, with his education and his opportunities, had been content to look on with the amused tolerance of a spectator.

That evening he wrote to the town clerk, enclosing a sum of money for the relief of the prisoner's family, and requesting that no mention should be made of his name. He did not remain long in Marstonbury. He returned to London a few days afterwards, and resumed his old life; but he was never quite the same man again. He took to reading his law books with a new diligence, and he was heard to say, in the smoking-room of his club, that the study of human nature was a more serious business than he had once supposed, and that the manners of the last generation, whatever their picturesque qualities, were not to be understood without a knowledge of the sufferings which had helped to form them.
