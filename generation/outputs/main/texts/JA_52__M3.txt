Mr. Arthur Wendover was the younger son of a respectable Berkshire family, whose estate, while ancient in its pedigree, was somewhat diminished in its revenues. He resided at Thornfield Lodge, a neat though modest property situated within easy sight of his elder brother's more imposing mansion. His income, though sufficient for a bachelor of quiet habits, would not support any indulgence in fashionable folly, and he was therefore the more alarmed when his sisters, Mrs. Croft and Mrs. Marlow, concerted a plan for his advancement which they considered as nothing less than his due.

The object of their scheme was Mrs. Helen Vere, a widow of considerable fortune and pleasing address, who had lately taken possession of Hayle Park, a fine estate lying some three miles distant from Thornfield. Mrs. Croft, who prided herself on a discernment that was rarely exercised to any useful purpose, had discovered in Mrs. Vere a lady exactly suited to remedy every deficiency of her brother's situation. She was rich, she was agreeable, she was not above five-and-thirty, and she had no encumbrance of children to dissipate the wealth she had inherited from a late husband whose memory she could not be reasonably expected to regret.

"I do not ask you to be romantic, Arthur," said Mrs. Croft, settling herself with the air of one whose advice was equivalent to a command. "I only ask you to be sensible. Mrs. Vere is everything that a man of your station could wish for, and if you are not blind to your own advantage, you will make yourself agreeable to her before some other man with fewer scruples steps in."

Mr. Wendover, who was standing by the window with his back towards the fire, in an attitude that betrayed more of uneasiness than of patience, replied that he was very much obliged to her for her concern, but that he did not entirely see the necessity of marrying for fortune alone.

"Fortune alone!" cried Mrs. Marlow, who was a lady of still quicker feelings than her elder sister, and somewhat less regard for proportion in her expressions. "Who talks of fortune alone? Mrs. Vere is handsome, she is accomplished, she is admired by everybody. I am sure I do not know what more you could expect, unless you are waiting for a girl of seventeen with no portion and a great deal of nonsense."

Mr. Wendover made no answer to this, but turned again to the window, where he amused himself by tracing the pattern of the leaded glass with a finger, in a manner that showed his thoughts were not wholly fixed upon the conversation. He had, in fact, a very strong objection to being managed by his sisters; but he had an equally strong objection to owning it, and he was therefore reduced to silence, which his sisters interpreted as yielding, and which was, in truth, anything but.

The occasion that brought the matter to a crisis was a dinner party at Hayle Park, to which Mr. Wendover was invited, along with his sisters and a dozen other persons of consequence in the neighbourhood. Mrs. Vere received her guests with that easy grace which is the result of good breeding and a consciousness of having nothing to fear from the judgement of others. She was a woman who had seen enough of the world to be above its little vanities, and who had suffered enough from it to value sincerity when she found it. Mr. Wendover, who had been prepared by his sisters to find her either formidable or affected, was surprised to discover that she was neither. She spoke to him with a candour and an absence of artifice that he had seldom met with in women who had anything to gain by concealment, and he found himself, before the evening was half over, listening to her with an attention that was not wholly disinterested.

Mrs. Croft, who watched the progress of the conversation from across the table with the keenness of a general surveying a battlefield, drew from it a conviction that her scheme was advancing with all the rapidity she could wish. She communicated her satisfaction to Mrs. Marlow by a look which that lady perfectly understood, and they both retired that night in a state of complacency that nothing but success could have produced.

Mr. Wendover, on the contrary, retired in a state of considerable perplexity. He was forced to acknowledge that Mrs. Vere was a woman whom he could like and even esteem. Her conversation was sensible, her manners were unaffected, and there was a warmth in her address that spoke of a heart not yet closed against the best feelings of our nature. But he was equally forced to acknowledge that his sisters had marked her out for him, and this circumstance alone was sufficient to give him pause. He was not so unreasonable as to reject a woman merely because his sisters approved of her; but he was not so reasonable as to be wholly unaffected by the reflection that his choice, if he made it, would be attributed to their management rather than to his own preference. He had a just opinion of his own understanding, and he could not bear to have it supposed that he was a man to be directed by others in a matter so entirely his own.

The difficulty of his situation was increased by a subsequent interview with Mrs. Vere, which occurred a few days later, when he called at Hayle Park by her express invitation. She received him alone, and after some conversation of a general nature, she spoke with a directness that he had not anticipated.

"I am told, Mr. Wendover," she said, fixing her eyes upon him with a look that was at once serious and kind, "that your sisters have a particular wish to see you established in life, and that they have honoured me with the distinction of being their chosen instrument for that purpose."

Mr. Wendover coloured deeply, and began to utter something about his sisters' good intentions, but she interrupted him with a gentle smile.

"I do not blame them. I am too well acquainted with the world to wonder at such efforts. But I must tell you, Mr. Wendover, that I am not a woman to be disposed of by others, and I should be sorry to think that any gentleman was induced to address me from any motive but his own."

She paused, and Mr. Wendover, who had by this time recovered his composure, replied with an earnestness that surprised himself, that he was wholly incapable of offering his addresses to any woman from a motive of duty or obligation, and that if he were ever so happy as to obtain the regard of a woman worthy of it, it would be because his heart was truly engaged.

Mrs. Vere heard this with an expression that he could not altogether read, but which seemed to denote more of satisfaction than of disappointment. She said only that she was glad to find him so decided, and the conversation turned to other subjects.

On his return to Thornfield, Mr. Wendover found his sisters waiting for him with an impatience that left him no room to doubt the nature of the intelligence they expected. Mrs. Croft began by enquiring, in a tone of assumed indifference, how he had found Mrs. Vere. He replied that he had found her very well. Mrs. Marlow asked whether she had been alone. He said she had. Mrs. Croft then ventured to hope that the visit had been a pleasant one. He allowed that it had.

This was all that could be drawn from him, and his sisters, who were not accustomed to such reserve on his part, retired to their own apartment to canvass the meaning of his coolness. Mrs. Croft was for considering it a proof that he was secretly engaged, and that his pride would not yet suffer him to own it. Mrs. Marlow, who was more inclined to alarm, feared it was the very reverse, and that he had taken some dislike to Mrs. Vere which nothing could overcome.

In this, however, she was mistaken. Mr. Wendover had taken no dislike to Mrs. Vere. He was, on the contrary, more inclined to like her than he had ever been, and the frankness with which she had spoken of his sisters' interference had removed the chief obstacle to his own inclinations. He was no longer under the apprehension that an attachment to her would be construed into a yielding to their management. She had herself declared that she would not accept him on such terms, and this declaration, by setting him free from the only embarrassment that had restrained him, left him at liberty to consult his own heart.

He did so, and the result was such as might have been expected from a man of sense and feeling, who had found a woman whose understanding he could esteem and whose manners he could love. He resolved to address Mrs. Vere, not because his sisters desired it, but because he himself wished it; and he had the satisfaction, when he carried his resolution into effect, of receiving an answer which, while it granted him everything he asked, did so with a warmth of affection that left no room to doubt its sincerity.

His sisters, when the engagement was announced, were divided between triumph and vexation — triumph, in that their scheme had succeeded, and vexation, in that they could not persuade themselves it had succeeded through their influence. Mr. Wendover, who was not destitute of a quiet enjoyment of the ridiculous, observed their conflict of feeling with an amusement that he took some pains to conceal, and applied himself, with a steadiness of purpose that did him credit, to the task of being happy in his own way, without appearing to be so in theirs.
