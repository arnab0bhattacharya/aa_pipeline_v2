The bell in the tower above the mews had not yet struck, and yet the sound seemed to hover, suspended in the late October afternoon, a vibration in the yellow leaves that clung to the branches of the elms in the square. Eleanor walked, slowly, as one who bears an invisible weight, down the length of Cartwright Street. She had walked it, this same stretch of paving stones, ten thousand times perhaps; and yet to-day, because the deed was signed and the vanes would come to-morrow, the street seemed to rear up, to fling itself wide, to present itself as it had been, and as it was, all at once, so that the very bricks were molten, fluid, slipping into the past.

How small it was, she thought; how insignificant, this cul-de-sac of grey-fronted houses with their white steps and their black iron railings, wedged between the roar of the omnibuses and the quiet of the garden square. And yet, what a weight it bore; how it pressed upon her, this street, with its layers of lives, of voices, of curtains drawn and undrawn, of cats on window-sills, of dustbins clattering in the dawn. She paused by the lamppost at the corner, the one with the rusted base, and looked at Number Four.

There, in the basement, Mrs. Carker had kept her parrots; and the smell of seed and the sudden screech of green wings tearing the air had been the very essence of the summer of ’ninety-seven, when Eleanor had come back from Italy, tanned, with a parasol torn by the wind, to find her mother dying. The parrots had screeched then; they had seemed to know, to mock the hushed voices on the stairs; and the green of their wings had been like the green of the cypresses at Settignano, which she would never see again, or so she had thought, in that first flood of grief, which was now not grief at all but a pale, clear thing, like water in a glass, something one could hold up to the light and see through.

And next door, at Number Five, the Winches. There had been a daughter, Maud, who played the piano; scales, arpeggios, the same passage of Chopin, stumbling, repeating, until the notes became part of the fabric of the house, part of the wallpaper, the very texture of the carpet on the stairs. Maud had gone to India; had married a man in the Civil Service; and had died, it was rumoured, of cholera, though the letter, when it came, had said only “fever.” Fever. The word had a quality of heat, of flickering red, as if Maud had been consumed by the very word itself, consumed by India, that red country, so different from this grey street where the rain now fell, finely, in a mist that blurred the window-panes.

Eleanor moved on. The leaves were thicker here, piled against the kerb, sodden, releasing a smell of decay, of earth, of the end of things. She had been twenty when she came to Cartwright Street; twenty, and eager, and possessed of a sense that life was a vast, luminous plain waiting to be traversed. She had hung yellow curtains in the front room; she had bought a blue rug; she had placed a vase of chrysanthemums on the table, and had sat, in the evening, with a book, feeling that she was at last at the centre of things, in the heart of the world.

And the people who had come and gone! They streamed through her mind now, a procession of faces, of hands, of voices saying fragmentary things. Mr. Craddock, at Number Seven, who had been a clerk in the Admiralty, and who had stood at his door every morning, looking up at the sky, saying, “Rain, I think, Mrs. Ray; rain before night.” And it had rained; it had seemed always to be raining, in those days, before the war, when the world was solid, when the houses were full of people who would always be there, who would never leave, who were as permanent as the bricks themselves.

But they had left. They had died, or moved away, or simply vanished, as if called by some silent voice, some summons that only they could hear. The Bakers, at Number Nine, who had given such grand dinners, at which one sat late, drinking wine, talking of art, of politics, of the future of the novel; they were in Brighton now, old, decrepit, their voices thin as thread. And the Haddons, opposite, who had been so beautiful, so young, so full of laughter; he had been killed in France, in the first year of the war, and she had gone away, had taken the child and gone, and the house had stood empty for a year, its windows blank, its steps untrodden.

Eleanor paused again, opposite her own house, Number Twelve. The steps had been scrubbed that morning; the brass knocker shone; the window-boxes were filled with late geraniums, red and sullen, as if they resented the approach of frost. She looked at the house, and it seemed to look back at her, with its three windows and its door, like a face that knows everything and says nothing. How many selves she had been, inside those walls! The young bride, hanging curtains; the mother, holding a baby up to the window to see the snow; the woman of forty, sitting alone in the dining-room, listening to the clock, which ticked, ticked, as if it were slowly, deliberately, measuring out the remaining moments of her life.

And Robert. He was there, suddenly, not as a memory, but as a presence, a shadow thrown by the evening light against the pale stone. She could see him, standing at the door, his hat on, his gloves in his hand, turning to say something; what had he said? She could not remember; the words had gone, had dissolved, had become part of the air, the stones, the mist that was settling now on the street, softening the outlines, blurring the edges, so that the houses seemed to float, to lose their solidity, to become as insubstantial as the people who had lived in them.

She thought of the day they had moved in; the confusion of boxes, the smell of fresh paint, the workmen standing about in the hall, eating bread and cheese. And Robert, standing in the middle of it all, laughing, saying, “Well, Eleanor, here we are!” Here we are. And here they had been, for forty years; here they had lived, and loved, and quarrelled, and grown old; here the children had been born, and had grown up, and had gone away; here Robert had died, in the back room, on a morning in March, when the crocuses were out, and the sound of a barrel-organ had come in through the open window, playing a tune that she had never been able to identify, but which, ever since, had seemed to her to be the very sound of death itself, of the soul departing, lightly, easily, as a leaf falls from a tree.

She turned away. The street was empty now; the lamps were being lit, one by one, down the length of it, pools of greenish light in the blue dusk. The houses seemed to draw back, to retreat into themselves, to become secretive, silent. She could see, here and there, a lighted window; a figure moving behind a curtain; the blue flicker of a gas-lamp. And she thought: I am the last; the last of all of them; the only one left who remembers. For what is a street, what are houses, without the people who have lived in them? Only shells; only husks; only empty vessels, waiting to be filled again, by other lives, other voices, other loves.

She walked on, to the end of the street, where the railings of the square gave on to the garden. The trees were dark now, masses of shadow, and the paths were wet, gleaming in the lamplight. A nurse passed, wheeling a perambulator; a man in a mackintosh hurried by, his head down, his footsteps echoing on the pavement. Eleanor stood by the gate, looking back down the length of Cartwright Street. It was almost invisible now, swallowed by the dusk, by the mist, by the silence that falls on London streets at the end of an October afternoon. It was a street; only a street; a row of brick houses in Bloomsbury. And yet it was, she knew, as she turned away, as much a part of her as the veins in her hand, as the blood in her body; it was herself; it was her life.

The rain fell, softly, persistently, on the empty street, on the scrubbed steps, on the geraniums in the window-boxes, on the brass knocker that shone like gold in the lamplight. And the bell in the tower above the mews struck, at last, six.
