The fog that hung over London that Tuesday morning was of a peculiarly viscous quality, a grey sludge that clung to the coats of the passers-by and settled into the crevices of the paving stones. It was a fit atmosphere for tragedy, I thought, as I stood in the doorway of my consulting rooms on Baker Street, watching the street below. My companion, Mr. Aldous Thorne, was already in a state of considerable agitation, pacing the narrow hallway with the restless energy of a man who has not slept and fears he never will again.

When the knock came, it was not the tentative tap of a suitor or the sharp rap of a bill-collector, but a heavy, rhythmic pounding that seemed to vibrate through the very floorboards. Thorne opened the door to find a man of formidable stature, his face a map of deep lines etched by wind and rain, his hands wrapped in a pair of threadbare gloves that he wrung nervously in his grasp.

“Mr. Thorne?” the man asked. His voice was a low rumble, like distant thunder.

“You are expected, Mr. Vane,” Thorne replied, stepping aside. “Please, come in.”

As the driver stepped into the warm, gas-lit room, I observed that he wiped his feet obsessively on the mat, a gesture of profound anxiety rather than mere cleanliness. He was a large man, broad in the shoulders, with a jaw that looked as if it had been chiselled from granite. His eyes, however, were pale and watery, holding the terrified look of a beast cornered.

“I have come to confess,” Vane said, before I had even gestured to a chair. “Or rather, I have come to be told what I have done. Inspector Halloway of the Yard says I am a murderer. He says I took a gentleman to Blackheath and that the gentleman was found dead in a room in Whitechapel at the same hour. It is a lie, sir, but I cannot prove it is not the truth, and that is what kills me.”

Thorne turned to me, his face pale but composed. “Watson, you must hear this. The police have already seized his cab. They believe the man he carried is the victim. But Vane swears he set the passenger down at the correct address in Blackheath, and that the passenger was alive and well when he left him.”

I looked at the driver. “You are certain of the time, Mr. Vane?”

“Precise to the minute, sir,” Vane said, his voice trembling. “It was half-past ten in the evening. The clock at St. Mary’s in Paddington had just struck. I set him down at the iron gate of 42 Grove Street. He had a heavy coat, a brown one, and he carried a valise. He paid me two shillings and a penny. I heard his footsteps on the gravel as he walked up the drive.”

“Why do you think the police suspect you?” Thorne asked.

“Because the dead man was found in a flat in Whitechapel at eleven o’clock. The police say the journey takes no more than forty minutes. If I had taken him to Whitechapel, I would have arrived at ten-thirty. But I took him to Blackheath. And I am the only one who can prove where I went. But who will believe a cabman’s word?”

I nodded slowly. “The discrepancy in location is the crux of the matter. A man cannot be in two places at once. If the victim was in Whitechapel, you either lied about your route, or you lied about your passenger, or—”

“Or the victim is not the man I carried,” Vane finished, a spark of hope igniting in his pale eyes. “But how can I show that?”

“Show us the cab,” I said. “If the police have it, we must go to it. The cab is a witness, Mr. Vane. It has wheels, it has a floor, and it has a smell. And like all witnesses, it does not lie, though it may be misunderstood.”

We hailed a hansom and drove out into the thickening fog. The police station on Victoria Street was a squat, imposing building of red brick. We were ushered into a small, dimly lit room where Inspector Halloway, a man of thin, nervous aspect, was examining a document. He looked up as we entered, his eyes narrowing.

“Mr. Thorne,” he said, his voice dry. “And you brought the suspect with you. How convenient.”

“We brought the suspect to prove his innocence, Inspector,” Thorne replied smoothly. “We wish to examine the vehicle. We believe the details of the interior may hold the key to the alibi.”

Halloway sighed, the sound of a man whose patience was wearing thin. “The cab is in the yard. You may look, but do not touch anything. If you disturb the evidence, I shall have you arrested for obstruction.”

We followed him out into the yard. The night air was biting, and the fog had grown so thick that the gas lamps looked like blurred haloes in the mist. The cab in question was a black, four-wheeled vehicle, its paint chipped in places, its brass fittings dull. It stood silent and imposing, a tomb of wood and iron.

I approached the cab with a magnifying glass in one hand and my notebook in the other. Thorne stood by the Inspector’s side, arms crossed, watching me with an expression of mild curiosity. Vane, however, stood rigid, his eyes fixed on the vehicle with the intensity of a man staring at a ghost.

I began with the exterior. The mud on the wheels was a dark, heavy clay, typical of the roads in the eastern suburbs, but I noted that it was mixed with a lighter, sandy silt. I scraped a small sample onto a piece of paper.

“Look at the wheels, Mr. Vane,” I said, not turning my head. “Do you recognize the mud?”

Vane nodded vigorously. “Yes, sir. The roads near the river in Whitechapel are sandy. But in Blackheath, it is clay. I went to Blackheath.”

“Indeed?” I said, my tone neutral. “And yet, the silt suggests otherwise. But let us look inside. The interior will tell us more than the exterior.”

I lifted the heavy canvas top and peered into the passenger compartment. The seats were upholstered in dark green velvet, worn at the edges. The floor was covered in a layer of dust and debris. I knelt down, my knees pressing into the cold metal of the step, and began to examine the floorboards.

There, near the right-hand corner, was a single, distinct impression. It was not a boot print, but the mark of a heel. It was small, narrow, and deeply indented into the dust. Beside it, scattered in a trail that led from the door to the rear of the cab, were fragments of a peculiar substance. It was not ash, nor was it paper. It was a fine, yellowish powder, with a faint, cloying scent that hung in the air.

I picked up a pinch of the powder with tweezers and held it to the gaslight. It was dry, granular, and uniform in size.

“Mr. Vane,” I said, standing up and brushing the dust from my trousers. “What does your passenger smell like? Or rather, what do you smell when you take a passenger who has been smoking or eating?”

Vane frowned. “It depends, sir. Some men smell of cigars, others of tobacco. Some have the scent of alcohol on their breath.”

“And this?” I held up the pinch of powder. “What is this?”

Vane’s face drained of color. “I… I do not know, sir.”

“It is mustard,” I said. “Dried mustard, mixed with a bit of salt. And there is a trace of onion. This is the residue of a sandwich, or perhaps a sauce, that has been dropped. Now, look at the heel mark. It is small. A man’s heel? No. It is too narrow. It is the heel of a woman’s shoe. A high-heeled shoe, likely made of leather, as the impression is deep and clean.”

Thorne stepped forward, his eyes widening. “A woman? But the victim is a man.”

“Precisely,” I said. “The police assume the victim is the man Mr. Vane claims to have dropped. But what if Mr. Vane did not drop a man? What if he dropped a woman? And what if the man who was found dead in Whitechapel is not the passenger at all, but a different person entirely?”

I turned to Vane. “Did you carry a woman in this cab tonight, Mr. Vane?”

Vane shook his head frantically. “No, sir! I carried a man! A man in a brown coat!”

“Were you certain it was a man?” I asked. “From the voice? From the height?”

“It was a deep voice,” Vane said, his confidence wavering. “And he was tall.”

“Could a woman disguise her voice? Could a woman stand tall in a cab?”

Vane looked at me, then at Thorne, then at the cab. The fog outside seemed to press in on the room, thick and suffocating.

“I… I did not look at the face,” Vane whispered. “The fog was heavy. I heard the voice. I saw the coat. I thought it was a man. But if… if it was a woman…”

“Then you have a witness,” Thorne said, his voice sharp. “A woman who was in the cab. A woman who smelled of mustard and onions. And a woman whose heel left a mark on the floor. If she is alive, she can tell us where she went. And if she is dead, then we have a different murder to solve.”

I looked at the Inspector, who was watching us with a mixture of suspicion and dawning interest. “Inspector Halloway,” I said. “You are looking for a man. But perhaps you should be looking for a woman. A woman who traveled from this cab to Whitechapel. Or perhaps, she traveled to Blackheath, and someone else went to Whitechapel in her place.”

The Inspector frowned. “This is speculation, Mr. Watson.”

“It is deduction, Inspector,” I corrected. “And it is based on the evidence in front of us. The mud on the wheels is a mix. The floor has a woman’s heel mark. The passenger was not who the driver thought he was. The police have the cab, but they do not have the truth. They have only the assumption.”

Vane looked at us, his eyes wide. “What must I do?”

“Wait,” I said. “We will find the woman. And when we do, we will know if you are a liar, or a victim of a far more complex plot than you ever imagined.”

We left the yard, the fog swallowing us as we stepped out into the night. The streets of London were silent, save for the distant rumble of a carriage. I looked back at the cab, standing in the yard, a silent witness to a night that had been far stranger than any of us had suspected. The case was not solved, but the direction had changed. And in the art of detection, a change of direction is often the first step toward the truth.
