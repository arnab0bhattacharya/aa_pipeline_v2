The pool in the garden of the Summer Palace at Lirien was a perfect, dark mirror, and in its center lived a water lily named Elara. She was not the largest blossom, nor the most fragrant, but she was the most attentive. While the other lilies floated in drowsy contentment, their roots tangled in the cool mud, Elara spent her days watching the sky. She had been born, as all lilies are, in the soft ooze at the bottom of the world, and had risen through the green-tinted water with a single, unarticulated purpose: to reach the light. But the light she found was not the harsh, demanding sun of midday, which baked the stones of the palace and made the air shimmer. It was the moon.

She first saw it on a night of perfect stillness, when the garden was asleep and the only sound was the gentle sigh of the wind through the willows. The moon, full and luminous, rose over the tiled roofline of the palace and cast its long, silver path across the water, a road of light that ended just at the edge of her floating pad. Elara felt a tremor run through her stem, a sensation entirely new. It was not the warmth of the sun, which was a command to grow, to open, to be seen. The moon’s light was a caress, a secret shared. It was cool and remote, a perfection of form and light that stole the breath she did not know she had. In that moment, she fell in love.

Her love was a quiet, desperate ritual. Every evening, as the first stars pricked the velvet of the sky, she would begin to unfurl. She would push her petals outward, one by one, a slow and deliberate blossoming, until her pale heart was laid bare to the night. She offered her faint, sweet perfume to the silver light, a gift borne on the water’s surface. She would hold that pose for hours, a pale supplicant on the dark altar of the pool, drinking in the moon’s reflection, feeling a kinship with that perfect, distant orb. The moon was a captive too, she reasoned, tethered to the sky just as she was tethered to the mud. They were both prisoners of their own spheres, and their only communion was this silent, nightly exchange of light and longing.

But the pool was not a silent, empty temple. It was a small, bustling world, and its inhabitants had little patience for her reverie. The frogs were the first to notice. They were a boisterous, practical clan, led by an old, warty bullfrog named Gromm. They would gather on the lily pads, their slick bodies gleaming in the moonlight, and watch Elara’s silent adoration.

“Look at her,” Gromm would croak, his voice a low, percussive thrum. “Stretching herself thin for a face in the sky. What good is a moon, I ask you? Can you eat it? Can you sit on it? No. It’s a cold, dead stone, and she’s a bigger fool than the fly who thinks the lantern is the sun.”

The other frogs would chuckle, a chorus of guttural, wet sounds. “Perhaps she thinks if she opens wide enough, the moon will fall right into her petals,” one of them would jest, and they would all laugh, a harsh, mocking noise that rippled across the water. Elara would tremble, but she would not close her petals. To do so would be to admit they were right, to betray the sacred moment of her blossoming. She would simply turn her face a little more toward the silver path, trying to shut out their voices.

The goldfish were more subtle, but their cruelty was sharper. They were the aristocrats of the pool, sleek and shimmering in their orange and white livery. They would swim in lazy, elegant circles around her stem, their mouths opening and closing in silent, perpetual judgment. A particularly proud fish, with scales like beaten copper and a name she did not know, paused one evening to address her.

“My dear,” he said, his voice a bubble of refined condescension. “This is becoming quite the spectacle. We all admire a certain… romanticism, of course. But one must know one’s place. You are a rooted thing, a creature of the mud and the shallows. The moon is a celestial body, a world away. It is not for us. We goldfish know this. We content ourselves with the treasures of our own realm—the bright pebbles, the cool shade of the lotus leaves, the occasional crumb from the Empress’s table. That is a sensible life. This… this is a delusion. You are making yourself a laughingstock. The frogs, at least, are honest in their mockery. We are merely… concerned.”

His words, spoken with such calm certainty, were like a cold stone dropped into the still water of her heart. He had named her foolishness, not with a jeer, but with pity. She felt a profound shame. Was her love truly so absurd? Was she, a simple flower, reaching for something that was never meant to be hers? That night, for the first time, her petals trembled as she opened them, and her perfume seemed to carry a note of sorrow.

Her days became a penance. As the sun rose, its golden light feeling coarse and vulgar after the moon’s refined silver, she would slowly, reluctantly, fold her petals back in. It was a closing, a retreat. The world by day was loud and demanding. The palace woke, the gardeners tended the grounds, the sun beat down, and she was just another lily on a pond. She would sink into the green, murky water, her heart heavy with the memory of the night and the long, empty hours until dusk. She felt the shame of the goldfish’s words and the sting of the frogs’ laughter. She was a creature divided, living for a light that was not her own, and suffering for it in the light that was.

One night, a storm rolled in from the mountains. The wind howled, whipping the willows into a frenzy, and the placid surface of the pool was shattered into a thousand chaotic waves. The frogs took shelter under the broadest leaves, and the goldfish huddled in the deepest crevices of the pool’s stone bottom. The moon was completely obscured by a thick blanket of cloud. The world was black and terrifying.

Elara was battered by the waves. Her stem bent and strained, and for the first time, she was tempted to close her petals against the fury, to protect her delicate heart. But she could not. She felt that if she did, she would lose the memory of the moon forever. So she held herself open to the storm, a tiny, defiant vessel of white in the churning darkness. She offered her perfume to the wind, a prayer into the void. She was not looking for the moon’s reflection now; she was looking for its memory, for the belief that it was still there, somewhere behind the clouds.

The storm raged, and a great gust of wind tore at her. One of her outer petals was ripped away, carried off into the blackness. She cried out, a silent, leafy cry of pain and loss. But she did not close. She endured.

And then, as suddenly as it had come, the storm passed. The clouds broke, and the moon, full and brilliant, emerged. Its light was not a gentle caress now, but a triumphant blaze. It flooded the garden, illuminating the dripping leaves and the trembling surface of the pool. Elara, bruised and battered, with one petal missing, felt the light hit her directly. It was as if the moon had seen her struggle and was rewarding her constancy. In that moment, the frogs’ mockery and the goldfish’s pity meant nothing. She was not a fool. She was a lover who had weathered the storm.

The moon did not speak to her, nor did it ever descend to meet her. It remained distant, silent, and perfect. But Elara understood, in the quiet aftermath of the storm, that her love was not a transaction. It was not about receiving anything in return. Her love was in the opening, the offering, the unwavering gaze. It was a choice she made every evening. The moon was her north star, the silent witness to her devotion, and in loving it, she had found a strength and a beauty within herself that the mud and the water could never have given her. And so, night after night, she continued to open her petals, a small, pale flame of devotion in the heart of the moonlit garden.
