The first sensation was a betrayal of the flesh, a strange, wet warmth that bloomed behind Kaelen’s eyes. He was in the Harmony Plaza of Aethelgard, the great white terraces gleaming under the morning sun, and the feeling was so alien he stopped mid-stride. He touched his forehead. It was damp. The air, filtered and perfumed with the scent of engineered jasmine, felt suddenly thick, heavy. He had a strange ache in his bones, a low thrum of discomfort he could not name. He had read about such things in the historical archives—fictionalised accounts of the ‘Sickness Era’—but the words were just ink, abstract concepts of a brutal, forgotten past. Now, the abstraction was a hot, living thing inside him.

He tried to continue his walk, but his legs felt unsteady. A young woman, Lyra, smiled at him as she passed. Her smile faltered. “Kaelen? Are you well? Your colour is… unusual.” The word ‘well’ was more than a greeting in Aethelgard; it was a statement of fact, a confirmation of the universal state of being. To not be well was a grammatical impossibility. Kaelen opened his mouth to reassure her, but what came out was a dry, rasping cough. The sound was obscene in the serene plaza. It was a jagged, ugly noise from a world of noise and disease and death. People stopped. Heads turned. The curated silence of their perfect world was shattered by the sound of a body failing.

Panic, a forgotten emotion, began to ripple through the crowd. They did not move towards him to help; they moved away. A space cleared around him, a perfect circle of fear. An older man, a Guardian named Valerius, stepped forward. His face, usually a mask of placid authority, was tight with alarm. “Kaelen. What is this?” he asked, his voice strained. Kaelen tried to explain the heat, the ache, the strange weakness, but the words were clumsy. He felt another wave of heat roll through him and stumbled, catching himself on a polished marble bench. The contact felt shocking, the cool stone a temporary relief to his burning skin.

“You must be taken to the Synod,” Valerius declared, not to Kaelen, but to the horrified onlookers. “Do not touch him. Maintain distance.” They treated him as if he were a volatile chemical, a source of radiation. Two Guardians, their movements stiff with revulsion, produced a containment field—a shimmering, transparent cube that hummed with energy. They guided it over Kaelen, enclosing him without making contact. The world outside became distorted, wavy. He could see their faces, their wide eyes and fearful mouths, but he was suddenly separate, an exhibit. He was no longer Kaelen, the quiet archivist who loved the scent of old data-crystals. He was the Anomaly. He was the Threat.

The journey to the Synod Chamber was silent. The floating containment cube glided through the pristine corridors of Aethelgard, and people pressed themselves against the walls to let it pass. Kaelen watched their reflections in the polished chrome, their faces pinched with a primal dread they had only ever read about. He was a ghost from their past, a spectre of mortality come to haunt their immortal garden. Inside the cube, the air grew warm and stale. His cough came again, a violent, rattling thing that made his whole body shake. He felt a wetness on his upper lip and touched it. His fingers came away stained with crimson. Blood. The word itself was archaic, a thing of butchery and history. Now it was a bright, terrifying smear on his fingertip.

The Synod Chamber was a vast, circular room, its domed ceiling a projection of a clear, star-filled sky. The elders of Aethelgard sat in a high, curving dais, their faces illuminated by the soft, ethereal light. They were the keepers of the Long Peace, the stewards of a century of perfect health. They looked down at him not with compassion, but with the clinical curiosity of entomologists examining a monstrous new insect.

“Subject Kaelen of the Archival Guild,” one of them, a woman named Elara, began. Her voice was calm, but it held an undercurrent of steel. “You present with a systemic biological deviation. Elevated temperature, respiratory distress, fluid expulsion… this is a condition for which we have no protocol.”

“I am… sick,” Kaelen rasped, the word feeling foreign and heavy in his mouth. It was a word from the old texts, a word that should have been extinct.

The word sent a visible shudder through the assembly. “Sickness,” Elara repeated, tasting the word as if it were poison. “A relic. The concept is known only through the historical record of the Great Correction. When the nanites purged the last pathogens from the human genome, such afflictions were rendered obsolete.”

“I need help,” Kaelen pleaded. “A healer. Medicine.” He was grasping at ghosts, words he’d found in the forbidden digital archives he was tasked with cataloguing. The Synod stared back, uncomprehending. The concepts were gone. There were no doctors because there was no disease. There were no hospitals because there was no injury or illness. Humanity had engineered its own salvation and, in doing so, had amputated its own memory of suffering.

“There is no such thing as a ‘healer’,” said a stern man named Marcus. “The body is a self-correcting system. Your system is evidently… flawed. Corrupted.”

The accusation was worse than the fear. He was not just a threat; he was a failure. A mistake. A glitch in the perfect code of their existence. He felt a profound, crushing loneliness. He was a species of one. “What will you do with me?” he whispered.

A long silence followed. The elders conferred in low, urgent tones. Finally, Elara turned back to him, her expression unreadable. “The primary directive is the preservation of societal integrity. You represent an unknown variable. A potential contagion. While we have no historical precedent for treatment, we have absolute precedent for containment.”

They would not kill him. Their society was too sterile for that. But they would not help him, either. He was to be an object of study, a problem to be managed. He was taken from the Synod and moved to the Reclamation Sector, a place where flawed bio-matter and malfunctioning technology were disassembled. His new home was a sterile white room with a single observation window. He was given nutrient paste and water, delivered by an automated arm. He was alone with the fire in his bones and the strange, rattling sound in his chest.

Days bled into one another. He was a specimen. Scientists in clean suits would watch him from behind the glass, taking readings, their faces blank. They never spoke to him. He was no longer a person to them. He was simply the Sickness. He would press his hand against the cool plasteel of the window and watch the people of Aethelgard walk by in the corridor beyond, their lives of placid, painless perfection continuing without him. He saw Lyra once. She was with a young man, laughing at something. She glanced towards the observation window, and for a fleeting second, her eyes met his. There was no recognition, only a flicker of the old fear before she looked away and hurried on. In that moment, Kaelen understood. He hadn't just lost his health; he had been erased from the human race. He was the ghost at the feast of immortality, the one reminder that their perfect world was built upon a foundation of forgotten graves. And as the fever raged on, he wondered if he was the first to fall ill, or simply the first one they couldn't ignore.
