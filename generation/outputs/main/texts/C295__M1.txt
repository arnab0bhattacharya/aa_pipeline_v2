The air in the drawing-room of the Grosvenor Square house was thick with the ghosts of old cigars and the scent of dried lavender. It was a room designed for conversation, but now it only held the silence between two men: Sir Arthur Finch, who had negotiated treaties in St. Petersburg and survived coups in Constantinople, and his great-nephew, Leo, who was attempting to negotiate his first season in society and was terrified of being pronounced a bore.

Sir Arthur, a relic in a velvet smoking jacket, swirled the amber liquid in his glass. His face was a roadmap of cynicism, his eyes holding the weary amusement of a man who had seen all the world’s tricks and found them wanting. Leo, all earnestness and well-bred anxiety, sat opposite him, a half-eaten slice of seed cake forgotten on the plate beside him.

“Uncle Arthur,” Leo began, his voice tight with worry, “I am to dine at the Marchioness of Claverham’s on Thursday. It is the most important supper of the season. Everyone will be there. I am… concerned.”

Sir Arthur grunted, a sound like gravel shifting. “Concerned? Good. Worry is the hallmark of the uninteresting man. It shows you have nothing of consequence to offer except your own fretfulness.”

Leo flushed. “But how should I behave? What should I say? I want to make a good impression.”

The old diplomat leaned forward, his eyes glinting with a sudden, malicious spark. The game was afoot. “A good impression? Utterly bourgeois. A peacock makes a good impression. A well-polished boot makes a good impression. You, my boy, must aim for something far more sophisticated. You must aim to be a magnificent failure.”

“A failure?” Leo looked aghast.

“Precisely,” Sir Arthur said, warming to his theme. “Success is so dreadfully common. Everyone is scrabbling for it, clawing and scraping. But to fail, and to fail with panache, with style? That is a rare art. Now, listen closely. I shall give you the rules.”

Leo, his brow furrowed in concentration, nodded eagerly.

“First,” the old man began, holding up a gnarled finger, “when you are introduced to a woman of importance, you must not compliment her. Compliments are the currency of sycophants. Instead, you must offer a slight, but elegant, critique. If she has a magnificent ruby necklace, you should say something like, ‘My dear Lady, what a brave choice of adornment. It reminds me of the gaudy baubles one sees in the souks of Constantinople.’ You see? It is memorable.”

Leo’s eyes widened. “But… won’t she be offended?”

“Offended? My dear boy, a woman of true quality is never offended by a display of superior taste. She will be intrigued. She will think, ‘Here is a man who sees beyond the glitter.’ She will remember you for a week.”

Sir Arthur took a slow, deliberate sip of his brandy. “Second, the art of conversation. When asked your opinion on a matter of substance – politics, art, literature – you must be unequivocally and inexplicably wrong. If asked about the new Balfour bill, you should declare that its true genius lies in its potential to destabilise the price of Welsh slate. If asked about the latest play at the Lyceum, you should opine that the lead actress’s performance was, and I quote, ‘a masterclass in the subtle art of being a hat-stand.’ Do not explain yourself. Let the statement hang in the air, a jewel of absurdity.”

Leo looked as though he might faint. “Uncle, that is… madness.”

“Is it? Or is it the only sane response to a world that takes itself so seriously? It is the last resort of the truly intelligent man, to mock the very game he is forced to play. It will separate you from the herd of bleating mediocrities who all agree with one another.”

He paused, letting the silence stretch. “And third, and most important of all. If a lady of rank and fortune appears to be showing you a particular… kindness, a flicker of interest, you must flee. Run from it as if it were the plague. Compliment her rival. Ask her for advice on how to court someone else. Show a profound and abiding disinterest. Women, like all predators, are drawn to the chase. Give them no quarry, and they will find you irresistible.”

Sir Arthur leaned back, a faint, triumphant smile on his lips. He had painted a portrait of social suicide so vivid and so complete that the boy would surely see it for the joke it was. He had given him the key to the kingdom, written in a language he could not possibly understand. Leo, however, was not laughing. He was staring into the middle distance, his lips moving silently, committing the absurd catechism to memory. Sir Arthur felt a flicker of unease.

The following week, the old man was taking his constitutional in the park when he saw the Marchioness of Claverham herself, a formidable woman built like a galleon in full sail, deep in conversation with the Duchess of Wiltshire. The Marchioness spotted him and, to his astonishment, bore down upon him with a radiant smile.

“Sir Arthur! I was just speaking of your remarkable great-nephew.”

Sir Arthur braced himself for the torrent of complaint. “Was he a dreadful boor? I apologise profusely. The boy is an imbecile.”

“An imbecile?” The Marchioness laughed, a sound like clinking crystal. “Good heavens, no! He is the most refreshingly original young man I have met in twenty years. So few people have the courage to speak their mind.”

The old diplomat could only stare.

“When he told me my sapphires were ‘a rather pedestrian choice, evocative of a cloudy Tuesday in Scarborough,’ I nearly swooned with delight! So perceptive! And his conversation! Everyone is still talking about his brilliant, utterly unique take on the new Ibsen play at the Vaudeville. He said the entire production suffered from a ‘surfeit of chairs.’ The critics are still trying to decipher it. It’s been in all the papers!”

Sir Arthur felt the ground shift beneath his feet. It was impossible. It was a grotesque parody of his own advice, and yet it was working.

“Best of all,” the Marchioness continued, lowering her voice conspiratorially, “is his courtship of Lady Beatrice.”

“Courtship?” Sir Arthur croaked. “I thought he was to be told to avoid her.”

“Oh, he has avoided her magnificently! He spent the entire Claverham supper asking me for advice on how to woo her, all while pretending she wasn’t in the room. He then sent her a poem comparing her eyes to ‘two unremarkable puddles of rainwater.’ The poor girl is utterly besotted! She says he is the only man in London who is not a fawning fool. They say he is to propose at the Ranelagh ball tonight.”

Sir Arthur Finch walked home in a daze, his mind reeling. He had handed the boy a loaded pistol, expecting him to shoot himself in the foot, and instead, the boy had shot a silver arrow through the heart of the entire London season. He had taken the old man’s most cynical, world-weary pronouncements and, with the literal-minded innocence of youth, turned them into a successful strategy. He had created a monster, a social titan, and had no idea how it had happened.

That evening, Leo came to see him, his face glowing with a strange and unfamiliar confidence. “Uncle Arthur,” he said, his voice ringing with sincerity, “I cannot thank you enough. Your advice was… perfect.”

Sir Arthur could only stare at him, a profound and terrible confusion in his eyes.

Leo beamed. “You were right about everything. Lady Beatrice… she said yes. She said I was the most honest and forthright man she had ever met.”

The old diplomat sank into his leather armchair, the brandy glass trembling in his hand. He looked at his great-nephew, this cheerful, successful idiot who had conquered the world by being told to run from it. He had spent a lifetime learning to say one thing and mean another, to navigate the treacherous currents of human vanity with lies and flattery. And in the end, the truth, or at least a version of it so absurd it could only be taken as truth, had been the most powerful weapon of all.

“My boy,” Sir Arthur said, his voice a dry rasp. He raised his glass in a toast. “You are a credit to the family. And you have taught an old fool a valuable lesson.”

“Uncle, you are too kind.”

“No,” Sir Arthur said, a slow, genuine smile finally touching his lips. “On this occasion, I mean it quite literally.”
