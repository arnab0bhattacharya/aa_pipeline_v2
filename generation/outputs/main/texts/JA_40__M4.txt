The introduction was made at Mrs. Ashby’s concert, and it was generally allowed to be a very proper one. Mr. Ellerton, of Ellerton Place in Surrey, was a gentleman of unexceptionable appearance and a fortune which, if not immense, was at least sufficient to be mentioned in the same breath with the word “establishment.” He had been in town some weeks, was known to be a man of taste and of a certain reserve, and had the good fortune to be patronised by Lady Ashby herself, who declared that he was the only man in the room who understood the value of silence. To Miss Catherine Pemberton, who was in London for the season under the protection of her aunt, Mrs. Delamere, he was therefore an object of no small interest; for a young lady of two-and-twenty, with a good understanding and no fortune, is not apt to be indifferent to the attentions of a gentleman so circumstanced.

He was presented, he bowed, he seated himself by her side, and for the space of half an hour he acquitted himself with so much propriety that Catherine began to think she had never met with a man whose manners were more exactly to her taste. He spoke of the music with discrimination, of the room with good-humoured contempt, and of the weather with that happy mixture of philosophy and resignation which bespeaks a mind superior to the common vexations of life. She was in the midst of a very agreeable reply, when she chanced to mention her brother’s name.

“Mr. Pemberton of the —th,” said she, with the natural pride of a sister, “is lately returned from the Peninsula, and is now at Bath with my mother.”

Mr. Ellerton’s countenance underwent a change so slight that a less observant person might have missed it. His smile remained, but it had lost its ease; his eyes, which had before rested on her with a pleasant openness, now seemed to withdraw themselves, as if some object had interposed between them.

“Indeed,” said he, after a pause of a moment. “I have not the honour of knowing your brother.”

“He is not known to many,” said Catherine, “for he has been abroad these five years; but he is very generally beloved by those who do know him.”

“I doubt it not,” replied Mr. Ellerton, with a civility that was somehow cold. “A soldier’s life is not favourable to the cultivation of general acquaintance, but it is very favourable, I believe, to the formation of particular friendships.”

He said no more upon the subject, and Catherine, though she felt that something had been said amiss, could not for her life discover what it was. The conversation flagged; Mr. Ellerton looked at his watch, declared that he had promised to meet a friend at White’s, and took his leave with a bow which was perfectly correct and perfectly unsatisfactory.

Catherine was not a young lady to be easily discomposed, but she was discomposed. She said nothing to her aunt, for Mrs. Delamere was one of those good women who are always ready to supply a reason for everything, and whose reasons are invariably of the most comfortable kind. “Mr. Ellerton is a man of reserve,” she would have said; “he is not to be won in a day.” But Catherine had a keener eye for the variations of manner, and she was persuaded that she had seen something more than reserve.

The next day brought an explanation, though not from the quarter she expected. She was walking in the Park with her aunt, when she perceived Mr. Ellerton at a little distance, in company with a gentleman of a very different appearance—a stout, red-faced man, with a loud voice and a hearty laugh, who was talking with great animation, and whom Mr. Ellerton seemed to be listening to with an air of patient endurance. As they approached, the stout gentleman turned, caught sight of Catherine, and to her great surprise, exclaimed:

“Why, it is Miss Pemberton!—Ned Pemberton’s sister, by all that is wonderful!—You do not remember me, ma’am, but I remember you when you were a little girl in a white frock. I am General Ashford, an old friend of your father’s.”

Catherine, who had a vague recollection of a loud-voiced man who had once dined at her father’s table and upset a decanter of port over her mother’s best gown, acknowledged the acquaintance with a good grace. Mr. Ellerton, thus forced into a meeting, was obliged to stop and speak, but his manner was constrained, and he seemed to be making a great effort to appear at his ease. General Ashford, on the other hand, was all cordiality.

“I have not seen Ned these five years,” said he, “not since that unfortunate affair at ——. By George, I thought the two of you would have had it out then, Ellerton. But Ned was always a hot-headed fellow, and you were always a cool one, and so it ended as such things do—with a great deal of talk and no bloodshed. I am glad to see you are friends again.”

“General Ashford,” said Mr. Ellerton, in a voice of ice, “I have not the honour of Mr. Pemberton’s friendship.”

“Not! Why, I thought the whole thing had blown over. It was about that horse, was it not?—the bay mare that Ned swore was his, and you swore was yours, and the lawyers could make nothing of it. I remember it well. It was a foolish business, and I told Ned so at the time. ‘Ned,’ said I, ‘the mare is not worth a quarrel with a man you have known all your life.’ But he would not listen to me. He never does.”

Catherine felt the blood rush to her cheeks. She had never heard of the horse; she had never heard of any quarrel; but she had heard enough to know that her brother had been in the wrong, or at least that he had been intemperate, and that Mr. Ellerton had been injured. She looked at him involuntarily, and met a glance which seemed to say, “You see how it is; I cannot explain myself, but you understand me.”

“I am sorry,” said she, with a frankness that was natural to her, “that my brother should have given you cause to think ill of him. I am sure he would be the first to regret it, if he knew that I had been so unfortunate as to pain you by my inadvertence.”

Mr. Ellerton was not prepared for this. He had expected embarrassment, perhaps apology, perhaps a defence of her brother; he had not expected the simple candour of a woman who was not ashamed to acknowledge a fault which was not her own. His countenance softened; he bowed, and said, with a warmth that surprised even himself:

“You have pained me, madam, only by making me feel that I have been unjust. I ought not to have allowed the remembrance of an old grievance to affect my behaviour to a lady who could have no part in it. I was wrong, and I beg your pardon.”

General Ashford, who had been listening with a countenance of profound astonishment, now burst into a laugh.

“Well done, well done! I always said you were a cool one, Ellerton, but I never knew you were so ready to cry peccavi. And you, Miss Pemberton, have done more in five minutes than the lawyers could do in five years. I will write to Ned tonight, and tell him that his sister has made peace for him.”

Catherine, who was not insensible to the pleasure of having done a good-natured thing, and who was not without a hope that Mr. Ellerton’s esteem might be worth the having, smiled, and said that she should be very glad if any word of hers could be of service; and the party separated with a civility on all sides which promised well for the future.

Mrs. Delamere, who had been standing at a little distance, and who had heard nothing but the sound of voices, now came up to her niece with an air of complacent curiosity.

“My dear Catherine,” said she, “what have you been saying to Mr. Ellerton? He looks quite animated. I declare I never saw him so agreeable.”

“I have been making an apology for my brother, ma’am,” said Catherine, “and he has been making one for himself.”

“An apology! For what?”

“For a quarrel, ma’am, which I never heard of, and which I hope I shall never hear of again.”

Mrs. Delamere, who was not fond of mysteries, would have pressed the point; but Catherine was not disposed to gratify her. She walked on in silence, meditating on the strange chance which had brought her brother’s enemy into her path, and on the still stranger chance which had made her the means of reconciling him. She thought of Mr. Ellerton’s look when he had said, “I have been unjust”; she thought of the warmth of his bow at parting; and she began to think that a man who could acknowledge a fault so handsomely was not a man to be despised.

Whether Mr. Ellerton thought the same of a woman who could apologize so gracefully, it is not for me to say. But it is certain that he called at Mrs. Delamere’s the very next morning, and that he called again the morning after, and that before the season was over, the name of Pemberton was mentioned in a connection which would have greatly astonished General Ashford, and which, it is to be hoped, would have pleased even the hot-headed Ned himself.
