It was in the spring of nineteen-oh-something that the sun, which had sustained British agriculture for so many centuries with a fair degree of reliability, began to shirk its duties. The newspapers, which are never so happy as when they are reassuring the public of its own prosperity, explained the phenomenon away as a temporary atmospheric disturbance. The *Daily Telegraph* attributed it to volcanic dust in the upper strata, while the *Standard* rather leaned toward an excess of marsh gas from the Fenlands. The government in London issued a white paper. It was a very handsome document, bound in blue, and it proved beyond a shadow of a doubt that the abnormal weather was merely a transient meteorological fluctuation, and that the British public might expect a return to normal conditions by midsummer.

Midsummer arrived, however, and brought no heat. The wheat in the Weald of Kent stood thin and yellow in the fields, looking more like a sickly crop of whiskers than a staple food. The apples in the orchards were hard and green, and the grass had given up the ghost entirely. It was against this rather disheartening backdrop that Mr. Samuel Gurd, a farmer of some considerable acreage near the village of Biddenden, found himself moved to practical dissent.

Samuel Gurd was a short, thick-set man, with a deeply lined face and a profound, constitutional distrust of anything originating in London. He had read the white paper at the reading-room of the local institute, and had returned home in a state of grim irritation.

“If it’s a transient meteorological whatsit,” he remarked to his wife over a supper of stale bread and very weak tea, “then I’m a Dutchman. The sun’s gone wrong, and the government hasn’t the foggiest notion what to do about it. So we shall have to do it ourselves.”

“Do what, exactly?” asked Mrs. Gurd, who was a woman of a nervously practical disposition.

“Grow things under glass,” said Gurd, banging his fist on the table to emphasize the radical nature of the proposition. “If the sun won’t come to the crop, we must trap what little heat there is and make the crop grow regardless. We shall cover the lower acres with glass.”

It was a scheme that sounded, to the ordinary agricultural mind of the period, quite preposterous. Glass was for gentlemen’s conservatories and for the cultivation of orchids; it was not a material for the serious business of feeding a county. But Gurd, who possessed a mind that leapt readily from observation to speculation, had calculated the matter out. The sun was dimmer, certainly, but it was not entirely extinguished. If one could construct a vast, sloping roof of glass over a few acres of decent soil, the trapped radiant heat would be sufficient to force the growth of potatoes and cabbages, and perhaps even some hardy strains of wheat.

He began by calling a meeting at the village hall. The attendance was remarkably good, driven less by respect for Gurd’s scientific theories than by the sheer, creeping panic of a population facing a winter without a harvest. The hall was lit by a few smoky oil lamps, and the air was thick with the smell of damp wool and anxiety.

Gurd stood at the small wooden platform and laid out his case with the direct, energetic sentences of a man who has no patience for conventional despair. He told them the government was lying, or at the very least, hoping. He told them the sun was dying by fractions, and that they would all starve if they waited for London to solve the problem. Then he proposed his glass scheme.

There was a murmur of discontent from the back of the room. Old Mr. Phipps, who owned the largest orchard in the district and had the most to lose, stood up to protest.

“It’s unnatural,” Phipps declared, his voice trembling with a mixture of anger and genuine agricultural horror. “You can’t force the Creator’s hand. If the Lord has seen fit to dim the sun, then it’s not for us to go putting up glass roofs as if we were trying to cheat Him out of a frost.”

“I am not trying to cheat the Lord,” Gurd replied, with weary irony. “I am trying to cheat the government, which is a much more practical and rewarding undertaking. The Lord, I fancy, has other things to worry about than Mr. Phipps’s cabbages. Now, I have three thousand square feet of glass in my sheds, left over from the old hop-drying kilns. I propose we pool our resources, buy up every pane of glass in Ashford and Maidstone, and build a winter garden. We grow food. We survive. We leave the meteorological fluctuations to the politicians in London, who are welcome to them.”

The argument was a lively one, as arguments in village halls generally are. It ranged from the comic absurdity of trying to grow turnips in a greenhouse to the earnest, argumentative idealism of the younger men, who saw in Gurd’s scheme a sort of glorious, defiant revolution against the natural order. In the end, desperation won out over convention. A committee was formed, subscriptions were gathered, and the strange, glass-roofed colony began to take shape in the lower fields of Biddenden.

The work was hard and entirely unprecedented. They stripped the iron frameworks from the disused hop kilns and dragged them down to the valley. They bought up the glass from demolished conservatories and bankrupt nurseries across the county. Gurd, directing the operations with a relentless, practical energy, had them lay down a thick layer of manure beneath the topsoil to generate additional heat through decomposition. It was a messy, smelly business, but it worked. Within a week, they had erected a vast, rambling, somewhat lopsided glasshouse that covered nearly two acres of ground.

Inside, the air was humid and faintly sweet, smelling of earth and warm dung. The temperature was a good ten degrees higher than the freezing, grey world outside. Gurd walked through the rows of freshly planted seedlings, his hands behind his back, looking for all the world like a somewhat grim and dirty captain upon the bridge of a very peculiar ship.

The government, in the meantime, continued to issue its reassurances. The Prime Minister made a statement in the House of Commons, expressing his confidence that the “unseasonable chill” would shortly pass, and urging the public to continue its normal consumption of coal, which was, of course, becoming steadily more expensive. The newspapers printed cheerful editorials about the resilience of the British character in the face of minor climatic inconveniences. In London, people continued to dine at their clubs and attend the theatre, albeit with an extra waistcoat.

But in the Weald, the glass roofs grew. By October, when the first real snows fell, the green shoots inside the glasshouse were already pushing up through the dark soil. The dim, pale sun, filtering through the panes, was caught and held, and the plants stretched toward it with a desperate, eager vitality.

Gurd stood at the door of his glasshouse one November morning, looking out over the white, frozen fields. A government inspector had arrived that day, a young man from the Board of Agriculture, who had come down from London to inquire why the farmers of Biddenden were not submitting their usual statistical returns for the autumn harvest.

The inspector was a thin, rather pale young man, with the kind of spectacles that suggest a profound acquaintance with paperwork and a complete ignorance of the outdoors. He stood in the freezing wind, looking at the vast, glittering structure of glass with a mixture of bewilderment and professional disapproval.

“But Mr. Gurd,” the inspector said, consulting a clipboard, “the official position is that the weather is merely temporary. You are not supposed to be growing potatoes under glass. It is not in the schedule of anticipated agricultural responses.”

“You tell that to the potatoes,” Gurd said, jerking his thumb toward the interior of the glasshouse, where the green leaves were already flourishing in the trapped, artificial warmth. “They don’t seem to care much for the official position.”

The inspector made a note on his clipboard, looking worried. “I shall have to report this to the Board. They will be most displeased. It suggests a lack of confidence in the meteorological office.”

“It suggests a lack of confidence in the sun,” Gurd corrected him, with a grim smile. “And the sun, as you may have noticed, is not taking our schedules into account. Now, are you going to stand there freezing, or are you going to come inside and have a cup of tea? We’ve got a fire going, and the cabbages are coming along nicely.”

The inspector hesitated, shivering in his London-made overcoat. He looked at the frozen, barren fields, and then at the warm, green glow emanating from the glasshouse. The contrast was, he thought, rather suggestive. He went inside, and the door closed behind him, shutting out the cold.
