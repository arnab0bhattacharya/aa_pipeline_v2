The town of Marlow’s End had, in the years before the fever, been a place of cheerful industry, its streets alive with the clatter of looms and the voices of children returning from school. Now a dreadful stillness possessed it; the manufactories stood silent, their windows boarded, and the few souls who ventured abroad moved with the wary, downcast air of those who expect at any moment to be struck down. It was in the third month of the pestilence that I first heard the name of Elijah Crane, and heard it spoken with a reverence that chilled me more than the sight of the fever’s victims had done.

I was a physician then, newly returned from the capital, where I had laboured in the fever wards until my spirit was well-nigh broken by the daily spectacle of suffering. I had held the hands of the dying, I had seen the dreadful blotches spread across the fairest faces, and I had watched the light of reason fade from eyes that had lately sparkled with hope. I had come to Marlow’s End seeking only a season of quiet, a chance to restore my shattered nerves amid the scenes of my childhood; but I found the town in the grip of a terror more profound than any I had witnessed in London, for here the people had abandoned themselves wholly to despair, and to the wild consolations of a fanatic.

Crane was a preacher, though he had never been ordained; a man of middle years, with a gaunt countenance and eyes that burned with an unnatural lustre. He had come to Marlow’s End in the second month of the pestilence, and had begun to preach in the market-square, declaring that the fever was the judgment of Heaven upon a wicked and unrepentant people, and that only those who followed him—who submitted to his rule, who partook of the waters he blessed, who abjured the vain ministrations of physicians—would be spared. The people, maddened by fear, had flocked to him in thousands; they had abandoned their homes, their trades, their dying kindred, to sit at his feet and listen to his ravings. He had established a camp upon the hill beyond the town, where his followers dwelt in tents and huts, and where, it was said, no one had yet sickened or died.

I confess that when I first heard these things my heart swelled with indignation. I had seen the fever’s work too closely to believe that any man could stay its progress by the muttering of prayers or the sprinkling of water. I resolved to go to this impostor, to confront him before his deluded followers, and to expose him as a charlatan who fattened upon the misery of the people. I prepared myself for the encounter as a soldier prepares for battle; I marshalled my arguments, I recalled every fact I had learned concerning the contagion, and I set forth one grey morning for the camp upon the hill.

The path wound upward through a wood of ancient oaks, and as I climbed, the sounds of the town—the tolling of the bell, the cries of the afflicted—grew faint and distant, until I seemed to have entered a region of unearthly quiet. The camp, when I reached it, was not the scene of frenzy I had expected. A great multitude of people were gathered there, but they were silent, and their faces wore an expression of strange tranquillity. They sat in groups upon the grass, some praying, some reading from worn books, and others tending the sick who had come to them from the town below. I observed, with a sensation I could not wholly analyse, that these sick persons were not driven away, as I had supposed they would be, but were received with gentleness and ministered to by the followers of Crane himself.

I found the prophet in a small tent at the centre of the camp. He was seated upon a stool, his hands folded upon his knees, and his eyes fixed upon the ground. He did not raise his head when I entered, but spoke as though he had been expecting me.

“You are the physician from London,” he said. “I have heard of you. You have come to expose me.”

I was disconcerted by his calmness, but I had not come so far to be silenced by a show of composure. “I have come,” I replied, “to ask you by what right you claim to stay the hand of death. You tell these people that your prayers will preserve them; you bid them forsake the remedies of science, and trust to your intercession alone. Do you not know that you are leading them to destruction? Do you not see that the fever spares neither the credulous nor the unbelieving?”

He raised his eyes to mine, and in their depths I saw a sorrow so profound, a compassion so universal, that my accusation died upon my lips. “I know,” he said, “that the fever spares no one. I know that my prayers cannot turn aside the pestilence. I have never told them otherwise.”

“Then why,” I cried, “do you permit them to believe it? Why do you suffer them to abandon their homes and their duties, to gather here in this delusion, when they might be employed in tending the sick, in burying the dead, in preserving the order of the town?”

“Because,” he answered, “they are afraid. They have seen their children die, their parents, their friends; they have seen the plague enter their houses and spare nothing. They are mad with terror, and terror is a disease more deadly than the fever itself. I do not promise them that they shall not die. I promise them only that they shall not die alone. I promise them that they shall not die in despair. I tell them that the pestilence is a trial sent by Heaven, and that those who endure it with faith and courage shall find peace, whether in this world or the next. Is that a lie? Is it a lie to give hope to the hopeless, to bind up the broken-hearted, to make the last hours of the dying a little less terrible?”

I stood before him in silence, and the anger that had burned in my breast was extinguished. I looked upon this man, whom I had come to denounce as a fraud and a deceiver, and I saw that he was neither. He was a fanatic, perhaps; he was deluded in his belief that the pestilence was a divine judgment; but he was sincere, and his sincerity was of a kind that shamed my own shallow confidence. He had not sought to enrich himself, or to gratify his vanity; he had sought only to comfort the afflicted, and to bring a little light into the darkness that had swallowed up the town.

“You are a physician,” he said, after a pause. “You have seen the fever face to face, and you have not fled. Stay with us. Help us to tend the sick. Your skill may save some whom my prayers cannot. And perhaps, in the midst of our labour, you may learn something of the peace that passeth understanding.”

I did not answer him, but I turned and went out of the tent. The sun had risen above the wood, and its light fell upon the multitude that sat upon the hill, and upon the town below, where the bell still tolled for the dead. I stood there for a long time, and I knew not what to think. I had come to expose an impostor, and I had found a saint. I had come to save the people from a delusion, and I had discovered that the delusion was, perhaps, the only thing that kept them from madness. And I, who had prided myself upon my reason, upon my science, upon my clear-eyed vision of the truth, felt suddenly that I was the blindest of them all.
