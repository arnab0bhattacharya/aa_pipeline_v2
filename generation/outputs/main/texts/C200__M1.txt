Elara Vance held that a mind, like a well-kept garden, should be free of weeds. Weeds, to her, were the polite nothings and gentle evasions with which the rest of Ashford Green seemed to suffocate itself. She spoke her thoughts as they came, cleanly and without ornament, and considered this a mark of intellectual honesty. Her father, the town’s apothecary, called it a lack of polish, but Elara saw it as a virtue. The truth, she believed, was a tonic; it might be bitter, but it was always good for the constitution.

The new topic of conversation, a weed in the town’s collective mind if ever there was one, was the marriage of Mr. Arthur Finch to the former Miss Beatrice Corbin. Arthur Finch was a man of some substance, a widower of forty with a fine house and a sensible temperament. Miss Corbin was a creature of twenty, all blushes and fluttering eyelashes, who had arrived in Kent from London with her aunt and a reputation for fragile beauty. The town was agog with the romance of it. Elara was agog with its improbability.

The couple were the subject of all discourse in the haberdashery, the bakery, and the lanes leading to the church. Mrs. Gable, who kept the general store and traded in gossip as her primary currency, was holding forth on the subject as Elara selected a spool of ribbon. “Is it not the most romantic thing, Miss Vance? Such a perfect match. He, so steady, and she, so delicate. A true fairy tale.”

Elara tied the ribbon around her finger, testing its tension. “A fairy tale, Mrs. Gable, is a story of enchantments and talking animals. This is a matter of finance and folly.” She spoke calmly, as one would state a chemical fact. “Mr. Finch is a man of property and practicality. Miss Corbin has the substance of a soap bubble. He will spend his days managing his estate and she will spend hers managing her complexion. What conversation will they share over breakfast? The price of turnips and the merits of lavender water?”

A silence, sharp as a shard of glass, fell over the shop. Mrs. Gable’s smile froze on her face. Mrs. Albright, who had been examining lace, dropped her selection with a soft gasp. Elara, however, felt a flush of satisfaction. She had pruned a particularly ugly weed of sentimentality.

“I merely state the observable facts,” she continued, emboldened. “A man of sense requires a partner of sense. This union seems to me an exercise in vanity on his part and a calculation on hers. It will not end in happiness, but in a quiet, domestic misery. He is not a man to be enchanted by dimples for long.”

She left the shop, the ribbon in her hand, feeling rather pleased with her clarity. She had not been cruel, she reasoned, only frank. She had merely said what every sensible person was thinking. It was not her fault if they preferred their comfortable illusions.

The illusion, it transpired, was far more entrenched than she had imagined. Within a day, the air in Ashford Green had changed. Conversations ceased as she approached. Women who had been her friends now offered only the briefest of nods. Her father, when he heard of it, sighed deeply and polished his spectacles without speaking. Elara was baffled. She had spoken the truth. Why should the truth be met with such chilly disapproval?

Her confusion deepened into genuine distress when she received a note, delivered by hand. The script was familiar, a bold, elegant hand she had studied on the pages of a borrowed book of poetry. It was from Mr. Julian Croft. He was the new magistrate, a man of intellect and quiet authority who had recently taken up residence in the old manor house. Elara had met him twice at the assembly rooms and had been struck by his thoughtful conversation and the serious, intelligent light in his grey eyes. He was the only person in the county, she felt, who might truly appreciate an unvarnished mind. She had, in the privacy of her own heart, begun to cultivate a most profound respect for him.

The note was brief. He requested the pleasure of her company for a walk in the churchyard that afternoon, as he wished to discuss a matter of some importance.

Her heart leaped. Here, at last, was a man of sense! He wished to speak with her, to perhaps discuss the very foolishness she had exposed. He would understand. He would see the logic in her position. She dressed with care, choosing a simple but well-made gown of deep green, and pinned a single, perfect rose to her bodice. She felt a thrill of anticipation. This would be a meeting of two minds.

She found him by the ancient yew tree, his hands clasped behind his back, his expression grave. He did not smile when he saw her. He bowed, his formality a cold wind against her warm hopes.

“Miss Vance,” he said, his voice low and even. “Thank you for coming.”

“Mr. Croft. Your note was intriguing.” She offered him a bright, frank smile.

He did not return it. He turned and began to walk slowly along the gravel path, and she fell into step beside him. For a long moment, he said nothing, and the silence was not comfortable, but heavy.

“I understand,” he began at last, his gaze fixed on the weathered headstones, “that you have been speaking of my sister.”

Elara stopped walking. The world seemed to tilt beneath her feet. “Your… sister, sir?”

“Beatrice,” he said, the name spoken with a tenderness that was like a physical blow. “Mrs. Arthur Finch.”

The blood drained from Elara’s face. The rose on her bodice suddenly seemed a gaudy, mocking thing. Beatrice Corbin, the soap bubble, the calculating girl, the cause of domestic misery… was his sister. The sister he had raised himself after their parents’ death. The sister whose happiness he cherished above all other things.

“I… I did not know,” she stammered, her prized frankness deserting her, leaving only a raw, gaping hole of shame. “I had no idea.”

“Knowledge does not excuse the sentiment, Miss Vance,” he said, his voice still quiet, but now laced with a cold disappointment that was far worse than any shout. “You spoke of my sister’s marriage as an act of folly. You spoke of my brother-in-law’s judgment as vanity. You declared, in a public place, that their union would end in misery. You did not speak of strangers. You spoke of my family.”

Every word was a perfectly aimed dart. Elara could see it all now: her own arrogance, her self-righteous pronouncements delivered with the smugness of a judge. She had not been pruning weeds; she had been trampling a flower, and the gardener she most wished to impress had witnessed it all.

“I thought… I thought I was merely being honest,” she whispered, the words tasting like ash.

“There is a difference, Miss Vance, between honesty and cruelty,” Julian Croft said, finally turning to face her. His grey eyes, which she had found so intelligent and kind, were now filled with a profound and weary sadness. “Honesty is a scalpel, to be used with care and precision for the good of the patient. You used it as a cudgel. You took a private matter of the heart and exposed it to the public gaze for your own satisfaction. You sought to display your cleverness, and in doing so, you wounded people I love.”

He looked at her, a long, searching look that seemed to strip away her every defence. “I had thought you a woman of rare perception,” he said softly. “I see now that you are merely a woman who enjoys the sound of her own voice.”

He bowed again, a gesture of finality. “Good day, Miss Vance.”

He turned and walked away, leaving her standing alone in the shadow of the yew tree. The sun was warm on her neck, and the birds were singing in the branches above, but Elara felt only a cold, deep chill. The truth, she now understood, was not a tonic at all. It was a poison, and she had just swallowed a fatal dose. She looked at the ribbon in her hand, a silly, frivolous thing, and let it fall to the ground. She was left with nothing but the bitter, unvarnished, and utterly devastating truth of her own foolishness.
