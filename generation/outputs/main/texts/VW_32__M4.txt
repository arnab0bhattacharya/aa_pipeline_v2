The heat of the afternoon had pressed the household into a kind of stupor, so that the villa seemed to hold its breath, its white walls glaring, its shutters drawn like eyelids against the sun. Miss Ada Penrose, sitting on the low stone step of the courtyard with a letter half-written on her knee, felt the stillness gather about her, not heavy, not oppressive, but rather as though the whole place—the geraniums in their earthen pots, the lemon tree with its dusty leaves, the well with its iron hoop—had consented to some slow, secret agreement with the hour. She had been three months in Andalusia, and still the light astonished her; it fell differently here, she thought, it did not merely illuminate but seemed to draw the colour out of things, so that the red of the tiles and the blue of the sky and the green of the distant hills became almost audible, a ringing in the mind that was not quite sound.

From within the house came the faint, fretful voice of Mrs. Hartley, calling for her salts, and then the deeper, more patient murmur of Mr. Hartley, who was somewhere in the shadow of the salon, fanning himself with a newspaper and pretending to read. The children—Edith and little Charles—had been put down for their rest an hour ago, and the silence that had followed was the first the household had known since morning. Ada did not move. She had learned, in these three months, the value of such moments; they were hers, they belonged to no one else, and she held them as a miser holds coins, turning them over, feeling their edges.

She had come to Spain, she reflected, with no expectation of pleasure. A governess of eight-and-twenty, with a plain face and a neat, unobtrusive way of dressing, she had long ago ceased to expect anything but the mild succession of days, the small duties, the letters home, the gradual accumulation of a life that was not quite her own. The Hartleys were kind enough, in their fashion; they paid her on the first of the month, and Mrs. Hartley had once said, in a moment of expansiveness, that she did not know what they would do without her. But kindness, Ada had discovered, could be as remote as cruelty; it did not warm you, it merely did not wound you.

Yet here, in this white villa on the slope above the village of Santa Lucia de la Fuente, something had begun to shift. She could not have said when it began, or how. Perhaps it was the morning, three weeks ago, when old Manuela, the cook, had fallen ill and Ada had gone down into the village to fetch the doctor, and had found herself speaking, without hesitation, the words that came—the strange, rolling, liquid words that she had studied in a book in London, that she had repeated to herself in her narrow room, and that now, suddenly, were alive, were useful, were hers. The doctor had understood her. The baker, standing in his doorway with flour on his hands, had understood her. The woman at the fountain, with her child on her hip, had smiled and answered her, and Ada had felt the words pass between them like a thread drawn tight, a small, bright connection in the dusty air.

After that, it had seemed, the village had taken her in. It was not that they were effusive; they were not. But they nodded to her now when she walked down the hill in the morning, and the baker saved her the small, crescent-shaped rolls she liked, and the shoemaker, a thin man with a melancholy face, had mended her boot without charge and would not hear of payment. She had become, in some way she did not fully understand, a person among them—not the English governess, not the foreigner, but the señorita who spoke, who listened, who carried messages and wrote letters and explained, when explanations were needed, what the Hartleys wanted and what the villagers meant.

She dipped her pen in the ink and wrote another line of her letter—something about the weather, something about the children—but her thoughts had drifted, and the words on the page seemed thin and far away, like voices heard through a wall. She looked up. The courtyard was empty, but beyond the archway, at the bottom of the garden, she could see the fig tree and, beneath it, the figure of a man—one of the villagers, she thought, perhaps the one they called Rafael, who came sometimes to tend the vines. He was standing quite still, looking out toward the hills, and there was something in his posture—the relaxed shoulders, the hands loose at his sides—that spoke of a contentment so complete, so unselfconscious, that Ada felt a sudden, sharp envy. To stand like that, she thought. To stand in the sun and look at the hills and feel no need to justify oneself, to explain, to be useful. To simply be.

A door opened somewhere, and she heard Mrs. Hartley's voice, clearer now, calling her name. Ada rose, folding the letter, tucking it into her pocket. The moment had passed, but the feeling of it remained, a warmth in her chest, a kind of quiet astonishment. She went through the cool, shadowed passage and into the salon, where Mrs. Hartley lay upon the sofa with a handkerchief over her eyes, and Mr. Hartley sat in his chair, the newspaper fallen to the floor.

"Ada," said Mrs. Hartley, not lifting the handkerchief, "the children will be waking soon. And I want you to go down to the village—the woman, the one who does the washing, she has not brought the linen, and I cannot think what has happened. You will go, won't you? You will explain to her?"

"Yes, Mrs. Hartley," said Ada.

She stood for a moment in the doorway, looking at them—the two of them, so helpless in their way, so dependent upon her, and she felt, strangely, not resentment but a kind of tenderness. They did not know, she thought. They did not know what it was to be understood, to be spoken to in one's own tongue, to feel the world open and receive one. They would never know. And yet, in a way, they had given it to her—this house, this village, this language that had become, in her mouth, a second self.

She turned and went out into the brightness, down the path, through the gate, and the dust rose about her feet as she walked, and the sun beat upon her shoulders, and the hills stood blue and distant against the sky. The village lay below, its roofs red and close together, and she could hear, faintly, the sound of the church bell, and the barking of a dog, and somewhere a woman singing. She walked toward it, and the words were already forming in her mind—the greetings, the explanations, the small, necessary courtesies—and they came to her not as a lesson learned but as a kind of music, a language that was hers, that she had earned, that she would carry with her now wherever she went.
