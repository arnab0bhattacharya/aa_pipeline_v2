The *Daily Chronicle* had been unkind, as is its custom, but this time it had overreached the bounds of mere criticism and ventured into the territory of vulgarity. It accused Lord Alistair Thorne of purchasing his reputation like a cheap bolt of silk, claiming that his recent donation of half his estate to the poor of Whitechapel was not an act of altruism, but a shrewd investment in social capital. The article was signed by a man whose name suggested a lineage of hatters, and it was printed in bold type, flanked by illustrations of beggars who looked suspiciously like actors from the East End variety circuit.

Lord Alistair sat in his study, a room lined with books he had never read and portraits of ancestors who had never lived, reading the paper with the detached interest of a man watching a dog chase a car. He did not look up when the door burst open. He knew the sound of that particular anger; it was a musical note, high and shrill, that had been the background score to their marriage for fifteen years.

"You are ruined," said Lady Thorne, standing in the doorway with a fan of ostrich feathers trembling in her hand. "The society will be horrified. They will say you are a fool, or worse, a showman. They will say you bought their pity."

"I have bought nothing, my dear," Lord Alistair replied, turning a page of the newspaper with a finger stained slightly with ink. "I simply disposed of a surplus. One cannot keep one’s money in a vault; it grows damp and smells of mildew. It was better to let it go and see what it became in the hands of others."

"Money is not a moral agent, Alistair," she snapped, stepping into the room. She was a woman of striking beauty, though it was a beauty that had long since ceased to be surprised by anything. Her eyes were the pale, cold blue of ice that had been left too long on the table. "And you are not a moral agent either. You are a banker. You calculate. You always have. I have watched you for fifteen years. I know that you do not give because you love humanity. You give because you enjoy the mechanics of the transaction. You like the idea of being the hand that feeds, because it allows you to stand at a safe distance from the dirt."

Lord Alistair smiled. It was a slow, enigmatic curve of the lips that did not reach his eyes. "And if I do? Is it not better to be a calculated saint than a spontaneous sinner? The latter is messy, my dear. It involves regret. The former involves only arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," she repeated, and the word seemed to sting her. She walked to the window, looking out at the grey London afternoon, where the fog was creeping up the steps of the church like a slow, white tide. "I always thought you were a bore. A magnificent bore, but a bore nonetheless. You speak of charity as if it were a quarterly dividend. You speak of the poor as if they were items in a ledger. I resented you for it. I resented the fact that you could be so generous with your money, yet so miserly with your soul."

Lord Alistair closed the newspaper. "You are being dramatic. You have always had a taste for the dramatic. It is one of the few virtues you possess."

"Perhaps," she said, turning to face him. Her voice had lost its shrill edge. It was quieter now, but there was a weight to it, a gravity that seemed to settle in the air between them. "But I have just read that article, and I find that I am angry. Not because they are attacking you, but because they are right. They think you are a showman. And I have spent so long agreeing with them that I have come to believe it myself. I have looked at your charities and seen only vanity. I have looked at your kindness and seen only strategy."

She paused, her hand resting on the velvet arm of the chair. "But today, I realized something. You did not go to the opening of the new hospital last week. You did not cut the ribbon. You did not give a speech. You simply sent the money. You did not want the applause. You did not want the thanks. You wanted nothing in return. Not even the satisfaction of being seen to be good."

Lord Alistair looked at her, his expression shifting from amusement to something more complex, something that looked like a faint, weary surprise. "You noticed that?"

"I noticed that you were bored," she said. "I noticed that you were annoyed by the noise. I had always thought that annoyance was a sign of indifference. But now I wonder if it was a sign of integrity. You cannot be a showman if you are annoyed by the audience."

"The audience is always too loud," he said softly. "They expect a miracle. They want the poor to be clean, and the rich to be humble, and the middle class to be happy. It is a tedious performance. I preferred to stay home and read."

"You read that book about the history of coins," she said. "You are reading it for the third time."

"Because it is true," he said. "Coins do not lie. They do not have motives. They are simply metal, shaped by men, passed from hand to hand. That is all we are, my dear. We are metal. We are shaped by our desires. And we are passed from hand to hand, until we wear out."

Lady Thorne walked to him and stood in front of his desk. She looked at the paper, then at him. "You are a terrible philosopher, Alistair. You use the language of aesthetics to hide the emptiness of your heart."

"Is it empty?" he asked. "Or is it simply full of things that cannot be seen? I do not have a heart, my dear. I have a conscience. And my conscience is very particular. It requires silence. It requires distance. If I were to stand in the street and weep with the beggars, I would be no better than the beggars. I would be part of the mess. I prefer to be the observer. The one who provides the light, but does not step into the fire."

She stared at him. For a moment, the mask of indifference slipped, and she saw something behind his eyes—a deep, abiding sadness, like the shadow of a cloud moving across a bright field. It was not the sadness of grief, but the sadness of excess. He had too much of everything, and therefore he had learned to value nothing.

"I do not understand you," she said.

"No one does," he replied. "That is the price of being a Thorne. We are too large to be loved, and too small to be worshipped. We are just... furniture."

"Then why do you give?" she asked. "If you are furniture, why do you move?"

Lord Alistair picked up a crystal decanter of brandy and poured two glasses. He handed one to her. "Because furniture has to be placed somewhere. And I prefer to be placed in a place where I am useful, rather than in a place where I am decorative. The poor do not need a decoration. They need bread. I give them bread. It is the only honest transaction I know."

She took the glass. Her hand was steady. "You are still a bore," she said.

"I am aware of it," he said. "But I am a bore who keeps his promises. And I am a bore who has just saved your marriage from the scandal of a broken promise. You will be seen as the wife who defended her husband, not the wife who mocked him. The society will say you are a good woman. You will enjoy that, I expect."

"I expect I shall," she admitted. She took a sip of the brandy. It burned pleasantly in her throat. "And the newspaper?"

"We shall ignore it. Let the hatter write his piece. It will be forgotten by tomorrow. Reputation is a thin thing, my dear. It is made of air and ink. But integrity is solid. It is made of steel. And I have quite a lot of steel in my character, though it is usually hidden under the silk."

Lady Thorne looked at him, really looked at him, for the first time in years. She saw the lines around his mouth, the grey in his hair, the quiet dignity of a man who had learned to live with himself. She did not love him. She did not think she ever would. But she respected him. And in a world that was rapidly becoming a caricature of itself, respect was a rarer thing than love.

"You are still dreadful," she said.

"Yes," he agreed, raising his glass. "But I am yours. And that is a much better position to be in."

She smiled, a small, genuine smile that transformed her cold face into something almost warm. "Indeed. And I am yours. How unfortunate for both of us."

"Terribly so," he said. "But let us have dinner. I hear the chef has prepared a soufflé. It is a dangerous dish. It rises or it falls. It is never in the middle. Much like our marriage, I suspect."

"Let us hope it rises," she said.

"I should hope so," he replied. "I have never cared for a fallen soufflé. It is so much more honest, but it is so much less fun to eat."
