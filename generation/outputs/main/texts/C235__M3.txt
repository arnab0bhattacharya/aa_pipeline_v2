The wheat had crossed the river by the first week of August. Elias Vane had bred it to survive the thin, chalky scrape of the Harling experimental farm, but he had not anticipated its hunger for the rich loam of the Yare valley. By the time the farmers of Blackmore noticed the pale, blue-green shoots pushing through their barley, it was already too late. It grew with a ferocity that belonged to tropical weeds rather than English grain, its roots sinking deep and wide, secreting a sour tang into the earth that poisoned the roots of anything else daring to compete. Within a fortnight, the experimental strain had choked out forty acres of prime Norfolk barley.

Elias stood at the edge of Josiah Thorne's field, the July heat pressing down on his shoulders like a wool blanket. The wheat was already chest-high, its bearded heads nodding in the thick air, heavy with grain. It looked healthy, robust—a triumph of selective breeding. But beneath the surface, it was a plague. The barley it had swallowed was gone entirely, not even stubble remaining. The soil itself seemed changed, cracked and drained of its dark color, turned a dusty grey by the wheat's relentless feeding.

"It came up overnight," Thorne said from behind him. The farmer's voice was flat, scraped clean of the anger Elias had expected. That was worse. "Like frost, but in July. I thought it was a blessing at first, a second crop I hadn't planted. Then the barley started to yellow. By the time I called the men in, it was growing faster than we could cut it."

Elias knelt and pulled a stalk from the earth. The root system was enormous, a tangled mat that had spread nearly two feet in every direction. He had crossed a hardy Crimean variety with an English heritage strain, selecting for drought tolerance and soil resilience over three generations. He had succeeded too well. The plant didn't just tolerate poor conditions—it aggressively colonized any soil it touched, draining nutrients at a rate that left nothing for neighboring plants. He had kept it contained at Harling through strict plot management, deep trenching, and careful harvesting. But a single bag of seed had gone missing from the storage barn in June. He had reported it, assumed it was an accounting error. Now he knew better.

"How far has it spread?" Elias asked.

Thorne pointed south, toward the cluster of rooftops that marked the village of Blackmore. "Crick's meadow is gone. The Allens lost their oats. It's in the Hedgerow fields, moving toward the river. The soil where it's been longest is already dying. Nothing will grow there next year. Maybe not for years."

Elias stood and brushed the dirt from his hands. The earth felt wrong, powdery and spent, like something that had been baked and ground down. He had spent six years developing this strain, trying to solve the problem of failing yields on marginal land. The Agricultural Board had been interested, had funded his work. They wanted wheat that could be grown anywhere, that could feed the growing industrial cities without relying solely on the best farmland. He had given them exactly what they asked for, and it was devouring the countryside.

The meeting was held in Blackmore's parish hall, a low-ceilinged room that smelled of damp wood and lamp oil. Forty farmers had gathered, their faces burnt dark by the summer sun, their hands rough and stained. They sat on wooden benches arranged in uneven rows, and when Elias entered behind Thorne, the room went silent in a way that felt like a door closing.

Thorne led him to the front, where a small table had been set. Elias set his satchel down and faced them. He had prepared remarks, notes on the strain's characteristics, on containment strategies, on potential remedies. But looking out at the rows of men who had watched their fields disappear under a tide of wheat they hadn't planted, he found the papers in his hand useless.

"I know what you're thinking," Elias began. "That I did this. And you're right. The wheat spreading through your fields is a strain I developed at Harling. It was bred for resilience, for the ability to grow where other crops fail. I did not anticipate that it would behave this way outside of controlled conditions. I did not anticipate that it would spread as it has."

A man in the second row stood. Elias recognized him—Martin Crane, who farmed eighty acres on the eastern slope toward the marshes. His face was drawn, his eyes red-rimmed.

"Anticipate," Crane repeated. "My father planted that field in 1873. I've worked it since I was twelve years old. Your wheat ate it in nine days. You talk about anticipation while my soil turns to dust."

Another farmer stood, then another. The room filled with voices, not shouting but steady, heavy with a weight that pressed against Elias's chest. They spoke of fields their families had worked for generations, of soil that had been built up over decades with manure and careful rotation, of livelihoods that hung on the balance of a single season's harvest. The wheat had taken not just crops but the foundation beneath them. The roots didn't just compete—they chemically suppressed other vegetation, acidifying the soil, rendering it barren for anything but themselves.

Elias listened. He did not defend himself. When the voices finally quieted, he spoke again.

"I can't undo what's happened to your fields. But I believe I can stop it. The wheat responds to copper sulfate. Applied at concentration, it will kill the root system. The soil will need treatment afterward—lime, heavy manuring, possibly a full season of fallow with a nitrogen-fixing cover crop. It will take time, and it will cost money. I will bear that cost. I will write to the Agricultural Board tonight and request emergency funding. And I will personally oversee the treatment of every affected field."

The room was quiet. Thorne, who had been standing by the door, stepped forward. "And if the Board refuses?"

"Then I will pay for it myself. I have some savings. It won't be enough for all of it, but it will be a start."

Crane was still standing. He looked at Elias for a long moment, his expression unreadable. "You bred this thing," he said. "You made it to grow where nothing else will. Did you ever once think about what happens when it grows where everything else already does?"

Elas met his gaze. "No," he said. "I didn't. That is my failure, and I will carry it."

Crane sat down. The room exhaled.

Thorne organized the work parties the next morning. Elias led them, carrying the copper sulfate in heavy drums from the railway depot at Harling station. They worked in pairs, one man pumping the sprayer while another walked ahead to clear the dead stalks. The chemical stank of metal and rot, and it turned the wheat a sickly brown within hours of application. Beneath the surface, the roots would take longer to die, breaking down slowly over weeks.

Elias worked alongside them, his hands blistering around the pump handle. The farmers didn't speak to him much beyond what was necessary—pass the drum, move left, hold the line. But they didn't drive him away, either. He was there, in the field, doing the work. That seemed to matter.

By the end of the first week, they had treated twenty acres. The treated soil looked worse than before—scarred, discolored, dead. But when Elias knelt and dug his fingers into it, he felt something beneath the surface. Not life, not yet, but the absence of the wheat's grip. The earth was exhausted, spent, but it was no longer being consumed. It was simply waiting.

He drove back to Harling that evening, the fields stretching out around him in the late summer light. At the edge of the experimental farm, he stopped and looked at the controlled plots where his wheat still grew in neat, disciplined rows. It looked beautiful, thick and golden, exactly what he had designed it to be. He stood there for a long time, watching the wind move through it, listening to the dry rustle of the heads. Then he walked to the storage shed, found the kerosene, and set fire to every remaining bag of seed.
