Of all the high streets in all the towns in England, I hold that the High Street of Chatham, in the 1840s, was the most theatrical. It was not merely a thoroughfare for the buying of bacon and the selling of shoes; it was a stage upon which the whole drama of human life was played out, from the earliest tragedy of a spilt pail of milk to the latest farce of a dog in a bonnet. The shops, with their bow-windows bulging with wares, were so many proscenium arches; the pavement was the boards; and the good people of Chatham were at once the actors and the audience, applauding, condemning, and weeping by turns.

Upon this stage, in a small but meticulously ordered shop that stood between a chandler’s and a milliner’s, lived and laboured the two brothers Grubb. The elder, Mr. Solomon Grubb, was a man as neat as his own stock-keeping. His apron was a monument to starch, his ledgers were a paragon of penmanship, and his very spectacles seemed to sit upon his nose with an air of profound and unimpeachable judgment. Solomon was the very soul of commerce; he knew the precise moment at which a cheese had ripened to its peak and the exact degree of indignation with which to greet a request for credit. His shop was his kingdom, his counter his throne, and the brass scales upon it his sceptre.

His younger brother, Mr. Samuel Grubb, was of a different clay altogether. Where Solomon was solid, Samuel was vaporous. Where Solomon saw pounds, shillings, and pence, Samuel saw castles in the air, galleons on a wine-dark sea, and fortunes to be made not by the slow, honest accretion of a farthing at a time, but by some grand, glittering, and invariably unspecified stroke of genius. Samuel was a young man whose waistcoats were always a trifle too splendid for his station, and whose conversation was a perpetual, sparkling fountain of ‘schemes’ and ‘prospects’ that never, by any chance, produced a single drop of actual water. He was the dreamer to Solomon’s doer, the flourish to his brother’s firm hand, and he lived upon the fruits of Solomon’s labour with a cheerful and untroubled gratitude that was, in its own way, the most exasperating thing about him.

For some years, this odd domestic partnership had continued, a sort of human seesaw in which Solomon’s solid weight kept Samuel’s airy aspirations from flying away entirely. But the delicate balance was thrown into violent disarray by the arrival upon the scene of Miss Arabella Pringle. Miss Pringle was the daughter of Mr. Josiah Pringle, a gentleman who had amassed a considerable fortune in the naval provisioning line, and whose warehouse down by the dockyards was a monument to the mighty power of preserved beef. Arabella was a handsome girl, with eyes as bright as the tin on her father’s cans, and she saw in the handsome, day-dreaming Samuel Grubb a romantic figure utterly unlike the solid, gravy-stained youths of her father’s acquaintance. Samuel, for his part, saw in Arabella not only a beautiful face but a golden key, which might unlock the door to that grander world he had always felt was his true inheritance.

Their courtship was a whirlwind of borrowed finery and romantic assignations in the shadow of the great warships in the harbour. It was only a matter of time, therefore, before the matter must come to a head. And it came to a head one rainy Tuesday evening, when the shop was closed and the two brothers sat in the little parlour behind it, the air thick with the smell of tea and Solomon’s quiet, methodical pen-scratching.

‘Solomon,’ said Samuel, clearing his throat with the air of a man about to make a speech in Parliament. ‘My dear brother. A moment of your time.’

Solomon looked up, his pen poised over a column of figures. ‘A moment is all I have, Samuel. The accounts for the quarter must be balanced before midnight.’

‘It is of accounts I wish to speak,’ said Samuel, striking a pose before the mantelpiece. ‘Not of the paltry accounts of cheese and candles, but the great account of a man’s life, of his happiness, of his future!’

‘You are engaged, I take it,’ said Solomon, with the weary air of a man who has seen this particular play before.

‘I am!’ cried Samuel, beaming. ‘The lovely Arabella has consented to be mine! Her father, Mr. Pringle, has given his consent! We are to be married!’

‘Indeed,’ said Solomon, setting down his pen. ‘And Mr. Pringle, I presume, is to provide the settlement, the house, and the means for your joint support?’

Samuel’s smile faltered. ‘Well, not entirely. There is a matter of… of presentation. Of establishing ourselves properly. A wedding, my dear brother, is not a thing to be done on a shoestring. It must be an event! A statement!’

‘And such a statement,’ said Solomon, his voice dangerously quiet, ‘requires funds. Which you do not possess.’

‘Which *we* do not possess,’ Samuel corrected him, with a magnanimous sweep of his hand. ‘But which we may obtain! I have been thinking, Solomon. I have been observing. This shop, this dear, good old shop… it has served its purpose. It has kept us, it has raised us. But it is a cage! A gilded cage for an eagle like me! We must spread our wings!’

Solomon rose to his feet. The floorboards seemed to creak in protest. ‘What,’ he said, his voice trembling with a cold fury, ‘are you proposing?’

‘The solution is simple! Elegant!’ Samuel was warming to his theme now, his eyes shining. ‘We sell the business! We sell the lease, the stock, the good-will! Think of it! A handsome sum, I am sure. Enough for a magnificent wedding, a stylish little villa in a genteel neighbourhood, and a capital sum to invest in one of my… my schemes!’

For a moment, Solomon was utterly silent. He looked at his brother as if he were a creature from some other, more foolish planet. Then, he spoke, and his words fell into the quiet room like stones into a well.

‘Sell the shop,’ he repeated, each word a separate, sharp-edged thing. ‘This shop. Our father’s shop. The shop I have swept every morning since I was fifteen years old. The shop whose every floorboard I know, whose every tin of biscuit I can locate in the dark. You would sell the solid, honest, bread-and-butter ground beneath our feet… to pay for a party?’

‘It is not a party! It is an investment in my future! In *our* future!’ Samuel protested. ‘Think of Arabella! Think of the Pringles! They are a family of consequence! We cannot have them thinking we are… common grocers!’

‘We *are* grocers!’ roared Solomon, his face flushed with a passion his brother had never seen. ‘It is an honest trade! It is a good trade! It is the trade that has paid for your fancy waistcoats and your hair-oil and your endless, feckless, useless dreams! And you would tear it all up, root and branch, and burn it on the altar of your own vanity?’

The two brothers stood facing each other across the small room, the chasm between them wider than the few feet of worn carpet. Outside, the rain continued to fall on the High Street, washing the dust from the cobbles and blurring the gaslights into long, golden tears upon the wet stones. And in the quiet of the little parlour behind the grocery, the dreamer and the doer, the two halves of a single life, found that they no longer understood one another at all. The future, which had seemed so simple to one and so secure to the other, now lay between them, a broken and uncertain thing.
