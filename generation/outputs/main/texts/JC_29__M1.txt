It is a strange thing to look back upon the period of that revolution in the valley of San Ramón, and to understand how little of its true meaning penetrated the mists of our isolation. We knew nothing, of course; and the knowledge which came to us later was of a kind to make one wonder whether ignorance is not, after all, the most merciful of all conditions. The heat lay upon the hills like a heavy, suffocating blanket, and the silence of the siesta hour was broken only by the drowsy hum of insects or the far-off, rhythmic thrumming of a machete clearing the undergrowth. It was a silence that seemed to possess a moral quality, a waiting, as if the very air of the republic were holding its breath for the inevitable explosion of its own passions.

We were there, my husband and I, because of the coffee; and we stayed there because of the peculiar, stubborn blindness of the Englishman abroad, who believes that a straight line and a strong fence can impose a moral order upon the tangled, chaotic nature of things. Edward was a good man, a man of iron principles and a narrow, practical vision, who saw the world as a series of engineering problems to be solved with the right tools and the right amount of force. He understood the mechanics of the pulper and the drying beds; he understood the weight of a sack of beans and the price of labour. But the soul of the place, that dark, brooding, superstitious heart which beat beneath the green canopy of the coffee trees, was to him merely an inconvenience, a source of friction to be smoothed away.

It was this soul that I found in the person of Padre Ignacio. He was the only educated man within fifty miles, a creature of immense learning and profound, weary resignation, who had been exiled to this remote parish for some indiscretion in the capital which he never named and I never pressed him to explain. He was thin and dry as a piece of old parchment, with eyes that held the light of a thousand forgotten suns and the darkness of a thousand sleepless nights. We became friends, I think, because we recognized in each other the same sense of exile, the same quiet desperation. He would come to the house in the late afternoons, when the heat began to relent and the shadows of the mountains grew long and purple, and we would sit on the veranda and talk of things that had no place in the world of coffee and revolution.

He spoke of the history of the republic, of the endless, bloody cycle of pronunciamientos and counter-pronunciamientos, of the way in which the men of the valley were drawn into the quarrels of the men of the city as if by some fatal, inexorable gravity. He spoke of the nature of faith, of the way it could be a comfort to the poor and a burden to the wise, and of the terrible, lonely responsibility of the priest who must be a shepherd to a flock of wolves. His voice was low and rhythmic, with the soft, musical lilt of the Castilian he had learned in a seminary in a country he would never see again, and his words seemed to hang in the air long after he had spoken them, like the scent of the jasmine that grew wild by the gate.

I do not know what Edward thought of these visits. He was polite to the Padre, of course, with the stiff, formal courtesy of a man who respects the office if not the man, but he was impatient with our talk, with our brooding over things that could not be changed. He would sit with us for a while, his hands folded in his lap, his face a mask of polite incomprehension, and then he would excuse himself with some excuse about the accounts or the machinery, and I would be left alone with the Padre and the encroaching dusk. I think he saw the priest as a symbol of the very things he was trying to overcome: the superstition, the fatalism, the inefficiency of the Latin mind. He did not understand that the Padre was not a symbol of these things, but their most profound and tragic expression.

The revolution, when it came, was not like a storm, which announces its approach with thunder and wind. It was like a fever, a slow, creeping sickness that stole into the valley and infected everything it touched. At first, there were only rumors, whispers carried on the lips of the peons, stories of a general in the lowlands who was marching on the capital with an army of a thousand men. Edward dismissed these stories with a wave of his hand. "Nonsense," he would say. "These people are always talking of war. It is their national sport. Nothing will come of it."

But something did come of it. One morning, we awoke to find the road to the valley blocked by a company of soldiers, ragged and hungry men with the wild, desperate eyes of those who have nothing to lose. They were not the soldiers of the government, but the soldiers of the general, and they had come to requisition supplies for their cause. Edward, with his English sense of order and his belief in the sanctity of property, went out to meet them, to reason with them, to explain that the coffee was not his to give but a trust to be held for the owners in London. I watched from the window as he stood before their captain, a small, dark man with a face like a clenched fist, and I knew, with a terrible certainty, that his words were like stones thrown into a bottomless well.

The captain listened to him with a patience that was more terrifying than any anger, and then he smiled, a thin, cruel smile that did not touch his eyes. "The revolution," he said, in a voice that was soft and deadly, "has need of many things. It has need of coffee. It has need of money. And it has need of silence." Edward, who was not a man to be intimidated, drew himself up to his full height and began to protest, but the captain raised a hand, and two of his soldiers stepped forward and took Edward by the arms. They did not harm him, but the message was clear. The world of fences and straight lines had come to an end.

It was in the days that followed that the true nature of our isolation became apparent. The soldiers remained in the valley, their presence a constant, oppressive weight upon our lives. The peons, who had once been so docile and submissive, now looked at us with a new and unsettling hostility. They saw us not as their employers, but as representatives of the foreign power that had for so long exploited their country, and their resentment, which had been simmering for generations, was now boiling over into open defiance. Edward, who had always prided himself on his understanding of the native character, was at a complete loss. He saw only the ingratitude, the treachery, the irrationality of a people who would rather starve than work.

It was during this time that Padre Ignacio came to see me for the last time. He came in the evening, when the soldiers were gathered around their fires and the valley was filled with the sound of their rough laughter and the smell of their cheap rum. He looked older than I had ever seen him, his face drawn and haggard, his eyes filled with a profound and inconsolable sorrow. He told me that he had been to the capital, to see if he could use his influence to bring some sense to the chaos, but he had failed. The men of the city were as blind and as bloodthirsty as the men of the hills, and the revolution had become a monster that was devouring its own children.

We sat in the darkness of the drawing-room, the only light a single candle that threw our shadows, huge and distorted, upon the walls. He spoke of the terrible choices that had been forced upon him, of the way in which he had been compelled to bless the banners of men he knew to be murderers and to give the last rites to boys he had baptized as infants. He spoke of the weight of the confessional, of the secrets of hatred and betrayal that had been poured into his ears, secrets that were now being acted out in the bloody drama of the valley. "I am a priest," he said, his voice barely a whisper. "I am supposed to be a channel of God's grace. But I feel only the darkness. I feel only the sin."

I wanted to comfort him, to tell him that he was a good man, that he had done his best in impossible circumstances. But the words would not come. I felt, in that moment, the utter uselessness of my own pity, the utter inadequacy of my own understanding. I was a woman of the world, a creature of comfort and privilege, and I had no right to offer consolation to a man who was wrestling with the very soul of humanity. We sat in silence for a long time, the candle flame flickering in the draught, and the silence was more eloquent than any words.

When he finally rose to leave, he took my hand in his. His skin was cold and dry, like the hand of a dead man. "Be careful, my child," he said. "The darkness is very deep. And it is not only in the valley. It is in the heart of every man." Then he was gone, swallowed up by the night, and I was left alone with the shadows and the sound of the soldiers' laughter.

The next morning, Edward came to me with a face of grim determination. He told me that the Padre was no longer to be welcome in our house. "He is a troublemaker," he said. "He is stirring up the peons with his talk of justice and his sympathy for the rebels. He is a danger to us all." I tried to argue with him, to explain that the Padre was our friend, that he was the only one who understood the terrible complexity of the situation. But Edward would not listen. He saw the world in black and white, in terms of loyalty and betrayal, of order and chaos, and the Padre had become, in his mind, an agent of the latter.

"He is a priest," I said, my voice trembling with a rage I did not know I possessed. "He is a man of God."

"He is a fool," Edward replied, his voice cold and hard. "And in times like these, a fool is more dangerous than a villain. He will not set foot in this house again. Do you understand?"

I understood. I understood that the world I had known, the world of quiet conversations and shared confidences, had been swept away by the tide of history. I understood that the fence Edward had built around our estate was not strong enough to keep out the passions of men, and that the straight line he had drawn between right and wrong had been erased by the bloody, chaotic reality of the revolution. I understood, too, that the Padre was right. The darkness was not only in the valley. It was in the heart of my husband, in the heart of the soldiers, in the heart of the peons. It was in my own heart.

I never saw Padre Ignacio again. I heard, later, that he had been killed in a skirmish near the river, shot by a soldier who did not know or care that he was a priest. But I do not know if that is true. Perhaps he simply walked into the darkness, into the vast, brooding silence of the mountains, and was swallowed up by it. I only know that his absence left a void in my life, a silence that was deeper and more profound than any I had ever known.
