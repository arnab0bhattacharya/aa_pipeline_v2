The engagement of Miss Harriet Ellwood to Mr. Charles Ashby was of that description which the world is pleased to call prudent, and which the world, having so called it, feels itself entitled to approve. Mr. Ashby was a widower of fifty-six, possessed of a handsome house at Rookwood, in Herefordshire, a clear estate of some four thousand a year, and a temper so uniformly gentle, so considerate of others, and so free from every species of caprice, that his neighbours, who had long pitied him his first marriage, now envied him his second with a sincerity that did them credit. Miss Ellwood was six-and-twenty, the daughter of a clergyman lately deceased, whose widow and children had found in Mr. Ashby’s friendship the sort of practical support which is more easily praised than repaid. If gratitude, esteem, and the settled conviction of doing right could constitute happiness, Harriet had every reason to expect it; and she had told herself so, with great composure, on a great many occasions.

She had been three weeks at Rookwood, and the wedding was fixed for the morrow, when the nephew arrived.

Mr. Ashby had spoken of him before, but always briefly, and with an air of some reserve which Harriet had attributed to the natural delicacy of a man who had no children of his own, and who felt that the heir to his name stood in a relation to his marriage which could not but be a little awkward. “My nephew, Edmund Ashby,” he had said, “is to come down before the wedding. He has been abroad. He is a young man of very good parts, I believe, but he has not been much at Rookwood since he was a boy.” And then he had changed the subject with a haste that was not quite his custom.

He arrived on a Tuesday afternoon, in weather so fine that the whole party were upon the lawn to receive him. Harriet, who had pictured a heavy, consequential young man, conscious of his expectations and disposed to be civil to her with a condescension that she should have found it difficult to forgive, was wholly unprepared for the figure that came across the grass towards them. He was tall, and rather grave than gay in his countenance, with a dark eye that had more thought in it than she had been used to see, and a manner that was perfectly unassuming, yet perfectly at ease. He greeted his uncle with an affection that was evidently not assumed, and was presented to her with a bow so simple, and a look of such frank, unembarrassed kindness, that she found herself, in the first moment of their acquaintance, entirely without any of the feelings she had prepared.

“I am very glad to know you, madam,” he said. “My uncle’s letters have told me that he is to be happy, and I could not have forgiven myself had I not come to wish him joy.”

It was said so well, so warmly, and with so little of the awkwardness which the situation might have excused, that Harriet could only answer in the same spirit; and Mr. Ashby, standing between them, looked from one to the other with a satisfaction which it did him honour to feel, and which Harriet, had she been less occupied, must have observed.

The evening passed in that sort of conversation which is the best test of character, because it is not intended as a test at all. Mr. Edmund Ashby had been in Italy and in Greece; he spoke of what he had seen with a liveliness that never degenerated into display, and of what he had felt with a delicacy that gave it all the charm of originality. He had a way of listening, too, which Harriet found it impossible to resist—a way of attending to what was said to him as if the speaker’s thoughts were of real consequence, and of answering rather to the meaning than the words, which is the rarest of all accomplishments in conversation. She had been used, in her three weeks at Rookwood, to be heard with kindness; she had not been used to be understood.

They spoke of books, of which she had read more than her modest circumstances might have suggested; of music, of which she was a passionate though unpractised admirer; of the country, which she loved with the ardour of one who had never been able to leave it. And in every subject he met her with a mind so congenial, and a sympathy so unforced, that she found herself saying things she had never said before, and discovering opinions she had not known she held, and laughing at herself for the discovery with a gaiety that was new to her.

It was not till the second evening that she understood what was happening to her, and then it came upon her with the force of a revelation, and with something of the shame of one. She was standing at the window, watching the last of the light upon the lawn, and he was standing near her, speaking of the Greek hills, and of the strange, painful pleasure of coming home from a country one has loved. “I have been thinking,” he said, “that it is a dangerous thing to have seen too much beauty abroad. One comes home with a standard that the home landscape cannot satisfy. But I have found this evening, Miss Ellwood, that the standard is not in the landscape at all, but in the eyes that look at it; and that a single evening’s conversation may do more to reconcile one to Herefordshire than a year of travelling.”

She did not answer. She could not. She was aware of a sudden, sickening clearness of vision, and in that clearness she saw, as in a glass, the whole of her situation—the morrow, and the morrow’s vow, and the gentle, trusting man who was to be its object, and the long, quiet, estimable life that lay beyond it, and the thing that she had never known she wanted until it was too late to want it. She saw that she had been happy for two days, and that she had not known it, and that she was not happy now.

“Miss Ellwood,” he said, in a lower voice, “I am afraid I have said something to distress you.”

“No,” she said, recovering herself with an effort that cost her more than she would have believed. “You have said nothing. It is the evening air. I am a little tired.”

He did not press her. He brought her a chair, and stood by it a moment in silence, and then, with a look which she did not see, but which she felt, he moved away to his uncle’s side.

That night she did not sleep. She told herself that she was a fool, and that the feelings of two days were not to be weighed against the obligations of a lifetime; that Mr. Ashby had been to her a father and a friend, and that to break faith with him would be a baseness of which she was not capable; that the nephew had never said a word to her which the most scrupulous delicacy could censure, and that to imagine a passion where there was only sympathy was the vanity of a girl who had read too many novels. She told herself all this, and she believed it, and she was wretched.

On the morning of her wedding, she met him in the hall, alone. He was dressed for the ceremony, and he looked pale. They stood for a moment without speaking, and then he said, “Miss Ellwood, I am going to say a very foolish thing, and I shall never say it again. I have known you two days, and I have no right to know you at all. But I have been thinking of you, and I shall go on thinking of you, and I want you to know it, because I think you ought to know it, and because I shall be a better man for having told you. And I want you to know, too, that I do not ask you for anything. My uncle is the best man I have ever known, and he loves you, and you will make him happy. That is all I have to say. I wish you every joy.”

She looked at him, and she saw that he was in earnest, and that he had spoken with a courage that she had not expected of him, and that he had asked her for nothing. And she loved him for it, and she told herself that she would not.

“Thank you,” she said. “I shall remember what you have said. I hope you will be happy too.”

And then the carriage came to the door, and the wedding went forward, and Harriet Ellwood became Mrs. Ashby, and was a good wife to her husband for five-and-twenty years. Mr. Edmund Ashby went abroad again in the spring, and did not return to Rookwood for a long time. And if, in the quiet evenings of her married life, when the fire was low and the house was still, she sometimes thought of a dark eye and a grave voice, and of the two days that had been so short and so long, she never spoke of it to any one, and she never repented of what she had done. For she had kept her promise, and he had not asked her to break it; and there are some things which are the better for being kept, even at a cost that the world, in its wisdom, would call too great.
