The wind screamed across the moor, tearing at the ragged hem of the curtains as I stood by the window of the study, my hand trembling not from the cold, but from the fury that boiled in my chest. Below, the valley of Ashby lay shrouded in a mist so thick it seemed to breathe, swallowing the smoke of the mills that churned day and night, poisoning the very air my tenants drew. I had come here to assert my rights, to take the ledger from the hands of Mr. Thorne, my uncle, and to speak directly to the men who worked the looms. I had expected resistance, perhaps; I had not expected the silence of the house, that heavy, suffocating quiet which told me that I was already a madwoman in the eyes of my own kin.

Mr. Thorne sat in the leather chair by the fire, his back to me, his posture one of feigned indifference. He was a man of fifty, with a face like a clenched fist and eyes that held no warmth, only a cold calculation of profit. He did not turn when I spoke.

“Uncle,” I said, my voice steady, though my heart hammered against my ribs like a trapped bird. “The rent for the cottages on the East Bank has not been collected. I intend to go to them myself tomorrow. I will speak with them as their mistress, not through the filter of your management.”

He turned then, slowly, with the deliberate grace of a predator. A smile, thin and cruel, played on his lips. “Mistress, is it? You speak of the East Bank as if it were a parlor where you might sit and sip tea. Do you know, Eliza, that a woman in the valley of Ashby who deals with the laborers is treated as a fool or a fiend? You are rich, you are young, and you are foolish. The gentry do not mix with the dross. I do.”

“I am not foolish,” I retorted, stepping closer. The firelight caught the gold thread in my gown, but I did not care for such trappings. “I am the heir to this estate. My father’s will is clear. I have a right to know the state of my own property.”

“Your father was dead before he signed that will,” Thorne said softly, and the malice in his tone was sharper than any shout. “And you, Eliza, are a child. You do not understand the mechanics of men. You do not understand that if you walk among them, you will only bring contempt upon our name. They will say you are mad. They will say the stress of the mill has driven you from your senses. Is that what you want? To be locked away in a quiet room, with a doctor watching your every move?”

The words struck me with the force of a physical blow. I felt a hot flush rise to my cheeks, but I did not back down. I had learned long ago that to yield was to die. “I do not care what they say. I care for the people. They are starving while you line your pockets with their wages. I will not be a thief in my own house.”

Thorne laughed, a dry, rattling sound. “A thief? You are a child playing at authority. Let us see how long you last. I will have the tenants notified that the rent is due to me. If you come, they will turn you away. They will call you a hysterical woman. And if you persist, I shall have you confined. It is my duty, as your guardian, to protect you from your own impulses.”

The word *confinement* hung in the air between us, heavy and ominous. I looked at him, really looked at him, and saw the truth that had been hiding behind his polite facade. He did not want to manage the estate; he wanted to own it, and he wanted to own me. My independence was a threat to his power, a crack in the foundation of his control.

“I will go,” I said, my voice low and hard as iron. “And I will bring the parson, Mr. Silas, with me. I hear he has a fondness for the truth, and for the poor.”

Thorne’s face darkened. “You dare?”

“I dare,” I replied. “And I will do it. I will not be silenced. I will not be broken. I am Eliza Ashby, and this is my home.”

I turned and walked out of the study, my footsteps echoing on the stone floor. The house felt different now, no longer a sanctuary, but a prison from which I had just escaped. As I descended the stairs, I passed the large mirror in the hallway and caught my own reflection. I looked wild, my hair disheveled, my eyes bright with a feverish intensity. I was indeed changed. The girl who had been polite and compliant was gone. In her place stood a woman who had tasted freedom, and found it bitter and sweet at the same time.

Outside, the wind had died down, but the sky remained a bruised purple, the stars hidden behind the clouds. I walked to the gatehouse, where the night watchman sat huddled by the fire. I asked for a carriage, and he looked at me with wide, uncertain eyes.

“Miss Ashby? It’s late. And the roads are muddy.”

“I will go on foot,” I said. “I will walk to the parsonage.”

He shook his head, but I was already pulling my shawl tighter around my shoulders and stepping out into the night. The cold bit into my face, but it was a good cold, a waking cold. It reminded me that I was real, that I was alive, that I was fighting. As I walked down the lane, I could see the lights of the village twinkling in the distance, like eyes watching me. They were afraid of me, they knew I was dangerous. But I did not care. I was not afraid of them. I was only afraid of myself, of the fire that burned within me, and whether I would have the strength to keep it burning until it consumed everything that stood in my way.

I thought of my father, who had loved this land with a passion that was almost religious. He had built the mills, not for profit, but for purpose. He had believed that a man’s labor was his birthright. I would honor his memory by fighting for his legacy. I would not let Thorne destroy it. I would not let the world define me. I would be Eliza Ashby, and I would be free.

As I reached the parsonage, I saw a light in the window. Mr. Silas was still awake. I raised my hand to knock, but my hand trembled. I took a deep breath, steadied myself, and knocked. The sound was loud in the silence of the night. I waited. The door opened, and there he stood, a tall, gaunt man with kind eyes that saw through me to the truth.

“Miss Ashby?” he said, his voice gentle. “Is everything well?”

I looked at him, and for the first time that day, I felt a surge of hope. “No,” I said. “But I am ready to begin.”
