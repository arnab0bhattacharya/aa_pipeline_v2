The light, which was not the light of Manchester, fell in long, slanting bars through the tall windows of the drawing-room, and where it struck the Aubusson carpet, the faded roses and impossible blue birds seemed to stir in a silent, sunlit wind. Mr. Crispin sat on the edge of a gilded chair, his hands laid flat on his knees, and felt the silence of the house press upon him, a silence made not of emptiness but of centuries of quiet living. It was a silence that had absorbed the ticking of the great long-case clock in the hall, the distant clatter of a tray in the kitchens, the sigh of the wind in the ancient cedar on the lawn. Here, time did not tick; it pooled.

From the garden came the drone of a bee, a fat, drowsy sound, and the scent of the limes drifted in at the open window, so sweet and heavy it was almost an ache. He had never known such smells. In his lodgings, there was the smell of boiled cabbage and damp wool, the smell of the city itself, a permanent haze of coal smoke that gritted the teeth and clung to the collar. But here, the air was a substance, clean and green and tasting of earth and water. He watched a dust-mote swim through a sunbeam, a whole universe in miniature, and thought of his pupils, the Masters Fairfax, and how they belonged to this world as naturally as the dust-mote to the light. They had never known anything else.

A slight gloom fell upon the room as a cloud passed over the sun. The colours of the carpet deepened, the gold of the chair-frames lost its gleam. Somewhere above, a floorboard creaked. This was the life, then. The easy, unthinking passage from one room to another, the casual arrangement of beautiful things, the sheer, unearned grace of it all. He had imagined it, of course, from the boys’ talk of half-term and ponies and the river, but the reality was a different country, a place with its own grammar and cadences which he could not parse. He felt a pang, sharp and sudden, of a longing so acute it was like a physical hunger.

“Mr. Crispin, how good of you to have come all this way.”

It was Mrs. Fairfax, standing in the doorway, a silhouette against the brighter light of the hall. She came forward, her silk dress rustling, a scent of lavender and something else, something expensive and faintly spicy, preceding her. She was not looking at him, not really, but at the arrangement of the room, her eyes sweeping over the sofa, the escritoire, the vase of sweet peas on the mantelpiece.

“The train journey was… bracing,” he managed, his voice sounding thin and reedy in the vastness.

“And the boys, they do so enjoy having their master to stay. It’s such a treat for them.” She paused, her gaze settling on him for a fraction of a second, a polite, assessing glance. “You must be quite worn out with them. They are a handful.”

He felt a flush of pride. “They are bright boys. A pleasure to teach.”

“Good, good.” She was already turning, her attention caught by something else. “Now, I must go and see about the flowers for the dinner table. Do make yourself quite at home.” And she was gone, the rustle of her silk fading down the long corridor.

At home. The words hung in the air. He was as much at home here as the cedar on the lawn would be in a Salford back yard. He looked down at his own hands, the knuckles slightly red, the nails trimmed short and practical. They were the hands of a man who marked essays and mended his own collars. He thought of the letter he had received, the elegant, sloping script of Mr. Fairfax: *We should be so delighted if you would spend the weekend with us… it would be such a pleasure…* He had read it a dozen times, a golden ticket, an escape. He had bought a new tie.

Later, in the drawing-room before dinner, the crystal glasses chimed as the footman poured sherry. Mr. Fairfax, a large, genial man with a booming laugh, clapped him on the shoulder. “Crispin! Good man! So glad you could make it. Keeps the numbers up, eh?” He winked, a broad, conspiratorial gesture that was meant to be inclusive but which landed like a stone. Crispin felt the warmth drain from his face. Numbers. He was a number. A body to fill a chair, to make the symmetry of the dining table complete. The invitation, the new tie, the bracing train journey—it was all a simple, practical matter of arithmetic. He looked across the room at the two young men from the county, all easy shoulders and drawling vowels, and understood. He was not one of them. He was an accessory, like the silver salt cellars or the footman himself.

The evening passed in a blur of candlelight and the low murmur of conversation. The food was exquisite, tastes he had no names for, and the wine made his head swim. He watched the others, their faces animated in the flickering light, and felt a profound and terrible solitude. He was not angry. The feeling was too vast and too quiet for anger. It was a sorrow, a deep and ancient sorrow for a thing he had never possessed. He thought of his own small room, the gas lamp hissing, the rows of books, the view of the brick wall opposite. It was his. This was not.

The next morning, Sunday, he stood at his window and watched the mist rise from the valley. The world was silver and green and impossibly still. A blackbird began to sing, its notes clear and piercing in the quiet air. It was the most beautiful thing he had ever heard. He felt a strange sense of release. The enchantment was still there, but it was no longer for him. It belonged to the place, to the light and the mist and the song of the bird. He was merely a witness. He would go back to the city, to the smoke and the noise, but he would carry this with him: the image of the mist in the valley, the sound of the blackbird, the knowledge that such a world existed, breathing its quiet, beautiful breath, quite without him. He straightened his new tie in the mirror, a small, neat knot of colour in a grey, northern world. It was a good tie, after all. He had bought it for himself.
