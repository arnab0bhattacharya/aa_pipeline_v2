Of all the singular cases which were brought to the attention of my friend, Mr. Sherlock Holmes, during the years in which I shared his rooms in Baker Street, there are few which so neatly combine the commonplace details of rural life with the darker passions of the human heart as the affair of the Ellerby correspondence. It was in the spring of the year 1893 that the matter was first laid before us, arriving not in the dramatic fashion of a terrified client at midnight, but through the penny post, in the shape of a cautious and remarkably intelligent letter.

The missive was from a Miss Susan Harker, the postmistress of the village of Thornden in Hampshire. She wrote with a precision that spoke well for her training, stating that over the preceding six months she had observed a curious irregularity in the mail addressed to Squire Giles Alder, of Thornden Manor. The envelopes, she declared, were invariably slightly damp upon their arrival in London, and the seals, though apparently intact, showed microscopic traces of having been softened by steam and re-attached. She had tested this theory by slipping a single hair beneath the flap of one such letter, and upon its return from the capital, the hair had vanished. It was plain to her that the Squire’s correspondence was being intercepted and read before it reached his hands.

What gave the matter urgency, however, was a postscript. The Squire’s gamekeeper, a surly fellow named Joseph Crabb, had vanished from the village three days prior to her writing, leaving his cottage empty and his dogs unfed. The local constabulary were idle, suggesting the man had simply gone to seek better employment, but Miss Harker’s feminine instincts scented a deeper villainy, and she implored Holmes to come to Thornden, addressing her letter to the police courts in the hope it would reach him.

Holmes was in one of his restless moods, pacing the room like a caged beast. “The thing has promise, Watson,” said he, glancing over the letter. “A damp envelope is a small thing, but it is the grain of sand around which the pearl of a mystery is formed. If our postmistress is correct, someone in London is intercepting Alder’s post before it is dispatched, or someone in Thornden is doing so after its arrival. The disappearance of the gamekeeper adds a certain roughness to the canvas. Pack your bag, Watson, and we shall catch the afternoon express to Winchester.”

It was a bleak and windswept landscape that greeted us as we drove out from the ancient cathedral city in a hired dogcart. The Hampshire soil was dark with rain, and the trees of Thornden Wood bent before a stiff south-westerly breeze. Miss Harker, a sharp-eyed woman of middle age, kept the village shop and post office in a structure of flint and brick. She received us with a composure that argued well for her nerves.

“You were wise to write, Miss Harker,” said Holmes, after a few preliminary questions. “Now, tell me, these damp letters. Were they dispatched from London, or did they pass through your hands to the Squire directly?”

“They came in the London mail-bag, Mr. Holmes,” she replied. “They are dispatched from a solicitor’s office in the city. I always sort the bag upon its arrival at noon. The Squire’s letters are damp when I take them out, and by the time the boy carries them up to the Manor, the paper has merely wrinkled, which a careless eye might attribute to the weather. But I know paper, sir, and that dampness is the damp of a kettle spout, not of the heavens.”

“A capital observation,” said Holmes. “The letters are tampered with before they reach your bag. That points to interference on the railway, or at the London sorting office. And the gamekeeper, Crabb? What manner of man was he?”

“A rough, silent man, Mr. Holmes, but faithful to the Squire. He had been at the Manor for twenty years. He was much in the woods of late, prowling at night. I saw his lantern near the old brick kiln at the wood’s edge but a week ago.”

We left Miss Harker and made our way up the drive to Thornden Manor, a solid Queen Anne house of red brick. Squire Alder was a man of some sixty years, robust but careworn, with the heavy jowl and bloodshot eye of one who lives too much in the saddle. He received us in his study, a room lined with books on agriculture and sporting prints.

“A gamekeeper gone missing is a serious matter for the rural police,” he said gruffly, in answer to Holmes’s explanation. “But what is this nonsense about my letters? Who would read my correspondence?”

“That,” said Holmes, “is what I hope to discover. You have a solicitor in London, I take it?”

“Ay. Marsh and Pell, of Chancery Lane. They handle the estate accounts, though there is little enough to handle, for the land is mortgaged to the hilt. I have had a sharp correspondence with them over a missing sum of three hundred pounds.”

Holmes’s eyes gleamed. “A missing sum! And your letters from them, have you kept them?”

Alder drew a bundle of papers from a drawer. Holmes took them to the window, his thin fingers turning the leaves with the delicacy of a botanist examining a specimen. Presently he stopped. “Here is the envelope of the last letter from Marsh and Pell. You will observe, Watson, that the flap is slightly discoloured, and the gum has been laid on in a double ridge. A child might miss it, but it is plain enough to a magnifying glass.”

He turned abruptly to the Squire. “Who carries the noon mail-bag from the station to the village?”

“The station is two miles off,” said Alder. “Old Thomas Vane takes his cart for it every day at eleven. He has done so for thirty years.”

We found Thomas Vane in the taproom of the village inn, a wizened, rheumy-eyed man who looked frightened at the sight of strangers. Holmes questioned him with that gentle insistence which could be so formidable. The old man’s hands shook upon his tankard.

“I takes the bag, sir, as I always has,” he stammered. “I leaves it at the post office. I touches naught.”

“But you stop at the old brick kiln in the wood,” said Holmes, his eyes fixed upon the man’s muddy boots.

Vane started. “Only to rest the horse, sir! It’s a heavy pull up the hill.”

“And there you give the bag to someone,” Holmes continued, relentlessly. “Someone who waits in the kiln. Someone who has a kettle boiling.”

The old man’s face went ashen. “I meant no harm, sir! He paid me—a crown a week. He said it was a joke, a wager about the Squire’s business.”

“Who?” cried Alder, his face dark with anger.

“Mr. Edgar, sir,” whispered Vane. “Your nephew.”

The Squire fell back in his chair. “My nephew, Philip? He lives in London. Why should he do this?”

“Because,” said Holmes, his voice cutting like a blade, “he desired to know the state of your finances and your intentions regarding your will. I observed from the lawyer’s letters that you were discussing the mortgage. A nephew with debts might have a strong interest in such matters. The dampness of the envelopes was caused by the steam used to open them, a clumsy but effective method. And now, what of the gamekeeper?”

A cold premonition seemed to seize Alder. “Crabb suspected something. He said he had seen a stranger in the wood at night. He thought it was a poacher.”

“He saw more than a poacher,” said Holmes. “He saw your nephew, Philip Edgar, come down from London to meet his accomplice, Vane, at the kiln. Crabb, a faithful man, challenged him. There was a struggle.”

Holmes turned to me. “Watson, we must examine the old brick kiln. The ground about it is soft clay, and I observed tracks of a dogcart and of two men, one wearing heavily studded boots, such as a keeper wears, and the other city-made shoes with a distinct wear on the outer heel. Come.”

We hurried to the wood, the Squire following with a white, set face. The kiln was a ruinous dome of brick, half-hidden by brambles. Holmes pushed through, his lens in his hand. He dropped to his knees, examining the trampled earth within. Suddenly he stopped, and his voice came back to us, sharp and cold.

“We are too late to save the man, but we are in time to avenge him.”

He pointed to a patch of disturbed clay. There was a dark stain upon it, and beside it lay a small, brass button, bearing the crest of the Thornden Manor livery. The earth had been hastily turned over, but beneath it, Holmes’s probing fingers soon disclosed the grim truth. Joseph Crabb would never return to his cottage.

“The nephew has struck him down,” said Holmes, rising and brushing the clay from his knees. “He came down to silence the keeper, and used the old kiln as a grave. Vane, terrified by the murder, has kept his peace through fear. We must telegraph to Scotland Yard, Alder, and to the London police, for Philip Edgar’s arrest. He will be taken at his chambers in town before he can dispose of the stolen letters.”

It was as Holmes predicted. Philip Edgar, a dissipated young man with gambling debts, was apprehended that night in his rooms in Bloomsbury, where several of the Squire’s private letters were found in his desk. He was tried at the Winchester Assizes, where the evidence of the postmistress, the frightened confession of old Vane, and the crushing deductions of Holmes regarding the damp envelopes and the footprints at the kiln, left him no avenue of escape. He was sentenced to hang, and the village of Thornden returned to its quiet, rural obscurity, save that Miss Harker ever after regarded her position as postmistress with a singular and well-deserved pride.
