The situation of young Cuthbert Peele, when he first came down to Bexhill-on-Sea, was this: he had failed his second M.B. examination with a completeness that left nothing to the imagination, and his father, a linen-draper in Camberwell who had already paid for two years of lectures and dissection, had made it abundantly plain that the family purse was now closed to further academic adventure. It was therefore with a certain bitter satisfaction, a sense of having reached the bottom of things, that Cuthbert answered the advertisement of Mr. Septimus Gammon, Pharmaceutical Chemist, of Sea View House, Marine Parade, for an assistant of good address and sober habits.

Mr. Gammon’s establishment was a narrow, tall-shouldered shop wedged between a confectioner’s and an agency for bicycles, and it smelled powerfully of carbolic, of camphor, and of the peppermint lozenges that Mr. Gammon compounded himself upon a marble slab in the back room. Mr. Gammon was a small, grey, side-whiskered man with a fixed idea that the youth of the present generation were going to the dogs, and a fixed habit of saying so. He engaged Cuthbert at fifteen shillings a week, found him a bedroom under the leads, and warned him solemnly against the wiles of the ladies who came down from London in August. “They’ll have you measuring out their scents and their complexion-washes,” said Mr. Gammon, “and the next thing you know you’ll be standing them a supper at the Assembly Rooms, and that’s how a chemist’s assistant loses his situation. I’ve seen it.” Cuthbert, who had never in his life stood anyone a supper, promised to be wary.

For three weeks he was as wary as could be desired. He learned to roll pills, to write “Mist. Sennae Co.” upon a label in a hand that Mr. Gammon called copperplate and Cuthbert privately thought a little florid, and to keep his cuffs clean in despite of the pestle and the syrup of squills. Then came the August Bank Holiday, and with it, upon the Marine Parade, a young woman in a white dress with a blue sash, carrying a parasol and a small leather satchel, who stopped at the window of Sea View House to look at a display of sponge-bags and eau-de-Cologne, and lingered, and came in.

Her name was Miss Adeline Trewin, of Brixton, and she was staying with her aunt at a boarding-house in De la Warr Road. She was nineteen, and she had a way of putting her head on one side when she asked a question that went through Cuthbert Peele like a draught of nitrous oxide. She wanted a bottle of lavender water for her aunt, and something for the freckles, and did the shop keep that nice violet powder that came in the round box with the picture on the lid? Cuthbert, who had never before in his life taken the least interest in freckles, found himself recommending a solution of corrosive sublimate with a fluency that surprised him, and then, because she seemed impressed by his manner, he heard himself say that he was, of course, only temporarily in the shop—that he was reading for his finals at St. Bartholomew’s, and had come down to the sea for the quiet.

“Oh, a medical student!” said Miss Trewin, and her eyes grew round with respect. “My brother is at Guy’s.”

“Indeed,” said Cuthbert, with a sinking at the heart. “Which year?”

“He is just through his first,” said Miss Trewin, “and he says the chemistry is dreadful.”

“The chemistry,” said Cuthbert, recovering himself, “is the foundation of everything. Without a sound knowledge of the reactions one is merely a quack.” And he sold her the corrosive sublimate and the lavender water, and she said he was very clever, and went out into the sunshine, and Cuthbert stood looking after her with the peppermint lozenges and the carbolic and the whole of Sea View House receding gently from about him.

For a fortnight he lived in a fever. Miss Trewin came to the shop nearly every day, for one thing or another—a sachet, a bottle of smelling-salts, a packet of court-plaster for her aunt’s heel—and Cuthbert, with the recklessness of a man who has already failed his second M.B., embroidered his circumstances until the embroidery became a tapestry. He spoke of his wards at “the hospital”; he spoke of a difficult case of compound fracture that had been brought in on the very night before he left town; he alluded darkly to a Professor Harkness, a great surgeon, who had told him privately that he had the finest pair of hands in the school. Miss Trewin listened with her head on one side, and Mr. Gammon, from the back room, listened with his side-whiskers fairly bristling, and said nothing, but polished the counter with a duster in a manner that boded no good.

The catastrophe arrived, as such catastrophes do, upon a Saturday afternoon. Cuthbert was alone in the shop, arranging a tray of tooth-brushes in an attitude of professional abstraction, when the door opened and there entered a young man of about two-and-twenty, in a grey flannel suit and a straw hat with a Guy’s Hospital ribbon, who said, cheerfully, “Hullo! You’re the chap that’s been filling my sister’s head with all this medical rot. My name’s Trewin. I’m at Guy’s. What’s yours?”

Cuthbert said his name was Peele.

“Peele,” said the young man, seating himself on the edge of the counter and swinging his leg. “Peele. Don’t know the name. What year are you?”

“Second,” said Cuthbert, faintly.

“Second!” said Mr. Trewin. “Rot. I’d have heard of you. Who’s your dresser?”

“My dresser?”

“Yes, your dresser. What ward are you in?”

Cuthbert, goaded, mentioned the name of a ward at random.

“That’s a surgical ward,” said Mr. Trewin, with the air of a man laying down a winning card. “Who’s the surgeon in charge?”

There was a pause. The peppermint lozenges seemed to be melting in the air. Cuthbert opened his mouth and shut it again. Then he said, with a dignity that he felt was crumbling about his ears, “I am not at Guy’s. I am at St. Bartholomew’s.”

“Oh, are you?” said Mr. Trewin. “Then what are you doing behind a chemist’s counter at Bexhill?”

And there it was. Cuthbert looked at the tooth-brushes, and the tooth-brushes looked at Cuthbert. He had a vision of Miss Trewin’s head on one side, and of the fifteen shillings a week, and of Mr. Gammon’s duster. He said, “I have failed my examination. I am here because my father will not pay for another. I told your sister a lie, and I am sorry for it, and I would be obliged if you would break it to her as gently as you can.”

Mr. Trewin stared at him for a moment with his mouth open. Then he burst out laughing—a great, hearty, young-man’s laugh that brought Mr. Gammon out of the back room like a jack-in-the-box, duster in hand.

“Upon my word,” said Mr. Trewin, “that’s the best thing I’ve heard this week. You’ve got more pluck than most of the second-year men I know. Look here, Peele, I’m not going to give you away. But you’ll have to tell my sister yourself, and you’ll have to tell her before her aunt gets hold of it, or there’ll be the deuce of a row. Come and have tea with us to-morrow at the boarding-house, and mind you bring the truth in your pocket along with the court-plaster.”

He went out, laughing still, and Mr. Gammon, who had heard everything, laid down his duster and said, “Well, Peele, I warned you about the ladies. But I will say this for you—you told him the truth when you might have gone on lying, and that’s more than most of them would do. You’ll stay on here, if you like, and I’ll raise you to eighteen shillings at Michaelmas.”

Cuthbert thanked him, and went to the window, and looked out at the sea, which was very blue and very innocent, and at the holiday-makers upon the Parade, and at a white parasol going down towards De la Warr Road. He thought of the tea to-morrow, and of Miss Trewin’s head on one side, and of the eighteen shillings, and it occurred to him that the truth, though a painful operation, is sometimes a good deal less painful than the disease. He went back to the tooth-brushes, and arranged them with a steady hand.
