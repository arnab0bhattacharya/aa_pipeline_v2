In the amber-lit drawing-room of Lady Pomeroy’s house in Mount Street, the world, or at least the more decorative half of it, was gathered to admire itself. The air was heavy with the scent of dying violets and the more persistent fragrance of social ambition. Duchess of Stowe, whose conversation was as scattered as her diamonds, was holding court near the piano, while Lord Alfred Fane stood by the fireplace, looking perfectly useless and therefore perfectly at home.

In a distant corner, somewhat hidden by the heavy velvet curtains, stood the Reverend Arthur Pendle. He was a curate of such profound shyness that he had once fainted at his own christening, or so his friends declared. He had come to Mayfair not to preach, but to tremble. He held a teacup as if it were a fragile bomb, and stared at the Persian rugs in the hope that they might open and swallow him. He was a young man of ordinary appearance, redeemed only by a pair of remarkably innocent grey eyes, which he kept fixed upon the floor, lest they should accidentally meet the gaze of someone who expected him to speak.

"Ah, Captain Bellamy!" cried Lady Pomeroy, sweeping across the room with the majestic certainty of a Mediterranean galleon. She bore down upon the trembling curate, trailing silks and absolute conviction. "I have been searching for you everywhere. They told me you had retired behind the curtains to avoid the crowd. How like a man who has faced the terrors of the Dark Continent to be frightened of a London drawing-room!"

The curate opened his mouth to protest, but he was a man of the cloth, and to correct a Duchess was a sin he had not yet been taught to commit. Before he could utter a syllable, he was swept into the very centre of the room, his teacup confiscated by a footman, and his destiny entirely rearranged.

"Ladies and gentlemen," Lady Pomeroy announced, tapping her fan against a marble table, "I present to you Captain Bellamy, the only Englishman to have crossed the Crimson Mountains of Upper Bongoland. You must tell us about the emeralds, Captain. The Bongo people, I am told, wear nothing but raw emeralds and crushed beetles. How perfectly barbaric! How utterly exquisite!"

The room drew a collective breath. Lord Alfred Fane looked up from his contemplation of his own cuffs. A young lady named Miss Arabella Vane, who had skin like white velvet and a heart like a particularly sharp lemon, leaned forward with sudden interest.

The Reverend Arthur felt the heavy gaze of Society upon him. It is a terrible thing to be misunderstood in Mayfair, but it is a fatal thing to remain silent. The curate took a breath, said a brief and entirely silent prayer for his own soul, and spoke.

"I fear," he said, in a voice that trembled like a leaf in a thunderstorm, "that the accounts have been somewhat exaggerated. The Bongo people, for instance, do not wear emeralds. They wear opals. I corrected the Chief on the matter myself. Opals, I pointed out, are far more appropriate for a tropical climate, as they contain all the colours of the sky and none of the heat of the ruby."

A murmur of delight rippled through the assembled guests. To correct a savage chief on his jewellery was the very height of British civilization.

"And the Crimson Mountains?" prompted Lady Pomeroy, settling herself upon a brocade sofa with the air of an Empress awaiting tribute. "Is it true they are composed entirely of red sandstone, and that at sunset they appear to be on fire?"

"They are not sandstone," the curate replied, finding, to his utter astonishment, a strange courage rising within him, much like a cork rises in water. "They are composed of a rare form of coral, pushed upward by a prehistoric earthquake. At sunset, they do not merely appear to be on fire; they are, in fact, quite genuinely aflame. The local flora secretes a phosphorescent oil, and the evening dew ignites upon contact with the rocks. It is a most inconvenient phenomenon for the traveller, as one cannot roast a pheasant without setting the entire horizon ablaze."

"How perfectly marvellous!" cried Miss Arabella Vane, her eyes wide. "And the natives? Did they not try to kill you?"

"The Bongo people," said the Reverend Arthur, warming to his subject with the terrible enthusiasm of a man who has realised that fiction is far more comfortable than fact, "are the most pacific race on earth. Their only form of warfare is the composition of satirical poetry. When I arrived in their capital, the King came out to meet me, not with a spear, but with a sonnet. It was a very clever piece of iambic pentameter, though he insisted upon rhyming 'England' with 'mango-land', which I felt was taking poetic licence too far."

"I should have thought," said Lord Alfred Fane, who had drifted over with the slow grace of a man who believes that gravity is somewhat vulgar, "that the chief danger in Africa was the wildlife. The lions, for instance."

The curate shook his head with the solemnity of a man addressing a funeral.

"The lions in the Crimson Mountains are entirely deaf," he said. "Owing to a peculiar disease of the inner ear, they are unable to hear their prey. They hunt entirely by smell. I survived my first encounter with a man-eating lion by the simple expedient of dousing myself in lavender water. The beast was so offended by the scent that it retired to a cave and wrote a monograph on the decline of British grooming."

The Duchess laughed, a sound like crystal breaking in a distant room. "You are quite extraordinary, Captain! And what of the famous Bongo emerald mines?"

"I am afraid," said the curate, adopting an expression of profound melancholy, "that the emerald mines are no longer in operation. The King closed them down. He had developed an aesthetic preference for moonstones. He said they reminded him of his first wife, who was pale, translucent, and slightly cracked."

A ripple of laughter swept the room, and the Reverend Arthur found himself the centre of an admiring circle. He had spent the last five years of his life preaching sermons to congregations of seventeen in a damp church in Hampshire, and not a single soul had ever laughed at his jokes. Now, here he was, in Mayfair, being celebrated as a hero of the Empire, simply because he had invented a race of poetical natives and a geological impossibility.

He continued, carried onward by the momentum of his own audacity. He spoke of the Crimson River, which flowed with water the colour of crushed strawberries; of the Bongo Temple of Aesthetics, where the priests worshipped a single perfect peacock feather; and of the Great Diamond of the South, which was so large that it was used as a doorstop by the King's chief minister. He described the sunsets as being "like a symphony in colours, only rather more expensive," and the flora as "mostly edible, though one preferred not to inquire too closely into its origins."

By the time he had finished, the entire room was in a state of rapturous enchantment. Lady Pomeroy declared him to be the most interesting man in London, which was, in her estimation, the highest compliment one could pay, as she found London entirely devoid of interesting men. Miss Arabella Vane looked at him with an expression that he recognised, from his reading of novels, as either deep admiration or a mild form of indigestion.

It was Lord Alfred, however, who delivered the fatal blow.

"My dear fellow," he said, lighting a cigarette with the air of a man performing a sacred rite, "the Geographical Society is looking for someone to lead an expedition to the source of the Crimson River. They have been searching for months, but all the candidates are either too brave or too boring. You, on the other hand, are clearly neither. Will you accept the commission?"

The Reverend Arthur felt the blood drain from his face. He looked around the room, at the expectant faces, the glittering chandeliers, and the Persian rugs that had so signally failed to swallow him. He thought of his damp church in Hampshire, of his seventeen parishioners, and of the sermon he was supposed to preach on Sunday about the evils of deceit.

"It would be an honour," he heard himself say, in a voice that did not appear to be his own.

As he left the house that evening, stepping out into the fog of Mayfair, he paused beneath a gas lamp and looked up at the sky. The stars were hidden behind a veil of smoke and cloud, as if the heavens themselves were embarrassed on his behalf.

"I have been mistaken for a man of action," he murmured to himself, "and I have behaved like a man of letters. I wonder which is the greater sin."

He walked home through the empty streets, his coat collar turned up against the damp, his mind filled with visions of crimson mountains, deaf lions, and a race of people who wrote satirical poetry. He had, he reflected, created an entire world out of nothing but his own terror and a passing acquaintance with the works of Mr. Ruskin.

It was, he thought, the most successful sermon he had ever preached.

In the cold light of the following morning, however, a letter arrived from the Geographical Society, confirming his appointment and requesting a preliminary report on the topography of the Crimson Mountains. The Reverend Arthur read it twice, folded it neatly, and placed it upon the table.

He looked at his reflection in the mirror. He was still a curate. He was still shy. But he was also, apparently, an explorer.

"I suppose," he said, addressing his own image with the quiet resignation of a man who has accepted his fate, "I shall have to discover the place now. It is the only honest thing to do."

And so, in the spring of that year, the Reverend Arthur Pendle set sail for Africa, carrying with him a Bible, a compass, three bottles of lavender water, and a profound sense of the ridiculous.
