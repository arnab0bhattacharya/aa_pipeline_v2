The morning-room of Lord Ashcombe’s house in Mayfair was a symphony in ivory and old gold, and Lady Ashcombe herself, seated at the writing-table, was the most exquisite note in it. She had the air of a woman who has stepped out of a portrait by Gainsborough and has never quite forgiven the century for having moved on. Before her lay a letter, and the letter was the only thing in the room that was not in perfect taste.

“It is from the committee of the St. Cyprian’s Home for Indigent Milliners,” she said, without raising her eyes. “They entreat me to speak the epilogue at their charity matinée. They say—they are very flattering—that no one who has not heard me speak the epilogue has truly lived.”

Lord Ashcombe, who was standing at the window with his back to the room, did not turn. He was a man of excellent profile and of opinions to match: both were best appreciated from a distance. “And you have refused them, of course.”

“I have accepted them.”

Now he turned. “Margaret.”

“It is one afternoon,” she said. “One afternoon, and a very indifferent epilogue, and a great deal of money for the milliners. The milliners, Ashcombe, are in want. It is almost the only want that society still permits them.”

“You are Lady Ashcombe.”

“I am aware of it. I was reminded of it at dinner last night, when the woman on my left spent the soup in telling me so. She had the face of a woman who has never been anyone, and the manner of one who intends to make up for it. She said that a great name is a great trust. I said that a great name is a great bore, and she has not spoken to me since. I regard it as the one triumph of the evening.”

Lord Ashcombe came away from the window and stood over her, and his voice, when he chose, could be as cold as the silver on his dressing-table. “You will write to the committee and tell them that you are indisposed. You will send them a cheque. You will not appear.”

“I shall appear,” said Lady Ashcombe, dipping her pen, “and I shall send the cheque as well. One should always be generous when one is being disobedient. It lends the whole affair a certain style.”

He looked at her for a long moment. “If you do this thing,” he said at last, “you do it alone. I shall not be in the theatre. I shall not receive you when you return. And society, Margaret—society will not be so kind as I am. Society has a memory for these things. It will cut you, and it will cut you in the most public manner it can contrive, because it is only in public that society is ever sincere.”

“Then I shall be cut in excellent company,” she said, “for I shall be cut by the very people who have been begging me for years to come back. Do you know, Ashcombe, I have a theory that the only thing society cannot forgive is the person who takes it at its word.”

He left the room without another syllable, and the door closed behind him with that restrained and awful click which is the aristocrat’s way of slamming.

The theatre, on the afternoon of the matinée, was a garden of hats and diamonds, and the air was heavy with that peculiar fragrance which arises when a great many respectable people have come together in the belief that they are doing good. The house was full, and the full house was, in the most literal sense, divided against itself. In the stalls the older families sat with their faces composed into that expression of Christian resignation which is the polite substitute for a verdict of guilty. In the boxes the younger set had come to see, not the epilogue, but the scandal, and had dressed for the latter.

Lady Ashcombe, in the wings, heard the murmur of it and smiled. She had known the murmur since she was seventeen. She had heard it first in a provincial town, when she was a girl with a name nobody could spell, and it had said then, quite clearly, that she would never do. She had done. She had done so well that the people who said she would never do had paid a guinea apiece to say it again in the stalls. It is the great advantage of the stage that it permits one to be avenged upon one’s enemies while appearing merely to entertain them.

She went on. She wore a gown of pale sea-green, and she moved across the stage as though the boards had been laid down expressly for her, which, in a sense, they had. She spoke the epilogue—a trifle by a fashionable poet, all roses and regret—and she spoke it with that exquisite and slightly mocking pathos which was her peculiar gift, so that the stalls forgot to be resigned and the boxes forgot to be amused, and for three minutes the whole theatre was one audience, which is the only miracle the modern world has ever really believed in.

When she had finished there was a silence. It was a silence of the most dangerous kind, for it was a silence in which a great many people were deciding what they thought. And then, from the upper gallery, where the gallery had paid a shilling and had no reputation to lose, there came a single shout of “Brava!” and the gallery, having no reputation to lose, shouted again, and the pit took it up, and the pit is always right about these things, and the whole house rose in a storm that beat against the boxes and made the chandeliers tremble.

In the third box on the left, the Countess of Mundesley turned to her neighbour with a smile of perfect sweetness. “I think,” she said, “that we had better go. One does not wish to be seen applauding, and one cannot very well be seen not applauding. It is the awkward position of the respectable.”

“But she was magnificent,” said her neighbour, a young man of no importance and considerable honesty.

“She was,” said the Countess. “That is precisely the grievance. If she had been dull we could have forgiven her everything. But to be dull is a duty, and to be brilliant is a defiance. She has defied us, and she has done it beautifully, which is the one thing we cannot forgive. Come. We will cut her at the Harcourts’ on Thursday, and by Christmas we shall be asking her to open our bazaars. It is the way of the world, and the world, my dear Arthur, is very like a woman—it can refuse to speak to you, but it cannot resist a gown.”

At the back of the stalls, a solitary figure in a grey frock-coat had stood throughout the applause, and had not applauded. Lord Ashcombe waited until the curtain had fallen and the house had begun to buzz, and then he went out into the street, where the afternoon was fading in a long wash of amber and rose, and stood for a while looking at nothing in particular.

She came to him in the hall at Mayfair at seven o’clock, still in the sea-green gown, with the flowers the gallery had sent her in her arms. He was standing by the fire, and he had a glass in his hand, and he did not put it down.

“You were in the theatre,” she said. “I saw you at the back of the stalls. You stood up, Ashcombe. You did not applaud, but you stood up. I saw you.”

“I stood up,” he said, “because it is the only way I know of leaving a theatre without being observed.”

“You stood up,” she said, “because you are my husband, and because, in spite of everything, you have a great deal too much taste to sit down. I forgive you the glass, and the fire, and the speech about society. But I shall never forgive you the grey frock-coat. It was a public occasion, and you looked like a man who had come to collect the rent.”

He set the glass down, and for the first time that day he laughed. “Margaret,” he said, “you are the most impossible woman in London.”

“I am,” she said. “And you have married me, which makes you either the bravest man in London or the most foolish. I have never been able to decide which, and I have been married to you for eleven years.”

“And the milliners?” he said.

“The milliners,” said Lady Ashcombe, laying her flowers upon the table, “are richer by a hundred and forty pounds, and I am richer by a hundred and forty friends who will cut me on Thursday and dine with me on Friday. Society has decided, Ashcombe. It has decided that it cannot approve of me and cannot do without me, and it will spend the rest of the season trying to reconcile the two. It is the only decision society ever really makes, and it makes it about everyone who is worth making it about.”

She went upstairs, and the sea-green gown went with her, and Lord Ashcombe stood alone in the ivory and old-gold room and looked at the flowers, which were somewhat extravagant, and at the fire, which was somewhat too warm, and at his own reflection in the glass, which was somewhat too pleased. “Upon my soul,” he said softly, to nobody in particular, “I believe the woman is right.” And he rang for dinner, and went up to dress.
