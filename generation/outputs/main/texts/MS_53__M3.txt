It was in the autumn of the year 2098, when the pestilence had already marked the fair face of England with the unmistakable scars of mortality, that I first journeyed to the town of Atherstone in the Midlands. I had been summoned from the hospitals of the decaying capital, where the dead lay stacked in the very halls where I, a young physician, had once hoped to learn the art of saving life, to investigate a phenomenon that was drawing the terrified and the dying from their homes. A prophet, they said, had arisen in that beleaguered town, one who claimed to hold the sole key to salvation, and who gathered the afflicted into his sanctuary, promising them immunity from the plague. My own heart was heavy with a sorrow that no words can adequately express, for I had witnessed the ravages of the disease too closely to believe that any mere human agency could arrest its progress. I had watched the fever consume the strongest frames, and had seen the light of reason extinguished in eyes that had but lately sparkled with health and intelligence. To me, the pestilence was a physical evil, a mystery of nature to be unravelled by the careful observation of symptoms and the cautious administration of remedies; and I went to Atherstone with a heart burning with indignation against the impostor who, I believed, preyed upon the fears of the suffering multitude.

The journey itself was a passage through a landscape of desolation. The fields, once so fertile and green, lay neglected, their harvests rotting unclaimed, for the labourers had fled or perished. The roads were thronged with wretched figures, some seeking a refuge they knew not where, others making their way, with a desperate and fanatical hope, towards the same destination as myself. The sky was overcast with clouds that seemed to press down upon the earth like a funeral pall, and as I approached the town, a storm arose, the violence of which appeared to echo the tumult of the human spirit in its agony. The lightning played upon the ruins of abandoned farms, and the wind howled through the leafless trees, bearing, or so my excited imagination whispered, the faint and mingled cries of those who, even at that hour, were yielding up their lives to the destroyer. I was alone in my small carriage, and the solitude of my situation weighed upon me with a force that I had not anticipated. I reflected upon the vanity of human endeavours, upon the fragility of all earthly attachments, and upon the awful silence that was settling over a world that had once been so full of life and activity.

Atherstone, when I reached it, presented a spectacle of strange and melancholy interest. The town was not wholly deserted, but its streets wore an aspect of singular desolation, as if the shadow of death had paralyzed the very energies of commerce and domestic life. Yet, in the midst of this universal gloom, one building was thronged with people. It was the ancient church of Saint Edmund, a venerable pile of Gothic architecture, whose tower had for centuries stood as a landmark to the surrounding country. Hither the followers of the prophet resorted, and hither I directed my steps, my mind made up to confront the deceiver and expose his falsehoods to the wretched beings who had placed their trust in him. I forced my way through the crowd that surrounded the doors, and entered the nave. The interior was dimly lighted by tapers, whose flames flickered in the draughts that swept through the broken windows. The air was heavy with the odour of incense and the sighs of the multitude. Upon a raised platform at the eastern end stood a figure, and upon him all eyes were fixed with an intensity of gaze that I had never witnessed before. This was the prophet, Selwyn.

He was a man of middle age, tall and slender, with a face that bore the unmistakable marks of intense mental suffering and spiritual exaltation. His hair, which fell in dark masses upon his shoulders, was streaked with grey, and his eyes, deep-set and luminous, burned with a fire that was not of this world. His attire was simple, even to austerity, and his voice, when he spoke, was low and tremulous, yet it possessed a penetrating quality that carried every word to the farthest corner of the edifice. He was not, as I had expected, denouncing the wrath of heaven in terms of theatrical ferocity; rather, he was pleading with his hearers, with a tenderness that moved me in spite of myself, to turn from the vanities of the world and to seek refuge in the purity of the soul. The pestilence, he declared, was a punishment for the sins of humanity, for the pride and the selfishness and the cruelty that had so long held sway over the earth. Only those who repented, who cast aside the burdens of their worldly desires and gave themselves up wholly to the service of the divine, would be spared. He spoke of his own visions, of the nights he had passed in solitary communion with the Almighty, of the revelations that had been vouchsafed to him. He spoke of them not with the confidence of one who boasts, but with the awe and humility of one who has been visited by a power greater than himself and who trembles beneath the weight of the message he is called upon to deliver.

As I listened, my indignation began to give way to emotions I had not anticipated. I had come to expose a charlatan, a trickster who turned the misery of others to his own account. But what I found was a man whose sincerity was written in every line of his countenance, in every cadence of his voice. I looked around at the congregation, and I saw that they were not the ignorant and the superstitious alone who had gathered there. There were faces of intelligence and refinement, of men and women who had known better days, who had been respected in their communities, and who had been driven to this refuge by the sheer desperation of their circumstances. They listened to Selwyn with a rapt attention, and when he paused, a murmur of assent and of tearful gratitude arose from the throng. I felt a pang of compassion such as I had not experienced since the beginning of the pestilence. These people were not deceived; they were comforted. And in a world where comfort had vanished, where science and philosophy had alike proved powerless to arrest the progress of the destroyer, was it not a kind of wisdom, a kind of grace, to offer them the only solace that remained?

When the service was over, I sought an interview with Selwyn. He received me in a small room adjoining the church, a room that was almost bare of furniture, and that served, it appeared, as his study and his sleeping chamber. He looked at me with a gaze that was at once searching and gentle, and I felt that he read my thoughts before I had uttered a word. I told him who I was, and I told him, with a directness that surprised even myself, that I had come to Atherstone with the intention of proving him an impostor. He heard me out without interruption, and when I had finished, he smiled, a smile of such infinite sadness that it went to my heart. He told me that he had no desire for power or fame, that he had been drawn from his obscurity by a call he dared not disobey, and that he knew, even as he spoke to the multitudes, that his words could not arrest the physical progress of the disease. He did not claim to work miracles. He believed, with a conviction that was unshakable, that the pestilence was a divine punishment, and that the soul that turned to God would be spared in a sense that transcended the mere preservation of the body. He had seen, he said, the dying pass away in a state of peace and exaltation that was a far greater miracle than any physical cure. And he had seen, too, the worldly and the self-seeking perish in agony and despair, as if the very pestilence were a mirror that reflected the state of the soul.

I left Atherstone before the dawn of the following day, for I found that I could not remain without a risk of being drawn into a vortex of emotions that I, as a man of science, was not prepared to encounter. As I journeyed back through the storm-swept country, I reflected upon all that I had seen and heard, and I felt that the foundations of my own certainty had been shaken. I had gone to expose a fraud, and I had found a faith. I had gone to unmask a hypocrite, and I had found a man whose sincerity and whose suffering were greater than my own. The pestilence continued its ravages, and Selwyn himself, I learned afterwards, fell a victim to it within the year. But the memory of that night in the ancient church of Saint Edmund, and of the conversation that followed, has remained with me as one of the most profound and disturbing experiences of my life. I had sought to bring light into a dark place, and I had found, instead, that the darkness was illuminated by a fire whose source I could not fathom, and whose meaning I could not wholly grasp.
