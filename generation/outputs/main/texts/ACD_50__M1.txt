It was upon a raw and gusty morning in the October of 1892 that my friend, the investigator Alistair Finch, and I were summoned to Richmond. The telegram had been brief and urgent, sent by a Mrs. Beatrice Ashworth, and it spoke of a sudden tragedy in her household. As our hansom rattled over the bridge and into the leafy precincts of the town, Finch, who had been studying the grey scud of the clouds, turned to me with a sharp, inquisitive glance.

“A stroke, it seems, and a mystery attached,” he murmured. “The two often travel in company, my dear fellow. A sudden shock to the system, and then the silence which must be interpreted.”

The house, named ‘The Cedars’, stood a little back from the road, a substantial villa of grey stone half-hidden by a screen of ancient yews. We were shown into a drawing-room where the air was heavy with the scent of hothouse flowers and anxiety. Mrs. Ashworth rose to meet us. She was a handsome woman of perhaps forty, but her face was pale and her fine eyes were clouded with a deep and settled distress.

“Mr. Finch, I am grateful you could come so quickly,” she said, her voice low but steady. “It is my husband, Captain Ashworth. They say it is a seizure of the brain, a stroke of apoplexy. He is… he cannot speak, cannot move. But it is the cause of it, sir, that I cannot bear. He was in his study yesterday afternoon, perfectly well. I heard a cry—a single, sharp exhalation—and then a great crash. When I ran in, he was slumped in his chair, his face quite grey.”

“And the physician?” Finch asked, his gaze sweeping the room, noting the fine Persian rug, the silver on the mantel, the half-finished embroidery on a side table.

“Doctor Macleod has been. He confirms the diagnosis and says only that time and rest may mend him. But he is wrong, Mr. Finch. My husband is a man of iron constitution. He spent fifteen years in the silver mines of Mexico. A little paperwork would not fell him. Something he saw, or something he read, struck him down. I must know what it was.”

She led us from the room and along a quiet corridor to a heavy oak door at the rear of the house. “This was his study. I have left it exactly as it was.”

The room smelled of old leather, Turkish tobacco, and something else—the dry, faintly spicy scent of the American continent. One wall was lined with books on geology and engineering. On another hung a collection of curios: a silver-inlaid saddle, a strange woven blanket of brilliant colours, and several pieces of worked silver. The desk, a vast mahogany affair, was the centre of the room. Upon it lay the singular items of which Mrs. Ashworth had spoken.

Finch approached the desk with the reverence of a priest at an altar. He did not touch, but merely observed. There lay a single sheet of paper, covered in a bold, spidery script, and beside it, a sketch on cheap artist’s paper, executed in charcoal. It depicted a church, a fine example of Spanish colonial architecture with twin bell-towers and a great carved doorway.

“A letter in Spanish,” Finch murmured, his eyes tracing the lines of the script. “And a drawing. Where is the envelope, madam?”

“There was none. It was brought by hand, I think. The housekeeper, Mrs. Gable, said a foreign-looking man, a labourer by his clothes, gave it to her at the back door yesterday afternoon. He would not leave his name.”

Finch bent low over the letter. “My Spanish is a little rusty, but I can make out the sense. ‘To Señor Capitán Ashworth… from your old friend, Miguel… I have found it. The place you dream of. San Miguel de las Almas. The church of the three bells is as you drew it. The silver is still there, my friend. Come quickly. Your fortune awaits beneath the stone of the saint.’ It is signed ‘Miguel Rios’.”

He straightened up and turned his attention to the sketch. “Captain Ashworth’s own hand, would you say, Mrs. Ashworth?”

“Oh, yes. He was a fine draughtsman. He sketched all the old missions he could find. He often talked of one, a place he called the ‘church of the three bells’, where he and a partner had buried a rich seam of ore before the troubles forced them to flee. He always spoke of it as a lost dream.”

Finch picked up a magnifying glass from the desk and examined the charcoal drawing with intense concentration. “A lost dream, you say. And this letter has found it for him again. Look here, Watson,” he said, gesturing me closer. “Look at the drawing of the bell-tower on the left. What do you see?”

I peered at the sketch. It was a competent, detailed rendering. “I see a bell-tower, Finch. A cross at its apex. What am I to look for?”

“Not what is there, but what is not. The style is all of a piece, save for one detail. This cross. The lines are heavier, more crudely drawn than the rest. The charcoal is deeply impressed into the paper. It was added last, and with a different intent.”

He then turned his glass upon the Spanish letter. “And the letter itself. The hand is that of an educated man, but see how the word ‘San Miguel’ is written. The ‘M’ is formed in a most peculiar fashion, with a sharp, angular first stroke. Now, look at the signature. ‘Miguel Rios’. The ‘M’ in Miguel is quite different—a soft, rounded curve. The same man did not write the body of this letter and the signature.”

Mrs. Ashworth stared at him. “But what does it mean?”

“It means, madam, that this is a forgery. A clever one, designed to appeal to your husband’s oldest and fondest ambition. But it is a lie. The man who wrote it, or rather, had it written, is no friend ‘Miguel Rios’.”

“Then who? And why would such a lie strike him down?”

Finch paced the room for a moment, his hands clasped behind his back. “Let us consider the facts. Your husband left Mexico a wealthy man, but he left in a hurry, did he not? He and his partner.”

“Yes. There was a dispute with a local syndicate. A violent one. His partner, a Mr. Croft, was killed in a skirmish. Edward escaped with his life and what he could carry.”

“Croft,” Finch repeated, the name hanging in the air. “And this ‘Miguel Rios’ was an old friend, you say? A trusted associate?”

“Edward never spoke of him by name, but I gathered he was a guide, a man who knew the mountains.”

Finch stopped before the wall of curios. His eyes fell upon a small, framed photograph of two men standing before a mine entrance. One was a younger Captain Ashworth, his arm around the shoulder of a man with a hard, sallow face and a silver-capped tooth that glinted in the sun.

“This man,” Finch said, pointing. “Is this the partner, Croft?”

Mrs. Ashworth leaned in. “Yes, that is he. Thomas Croft.”

“Look closely at his mouth, Watson,” Finch commanded. “The silver tooth. Now, look at the signature on the letter. The ink is fresh, but see how the pen has caught on the paper just after the ‘s’ of Rios? A tiny, regular splatter. It is the mark of a man who writes with a silver-capped pen, or perhaps whose hand is not entirely steady.”

He turned to face us, his expression grim. “The letter is a decoy. The true message is in the sketch. It is not a map to a treasure. It is a threat. The crudely drawn cross upon the church is the symbol of a grave, is it not? The message is not ‘come and find your fortune’, but ‘I have found you, and I have come to settle the account of Thomas Croft’.”

A gasp escaped Mrs. Ashworth’s lips. She sank into a chair.

“The shock was not from joy,” Finch concluded, his voice quiet and certain. “It was from terror. Captain Ashworth looked upon that sketch and understood that a man he thought long dead, or a relation of that man, knew his whereabouts and his past. The news did not overwhelm him with hope, madam. It struck him down with the cold dread of a ghost returning to claim its due. The question is not what news your husband received, but rather, who has brought it, and what they intend to do now that the message has been delivered.”
