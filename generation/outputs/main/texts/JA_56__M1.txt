It was a source of considerable surprise to the Reverend Mr. Edmund Vance that he should be called upon to advise any person of consequence on the subject of reading, and a source of no little consternation that the person in question should be Miss Isabella Thorne. Mr. Thorne, a gentleman of unimpeachable standing and unshakeable opinions, had taken it into his head that his daughter’s mind, though amiable, was insufficiently furnished, and had determined that the clergyman of the parish was the proper man to remedy the deficiency. The clergyman, whose admiration for Miss Thorne had been a carefully guarded and entirely private sentiment for the better part of two years, could think of no reply that did not betray a degree of agitation quite unsuited to his calling.

“I am convinced, Vance,” Mr. Thorne had declared, settling his bulk more firmly in the armchair at Delacourt Hall, “that a young woman’s reading is a matter of the first importance. It forms the character. And Isabella, bless her heart, has been left too much to the whims of circulating libraries and the chatter of her mother’s acquaintance. She requires a firm, scholarly hand to guide her.”

To be described as a firm, scholarly hand was flattering to Mr. Vance’s professional vanity, but to be tasked with guiding Miss Thorne felt less like a professional duty and more like a divine test of his fortitude. He had pictured her, on occasion, as the subject of a sermon on feminine virtue; he had composed imaginary letters to her in his mind; he had even, in moments of unguarded weakness, imagined a future in which she would read his own sermons with approval. He had never, however, imagined this: a series of weekly appointments in the library at Delacourt Hall, with Miss Thorne seated opposite him, and the collected works of the English moralists between them.

The first lesson began on a Tuesday in October. The library was a handsome room, filled with the scent of leather and dried lavender, and the afternoon light fell in a way that seemed designed to make its occupants look their best. Miss Thorne, who was generally considered the most accomplished young lady in that part of Hampshire, appeared to be labouring under a similar sense of occasion. Her posture was a model of propriety, her hands were folded neatly in her lap, and her gaze was fixed upon a point somewhere in the vicinity of Mr. Vance’s left ear.

“I have taken the liberty,” Mr. Vance began, his voice sounding strange to his own ears, “of selecting a work by Mr. Locke. His *Conduct of the Understanding* is, I believe, most instructive on the subject of forming sound judgements.”

Miss Thorne inclined her head. “You are very kind, Mr. Vance.”

“Not at all. It is my duty.”

There followed a silence of some duration. Mr. Vance, feeling that a pedagogical demonstration was in order, cleared his throat and began to read aloud a passage concerning the nature of prejudice. He was a man of good sense and a clear elocution, yet he found himself stumbling over the simplest words, his attention entirely consumed by the consciousness of his listener. He could feel her presence as one feels the heat of a fire, and his attempts to convey the rational coolness of Mr. Locke’s prose were severely compromised by a warmth that had very little to do with philosophy.

When he had finished, he looked up, expecting some sign of comprehension or, at the very least, of polite attention. Miss Thorne’s expression, however, was one of profound and unassailable serenity. It was the look of a person who has succeeded in thinking of nothing at all with great determination.

“And what, Miss Thorne,” he ventured, “do you understand from that passage?”

She appeared to consider the question with immense gravity. “That we should not be prejudiced, Mr. Vance.”

“Precisely. And how does Mr. Locke propose we achieve this?”

A slight flush crept into her cheeks. “By understanding, I suppose.”

The conversation proceeded in this vein for the better part of an hour. Each question from Mr. Vance was met with an answer of such perfect, unhelpful generality that it could not be wrong, yet served no purpose whatsoever. Each silence grew longer and more profound than the last. He had imagined a meeting of minds, a spirited and enlightening exchange; instead, he was conducting a catechism for a pupil whose only ambition was to be released from the examination. He felt himself to be a torturer of the most refined and deliberate kind.

On the following Tuesday, he arrived with a copy of *The Spectator*, hoping that its more conversational style might prove less intimidating. He found Miss Thorne in the same attitude of serene composure, though he fancied he could detect a new quality of resignation in her air. She had, he suspected, spent the preceding week steeling herself for the ordeal.

Their lessons, he soon discovered, were the talk of the neighbourhood. Mrs. Thorne, a lady who took a great interest in the formation of her daughter’s mind by proxy, accosted him after church one Sunday.

“We are so obliged to you, Mr. Vance! Isabella tells me your discussions are most improving. She was saying just the other day how much she admires your… your application to the subject.”

Mr. Vance could only bow, his face burning with a confusion that was part guilt and part indignation. He was engaged in a farce, and the entire parish was an appreciative audience. He was a man of God, tasked with leading a young soul to the light of reason, and he had succeeded only in plunging them both into an abyss of mutual mortification. He could not speak of his feelings; she could not admit her boredom. The very propriety of their arrangement was the instrument of their torture. To be alone together without a chaperone was unthinkable; to be anything less than formal was impossible. They were trapped by the very decorum that should have protected them.

One afternoon in November, as he was attempting to elucidate a particularly dense passage in Johnson’s *Rasselas*, a sudden and unexpected sound broke the silence. It was a small, stifled noise, half-way between a cough and a sigh. Mr. Vance looked up sharply. Miss Thorne’s face was averted, but her shoulders were trembling almost imperceptibly. For one dreadful moment, he thought she was weeping. Then, as she turned her head slightly, he saw the unmistakable truth. She was biting her lip, her eyes were bright with unshed tears, and the corners of her mouth were fighting a desperate battle against a smile.

The sight struck him with the force of a revelation. The entire edifice of propriety and pedantry, of scholarly effort and maidenly reserve, was suddenly revealed as the absurd and painful comedy it was. A wave of profound relief washed over him. He was not a tormentor; she was not a martyr. They were merely two people engaged in an elaborate and ridiculous charade.

He closed the book with a decisive snap. The sound made her look at him, her expression one of startled apprehension. But Mr. Vance, for the first time in weeks, felt entirely at his ease.

“I believe, Miss Thorne,” he said, a warmth entering his voice that had nothing to do with embarrassment, “that we have exhausted the wisdom of the moralists for today. Pray, tell me, what is your opinion of the new turnpike road they are proposing for the Salisbury road?”

Miss Thorne stared at him. For a moment, she seemed at a loss. Then, the tension in her face dissolved completely, and she smiled—a genuine, unrestrained smile that transformed her features from a study in propriety into a portrait of radiant good humour.

“Oh, Mr. Vance,” she said, her voice full of a light and airy relief, “I think it a very sensible notion, but I do pity the farmers whose land it will cross.”

They spoke of the turnpike road for the remaining half-hour, and of the price of corn, and of the follies of the new curate at the neighbouring parish. It was the most comfortable and enjoyable conversation Mr. Vance had ever had. When the time came for him to leave, he took his leave with a strange new feeling of hope. The great, unspoken difficulty between them had not been resolved, but it had been, for a moment, shared. And in that shared understanding, he felt, lay the possibility of a future far more agreeable than any to be found in the pages of a book.
