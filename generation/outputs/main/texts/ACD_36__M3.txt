It was upon a raw November evening, with a damp mist rolling in from the river, that I found myself accompanying Mr. Septimus Edge in the direction of Cloisterham Cathedral. The ancient city, nestled in a western shire, wore an air of somnolent melancholy, its cobblestones gleaming wet under the scarce gas-lamps. Edge walked with his hands clasped behind his back, his tall, angular form cutting swiftly through the fog.

"I dare say, Penhaligon," he remarked, his sharp grey eyes glancing sideways at me, "you are wondering why the Dean of Cloisterham should require the services of a private inquirer, rather than those of the local constabulary."

"I confess it crossed my mind," I admitted. "Cathedrals are hardly my usual beat."

"Nor mine," said Edge. "But the Dean is a man of delicate sensibilities. He desires the matter handled without a breath of public scandal. It appears the cathedral organ has taken to playing of its own accord, and the choirboys are in a state of mutinous terror."

"And that is all?"

"Hardly, Penhaligon. The ghostly music is but a curtain-raiser. The Dean suspects, though he cannot yet prove it, that the communion plate has been tampered with. He has reason to believe the ancient altar silver has been replaced with gilt copper."

We were admitted by a verger into the Deanery, a solid, red-brick house standing in the shadow of the great west front. The Dean himself, the Very Reverend Arthur Vivian, met us in his study. He was a tall, handsome man of perhaps fifty, with a clean-shaven, intellectual face, but his manner was flurried and his hands plucked nervously at his black silk apron.

"Mr. Edge," he said, "I am grateful that you have come so promptly. The affair is most distasteful. I am a man of peace, sir, and these worldly intrusions upon the sanctity of the Close are deeply unwelcome."

"You have the plate here?" Edge asked, dispensing with pleasantries.

The Dean unlocked a drawer and produced a velvet-lined case. From it he lifted a chalice and a paten, which he laid upon the desk. Edge picked up the chalice, weighed it in his palm, and drew a small magnifying glass from his pocket. He examined the hallmark, then scratched the underside of the base with a penknife. A thin line of copper showed plainly beneath the gold film.

"A clever piece of electrotyping," said Edge quietly. "The work is recent. How long has it been since the genuine plate was used?"

"At Michaelmas, six weeks ago," the Dean replied. "It is kept in the vestry safe, which is only opened upon high festivals. I discovered the substitution only yesterday, when I went to inspect the plate before Advent."

"And the organist? What of his ghost?"

The Dean’s brow darkened. "Mr. Silas Grew is a man of considerable musical talent, but of a nervous and excitable temperament. He resides in the organist’s lodgings adjoining the cathedral. He declares that upon three occasions in the past fortnight he has heard the great organ sound a low, sustained chord in the dead of night. He claims to have found the console empty and the blower engine cold."

"The choir, I gather, have embellished the tale?"

"Indeed. The boys will not enter the cathedral after dark. There is talk of the walled-up monk, a tiresome legend which I had supposed buried for a century."

Edge steepled his fingers. "I shall require access to the cathedral, the vestry, and the organ loft. I should also like a word with Mr. Grew."

It was past eleven o’clock when Edge and I let ourselves into the cathedral by a side door, of which the Dean had furnished us a key. The interior was vast, black, and profoundly still. Our footsteps echoed and died in the aisles. Edge led the way not toward the organ loft but toward the vestry, a panelled room behind the choir.

He examined the iron safe with a professional eye, testing the hinges and the lock. Then he turned his attention to the vestry floor, a chequered pattern of black and white marble. Dropping to his knees, he produced a small electric torch and played it along the junction of the stones.

"Here, Penhaligon," he murmured. I knelt beside him. At the base of the wall, behind a heavy oak chest, one of the marble slabs showed a faint but unmistakable accumulation of pale dust along its edge, and the mortar was cracked and slightly crumbled.

"This slab has been lifted recently," said Edge. "The dust has been swept back, but not with sufficient care. The crypt lies beneath this room, does it not?"

"The Dean mentioned an old passage connecting the crypt to the chapter house."

"Exactly. And the organ loft, I observed this afternoon, is accessible by a narrow spiral stair from the north transept, which is itself adjacent to the chapter house. The geography is becoming plain."

We made our way through the dark choir and up the spiral stair. The organ loft was a narrow platform, heavily shadowed by the great pipes. Edge sat down upon the bench and swept his torch over the console. He examined the stop knobs, the pedals, and the music rack. Presently he rose and crossed to a small tool cupboard fixed to the wall. Inside lay a tuning hammer, some lengths of wire, and a leather case.

Edge opened the case and gave a low exclamation. Inside, nested in green baize, was a small, heavy chalice of unmistakable workmanship, its surface dark with the patina of age. Beside it lay a paten of the same metal.

"The genuine altar plate," said he. "Replaced here while the gilt copies sit in the safe below. The thief grew cautious, perhaps, and moved his swag to a less obvious hiding place."

"But why leave it in the organ loft?"

"Because, my dear Penhaligon, the organ loft is the one place in the cathedral which Mr. Grew has a right to enter at any hour. The ghostly chords were not supernatural; they were a signal. The organist has been playing a note to indicate to an accomplice outside that the coast is clear, or that some rendezvous is arranged. The bellows need not be pumped for a single low chord to sound; the residual air in the wind-trunk is sufficient."

"But who——"

The question died on my lips. From the foot of the spiral stair came the sound of a footstep, slow and heavy. Edge snapped off the torch and drew me back into the shadow of the great pedal-pipes. We stood in absolute darkness.

A figure mounted the stair and stepped onto the loft. In the faint, diffused light from the clerestory windows I could make out a stout, square-built man with a heavy jaw. He moved with the assurance of one familiar with the place.

"That you, Grew?" a voice whispered, low and gruff.

There was a pause. Then, from the darkness behind me, Edge’s voice came, clear and even.

"No, Mr. Tope. But I have been expecting you."

The man started violently and swore. Edge’s torch clicked on, flooding the loft with white light. The figure was that of a thickset, red-faced man of fifty, wearing the seedy black coat of a minor cathedral official.

"Who are you?" the man demanded, retreating a step.

"My name is Edge. I am acting on behalf of the Dean. I think you have been removing the altar plate and substituting gilt copies, Mr. Tope. And I think Mr. Grew, your organist, has been assisting you by sounding a chord upon the organ to signal when the Close was quiet and the coast clear for your movements between the crypt passage and the vestry."

The man’s face went ashen. He stood glaring at Edge, his hands clenching and unclenching, but he did not speak.

"The game is up," Edge continued. "I found your tool-marks upon the vestry safe, and I have here the genuine plate, recovered from this cupboard. The marble slab in the vestry, which gives access to the crypt passage, was your highway. You are the cathedral verger, Mr. Tope, and you have had the run of the building."

It was the verger, then. The red-faced man stood trembling, all bluster gone out of him.

"It was Grew’s idea," he burst out, his voice cracking. "He said the plate would never be missed till Advent, and we could sell it in London. He played the chord to warn me when the night-watchman was about. The ghost story was his notion, to keep the boys away after dark."

"I thought as much," said Edge, producing a pair of handcuffs. "You had better come with me. The Dean will see you presently."

As we descended the dark stair, the great bell of the cathedral clock tolled midnight, its boom rolling through the empty nave like a solemn sentence.
