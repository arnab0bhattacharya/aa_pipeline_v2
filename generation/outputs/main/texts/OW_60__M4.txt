The Lady Araminta was, by her own impeccable estimation, the most distinguished ornament of the Royal Lake at Bellamont. She was a swan of the old blood, her lineage a matter of public record amongst the heraldic geese, and she carried her snowy plumes with the air of one who knows that a single feather from her wing would be worth more than a whole flock of common ducks. Her neck, a perfect column of ivory, was arched in a perpetual attitude of gracious condescension, and her eyes, which were of a cold and brilliant jet, surveyed the world with the serene disdain of a connoisseur examining a rather poorly executed piece of bric-à-brac. The lake, she was wont to observe to the reeds, was a tolerable enough piece of water, though dreadfully over-populated.

The population in question consisted chiefly of the ducks, a vulgar and gregarious tribe who inhabited the further, muddier shore. They were, to a bird, distressingly plain. Their quacks were a coarse dialect, their waddling gait an affront to the dignity of the water, and their obsession with the scraps of bread and biscuit flung by the children of Bellamont was, in Araminta’s opinion, nothing short of a scandal. She herself partook of these offerings only when they were of the finest quality, and then with a studied nonchalance, as if she were conferring a favour upon the giver. To see a duck, a mere dabchick of a creature, gobbling a crust that might have been hers was to witness a violation of the natural order.

One crisp December afternoon, as the pale sun made a brave but futile attempt to warm the frosty air, a small boy in a scarlet coat threw a handful of bread upon the water. The ducks, with their usual lack of decorum, rushed forward in a churning, quacking mob. Araminta, gliding in from the centre of the lake, fixed one of the foremost ducks—a stout, self-satisfied fellow named Crispin—with a look of such glacial reproach that a lesser bird would have sunk. Crispin, however, was made of sterner stuff.

‘I fancy,’ said Araminta, her voice a silken ripple, ‘that you have mistaken this lake for a trough.’

Crispin, his beak full, merely blinked his beady eyes. ‘The bread is for all, my lady,’ he mumbled, spraying crumbs. ‘The boy in the red coat does not ask for pedigrees.’

‘The boy in the red coat is a barbarian,’ she retorted, ‘and his taste in bread is as questionable as his taste in company. That crust is quite clearly meant for a more refined palate. It is an impertinence for you to intercept it.’

‘It is bread,’ said Crispin, with a maddening simplicity. ‘And it is cold, and we are hungry. You may have the lake to yourself in summer, my lady, and we shall not complain. But in winter, one must share.’

‘Share?’ The word fell from her beak as if it were a dead fish. ‘My dear creature, the very concept of sharing is a confession of failure. It is the creed of the mediocre. I do not share; I bestow. And I find I have nothing to bestow upon you.’ And with a contemptuous flick of her tail, she turned and glided away, a vision of affronted majesty, leaving the ducks to their plebeian feast.

The days grew shorter, and the air took on the keen, metallic edge of a true winter. The lake, which had been a mirror of shifting greys and greens, began to lose its fluidity. At first, a thin, brittle film of ice formed in the shallows, a delicate tracery of silver that the morning light turned to a thousand tiny diamonds. Araminta found it charming. It was a decorative flourish, a fitting setting for her own exquisite form. But soon the film thickened, and the diamonds were replaced by a dull, opaque sheet that crept, day by day, towards the centre of the lake.

The ducks, who had an unglamorous but practical understanding of such matters, began to congregate in a specific, unremarkable corner near a clump of half-submerged willows. There, a spring, unseen in summer, bubbled up from the lake-bed, keeping a small patch of water stubbornly, blessedly open. It was their secret, a piece of humble, life-saving knowledge, and they guarded it with a quiet, instinctual solidarity.

Araminta, however, was oblivious. She continued to patrol her shrinking domain, her pride a fortress against the encroaching cold. The ice now covered all but a small, central arena, and her movements became stiff and ridiculous. The other swans, less proud or perhaps more sensible, had long since flown to the warmer waters of the river, but she would not. To flee would be to admit that the lake was not, in fact, hers. She would endure. She would prevail.

Then came the great freeze. It descended in the night, a silent, remorseless conqueror. When dawn broke, pale and cheerless, the entire lake was a single, unbroken plane of steel-grey ice. Araminta awoke to find herself imprisoned. Her beautiful, webbed feet, designed for the elegant propulsion of water, were now clumsy and useless on the hard, slick surface. The world was silent, a vast, frozen tomb. A tremor of something she refused to name—for it was perilously close to fear—ran through her.

She saw them then. A huddle of brown and green feathers, a little way off, near the willows. The ducks. They were gathered around a small, dark patch in the ice, a patch that steamed faintly in the bitter air. They were taking turns, one or two at a time, slipping into the water for a brief, reviving moment before clambering out to let another take their place. It was an orderly, communal ritual of survival.

Araminta’s first impulse was to turn away. To seek their aid was to surrender everything she held dear. It was to admit that her lineage, her beauty, her impeccable taste, were of no more use than a painted fan in a hurricane. It was to concede that the bread on the water was, indeed, for all.

But the cold was a democrat. It did not care for bloodlines. It gnawed at her regal bones with the same indifference it did the ducks’. Her magnificent plumage, once a source of such pride, now felt like a heavy, icy shroud. She tried to move, to find some purchase, but her feet slid helplessly. A wave of profound, humiliating weakness washed over her.

Crispin, from his place by the open water, saw her. He did not laugh, nor did he gloat. He simply looked, his head cocked to one side. Then he waddled, with his absurd, ungainly gait, across the frozen expanse. He stopped a respectful distance away.

‘The water is still open,’ he said, his voice flat and without triumph. ‘Near the willows. It is warm enough to keep one alive.’

Araminta looked at him, her spirit warring with her frozen body. To accept his help was an unthinkable vulgarity. To refuse it was to die. For a long moment, she was silent, the wind whistling through her feathers. She thought of the bread, of her scorn, of the beautiful, useless diamonds of the first ice. And she understood, with a clarity as sharp and cold as the air itself, the comedy of her own pride. It was a joke, and she had been its butt.

‘I…’ she began, her voice a faint, reedy whisper. ‘I appear to have… miscalculated the season.’

Crispin gave a low, soft quack. ‘Seasons are like that,’ he said. ‘They do not consult the gentry.’ He turned and waddled back towards the dark, breathing patch of water. After a moment, the proud Lady Araminta, her head held not quite so high, followed him, her movements a clumsy, sliding parody of her former grace. She did not thank him. She could not. But as she slipped into the shockingly, mercifully cold water, she looked at the circle of ducks, and for the first time in her life, she saw not a mob, but a society.
