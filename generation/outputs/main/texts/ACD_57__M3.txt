It was upon the evening of the twelfth of November, in the year 1894, that I found myself accompanying Mr. Martin Hewitt to the residence of Lady Vexley in Belgrave Square. The matter had been placed in our hands but two hours previously, under circumstances which were as singular as they were pressing.

Lady Vexley was a widow of considerable standing, who maintained her household with an exacting precision that bordered upon the martial. It was her custom, upon the approach of the winter season, to host a select dinner, the invitations to which were invariably penned by her own hand upon a distinctive cream-laid paper, stamped with a coronet. It was this very custom which had precipitated the crisis.

"You must understand, Mr. Hewitt," her ladyship had said, when we were shown into the morning-room, "that I make it an unvarying rule to superintend every detail of my entertainments. Last Thursday, my housekeeper, Mrs. Pell, brought me the usual packet of accepted invitations which had been returned by post. Among them I found one addressed to a Colonel Sir Hubert Ffolliott."

Hewitt, who had been listening with that attentive stillness which I had learned to mark, inclined his head. "And you had not sent one to him?"

"I had not. What is more, the invitation was in my handwriting. I am not conscious of having written it, yet there it was—unmistakably my hand. I thought it some trivial error of memory, until this morning, when I received a note from Sir Hubert himself, confirming his attendance this evening."

"You are acquainted with the gentleman?"

"Very slightly. He was, some years ago, a figure of some note in the diplomatic service. But that is not the worst of it." She paused, and her composure faltered. "Sir Hubert Ffolliott was convicted of forgery three years since. He was sentenced to seven years' penal servitude."

Hewitt's brows drew together. "He is, then, at present in prison?"

"He should be. Yet he writes to me from his chambers in Jermyn Street, and he proposes to dine here tonight."

It was this extraordinary intelligence which had brought us to Belgrave Square at half-past seven, before the first carriage was expected. Lady Vexley conducted us to the dining-room, where the table was already laid for eight. The silver gleamed beneath the chandeliers, and the fire cast a warm reflection upon the panelled walls.

"Your household staff, Lady Vexley—has anyone left your employ recently?" Hewitt asked, his eye moving methodically over the table.

"My butler, Griggs, gave notice a fortnight past. He had been with me six years. I was somewhat surprised, for he had always seemed contented."

"And the paper upon which the invitations are written—it is kept where?"

"In the writing-desk in my morning-room. The desk is locked, but the key hangs upon a chain at my belt. I never remove it."

Hewitt turned to the window. The night was raw and foggy, and the gas-lamps in the square were haloed in yellow mist. "I think, Lady Vexley, that we shall do well to receive your guests as though nothing were amiss. I shall be present as a friend of the family. My colleague, Mr. Brett,"—here he indicated me—"will assist me. You will allow the dinner to proceed so far as the soup. After that, I must beg your indulgence."

The guests began to arrive shortly after eight. They were, for the most part, the predictable figures of fashionable society: a peer, an ambassador, a lady of literary pretensions. But it was Sir Hubert Ffolliott who commanded my immediate attention. He was a tall man, soldierly in bearing, with a close-cropped grey beard and keen dark eyes. His evening dress was immaculate, and he carried himself with an easy assurance which betrayed no consciousness of irregularity.

Lady Vexley received him with a composure which did her infinite credit. He bowed, murmured some conventional pleasantry, and passed into the drawing-room. I observed Hewitt, who had stationed himself near the doorway, follow the man with a gaze that was at once casual and penetrating.

"He has been in no prison," Hewitt whispered, as we withdrew to a corner. "His hands are too soft, his colour too high. But there is something else, Brett. Did you remark his cuffs?"

"I did not."

"French cuffs, with links of engraved gold. Upon the right cuff, near the edge, there is a faint discoloration—a brownish stain, as of some chemical reagent. And upon the third finger of his left hand, there is a tan-line where a ring has recently been worn and removed."

We took our places at the table. The soup was served. I found myself seated opposite Sir Hubert, and I devoted my attention to observing him whilst appearing to attend to the conversation of my neighbour. He ate with appetite, but his eyes moved restlessly from face to face. Once, I saw them linger upon a silver wine-cooler which stood upon the sideboard, and then dart away.

Hewitt, who was seated beside Lady Vexley, had engaged the ambassador in a discussion of some triviality. Presently, however, he turned to his hostess with a remark which I could not catch. Lady Vexley rose, murmured an apology, and left the room. The soup was scarcely finished when she returned, her face pale but composed.

"Gentlemen," she said, "I fear I must ask you to excuse me for a few moments. Mr. Hewitt, would you be so kind as to step into the morning-room?"

Sir Hubert's spoon halted midway to his lips. For an instant, his easy manner cracked, and I saw something hard and watchful settle behind his eyes. Then the mask was restored, and he resumed his meal.

In the morning-room, Lady Vexley turned upon Hewitt with an air of controlled agitation. "The writing-desk has been forced," she said. "The lock is broken. And the packet of invitation paper is gone."

Hewitt knelt before the desk and examined the lock with his lens. "Forced with a thin blade—a paper-knife, I should judge, from the width of the scratch. The work of someone familiar with the mechanism. Lady Vexley, this Griggs—your former butler—where is he now?"

"I do not know. He left no address."

"I think we shall find him before the evening is much older. And I think, too, that we shall find that he and Sir Hubert Ffolliott are better acquainted than appearances suggest." Hewitt rose and crossed to the window. "But the more pressing question is this: why should a man go to the trouble of forging an invitation to a dinner he could have no possible wish to attend, unless he desired access to this house?"

He turned sharply. "What is kept in this house, Lady Vexley, that a man of Ffolliott's particular talents might value?"

Lady Vexley hesitated. "My late husband was a collector of rare manuscripts. Among them is a folio of Persian miniatures, valued at some four thousand pounds. It is kept in the cabinet yonder."

Hewitt walked to the cabinet, a tall mahogany piece against the far wall, and examined the lock. "Opened with the same blade," said he, "and recently. The folio is gone."

We returned to the dining-room. The guests were still at table, the conversation flowing freely. Sir Hubert was holding forth upon some question of Balkan politics, his voice smooth and assured. Hewitt took his seat, leaned towards him, and spoke in a tone which carried no further than the two of them.

"Sir Hubert, the folio is missing, and your former butler, Griggs, is nowhere to be found. The lock of Lady Vexley's writing-desk bears the marks of a paper-knife, and the lock of her cabinet shows the same. I observe, upon your cuff, a stain which is consistent with the iron-gall ink used in the preparation of forged documents. And upon your hand, the mark of a ring which I fancy matches one described in the records of the Central Criminal Court."

The colour drained from Sir Hubert's face. His hand moved towards his pocket, but I had already risen, and the weight of my hand upon his shoulder was sufficient.

"It is over, Sir Hubert," said Hewitt, quietly. "The police are even now at the door."
