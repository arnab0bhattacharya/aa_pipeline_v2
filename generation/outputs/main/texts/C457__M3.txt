The letter came on a Tuesday, borne in the pocket of the carrier’s cart from Louth. It was addressed to Clara Sowten in a hand she had never seen before. It bore the Boston postmark. For three days she kept it on the mantelshelf unopened, because she did not recognize the sender’s direction — a Mr. E. Margesson of Bucklesby — and because her mother, Hettie, had been coughing badly again and required tending.

It was Hettie’s cough that decided the matter in the end. Sitting up with her through the small hours of Friday, the house quiet but for the labouring breath and the clock, Clara took the letter down and broke the seal. The paper was good quality. The handwriting was that of an educated man, though the letters sloped and cramped as if written in discomfort or in haste.

The letter was from her father.

He was not dead. He had not drowned at Hull in the year of her birth, as she had been told. He had left. He had gone to America and then come back, and now he was in Bucklesby, which was not fifteen miles away, and he was dying of a complaint of the lungs, and he wished to see his daughter before he went.

Clara read it twice and then a third time, kneeling by the range with the paper close to the heat. She did not cry. The feeling that came was something flatter and colder than grief — a sensation like pressing one’s hand against a wall and finding it hollow. She folded the letter and placed it inside her copy of Common Prayer and went back to her mother’s bedside.

She did not speak of it that night, nor the next morning. She performed her duties at the draper’s shop in Briggate as usual, measuring out flannel for a farmer’s wife, cutting bias binding, making up the account book. But all the while her mind was turning, turning, like the great wheel at the mill upstream.

On Sunday she told her mother.

They were in the kitchen of the cottage on Sowerby Lane. Hettie sat in the chair by the range, a shawl about her shoulders, her face thin and sharpened by illness into something birdlike and exact. Clara laid the letter on the table between them, smoothed flat.

Hettie looked at it and then looked away. She looked out of the window toward the bare alders that lined the lane. ‘Where did you get that?’ she said.

‘It came Tuesday past. I should have shown you sooner.’

‘You should have burned it.’

Clara waited.

Hettie’s hand went to her shawl and gripped it at the collar. Her knuckles were large for a woman’s hands, reddened, the nails broken short from work. ‘He has no right,’ she said. ‘Not after all this time. Not after what he did.’

‘You told me he drowned.’

‘I told you what was kinder.’

‘Kinder to whom?’

Hettie turned from the window. Her eyes were dark and direct, the same eyes Clara saw in her own reflection. ‘To you,’ she said. ‘To me. To us both. What good would the truth have done a child? That your father looked at the two of us one morning and decided he wanted no part of what we were? That he walked out of this cottage and onto a packet boat and left me to make shift with a newborn and the parish breathing down my neck? I told you he was dead because dead is what he was to us.’

Clara picked up the letter. The paper trembled, and she could not steady it. ‘He says he is ill.’

‘Good.’

‘Mother.’

‘I mean it. Let him die in whatever hole he has found for himself. He made his choice. He does not get to unmake it now that he is frightened of what is coming.’

Clara read aloud a portion of the letter, the part where he wrote that he had thought of her every day, that he had kept her name in his heart, that he knew he had no claim but only a hope. The words were plain. There was nothing grand or theatrical in them. He did not ask forgiveness. He asked only to see her face.

‘Pretty words,’ Hettie said. ‘He always had pretty words. That was how he got me to leave my father’s house. That was how he got me to come here, to this county, away from everyone I knew. And then when the pretty words ran out, so did he.’

Clara set the letter on the table again. She looked at her mother. ‘I want to go.’

Hettie’s face changed. The hardness did not leave it entirely, but something else came into it — something raw and unfinished, a wound that had never properly closed because it had never properly been examined. ‘Clara,’ she said. ‘Clara, I am asking you. I am your mother. I am the one who stayed. I am the one who fed you and clothed you and sat up with you through the fevers. I did not abandon you for a ship and a new life. Do not go to him.’

‘He is my father.’

‘He is nothing. He made himself nothing.’

‘Then why are you so afraid of my going?’

Hettie did not answer at once. She sat and gripped her shawl and looked into the fire. The clock ticked in the passage. Outside, a cart went past, the horse’s hooves muffled in the lane mud.

‘Because he will have a story,’ Hettie said quietly. ‘He will have his version of things, and he will tell it to you, and you will believe him, because you will want to believe him, because you are young and you still think that people who leave do so for reasons. And his story will be different from mine, and you will carry both of them inside you and not know which is true, and it will eat at you the way it has eaten at me.’

‘I am not a child.’

‘No. You are not. But you are my child.’

Clara stood. She took the letter and put it back into the prayer book. She went to the range and riddled the coals, though they did not need riddling. She set the kettle on. She did these things because her hands required occupation and because the kitchen required the ordinary motions of living to continue.

All that week she carried the letter inside the prayer book, and the prayer book inside her reticule, and she carried also the weight of two irreconcilable things. At the shop she served customers. At home she tended her mother. She ground the cough mixture in the mortar. She heated the poultices. She helped Hettie to bed and sat with her until the breathing steadied into sleep.

On Thursday, in the late afternoon, when Hettie was resting and the light was going amber over the fields toward the wolds, Clara walked out along the lane and took the road toward Bucklesby. She did not decide to go so much as she found herself going, her feet carrying her along the ruts and the frozen margins of the carriageway while the rest of her lagged behind, protesting.

It was a cold afternoon. The sky was the colour of lead. Rooks circled above a copse of elms to the east. She walked for an hour and then saw the church tower of Bucklesby rising above the low roofs, and she stopped.

She stood in the road and looked at the tower and the scatter of houses around it and the smoke rising from their chimneys, and she understood that somewhere in that small cluster of buildings her father was lying ill, and that she could be there within the quarter hour, and that she could sit beside him and look at his face and hear whatever it was he wished to say. She understood also that if she did so, she would carry it home with her into the cottage on Sowerby Lane, into the kitchen where her mother sat by the range, and that it would stand forever between them like the letter on the table, unanswerable.

She stood for a long time. The light failed. The rooks settled. A boy driving sheep called out to her, asking if she was lost, and she told him no, she was not lost, and she turned and walked home.

When she came in, Hettie was awake. She had not slept. Her eyes were on the door.

‘I did not go,’ Clara said.

Hettie looked at her. Her mouth worked. She turned her face away and said nothing, but her hand went out across the table, and Clara took it, and they sat like that, the two of them, in the dim kitchen, the fire settling, the clock marking the minutes that remained.
