If any unsuspecting traveller, journeying through the particular corner of Suffolk that lies between the market-town of Melford and the marshy edges of the Orwell, had found himself benighted in the village of Plumberry St. Peter, he might have supposed the entire female population to be in a chronic state of conspiracy. For the women of Plumberry St. Peter were bound by a vast, unwritten, and unbreakable treaty to worship at the shrine of Mrs. Toppam’s only son, Augustus. Mrs. Toppam was a widow of substance, meaning she possessed a comfortable cottage, a prolific kitchen-garden, and a tin box beneath her bed-chamber floorboard containing precisely one hundred and eighty-seven pounds in Bank of England notes and gold. Augustus was the apple of her eye, the lord of her existence, the undisputed monarch of her tea-urn, and a young gentleman of such unbounded promise that he could do no wrong, principally because she never permitted him the opportunity to try. He had a small, pale face, an exquisitely waxed moustache, and an aversion to honest exertion that he wore as proudly as a knight wears his crest.

‘He is all heart, is my Gussie!’ Mrs. Toppam would declare to the grocer, Mr. Pottle, who, being a man of acidulous temperament and much dyspepsia, would merely rub his spectacles and reply, ‘Ah, ma’am. All heart, and precious little head, I make no doubt.’ But Mr. Pottle was a bachelor, and what could bachelors know of the divine right of mothers?

It came to be, in the year of our Lord eighteen hundred and forty-something, that Augustus announced his intention to go up to London, to cut a figure, to enter the genteel professions, and—above all—to become a gentleman. Mrs. Toppam wept, and Mrs. Toppam smiled, and Mrs. Toppam opened the tin box, and Mrs. Toppam counted out one hundred and fifty golden sovereigns into a leathern wallet, pressing it into the hands of her idolised boy with a fervour that would have made an anchorite blush.

‘Go, my Gussie,’ said she, ‘and show ’em what Plumberry St. Peter can do! And don’t spare the porters, for a liberal hand bespeaks a generous heart!’

Augustus went. He was conveyed to the Imperial Metropolis by the heavy coach, and was deposited, with his leathern wallet and his waxed moustache, in the very heart of the Strand. London received him with open arms, and open pockets; it smiled upon his inexperience, it flattered his vanity, it introduced him to the most fascinating amusements, and it separated him from his mother’s savings with a dexterity and a thoroughness that would have been quite sublime, had it not been so extremely rapid. Within the brief, bright, and bustling period of exactly one year, Augustus Toppam was reduced to a solitary shilling, a soiled suit of clothes, and an unpaid bill at a boarding-house in Bloomsbury. His acquaintances—those sparkling, glittering, delightful companions who had assisted him in the patriotic work of spending—vanished like morning mist. The moustache drooped. The small, pale face grew smaller and paler.

He could not go back. He dared not go back. The idea of returning to Plumberry St. Peter, to the cottage, to the grocer, to the maternal tea-urn, with nothing but the confession of his folly, was a physical impossibility. He would rather, he felt, have been turned into a paving-stone and trodden on by hackney-coaches. He must work; but what could he do? He could not dig; to beg he was ashamed. He could, however—and here was the sole accomplishment of his London career—carry a tray. He could wear a dress-coat. He could bow with a profound and cynical servility. He could wait.

And so it was, by one of those singularly ironical arrangements in which a benevolent Providence occasionally delights, that Augustus Toppam found himself engaged, on wages of fifteen shillings a week and his board, as a waiter at the Grand Monarch Hotel, Charing Cross. The very hotel at which he had arrived, a twelvemonth before, in a post-chaise, with a leathern wallet; at which he had dined in the coffee-room, at the best table, commanding the street; and from which he had tipped the head-porter with a golden half-sovereign, merely for carrying his carpet-bag up one flight of stairs!

The Grand Monarch was a world in itself. It was a great, humming, bustling, steaming, chattering, jingling world; a world of gas-lights and plate-glass, of mahogany and crimson velvet, of strong cigars and stronger smells, of perpetual motion and perpetual hunger. It was a place where the very bell-handles seemed to be in a chronic state of nervous excitement, and the doors opened and shut with a feverish anxiety, as if they, too, were aware of the transient nature of human patronage. The halls were thronged with a motley procession of humanity: heavy dragoons, light comedians, fat widows from the country, speculators from Manchester, politicians with their pockets full of nothing and their heads full of crotchets, and young gentlemen from the Universities, who had come up to see life, and were making the most of it.

And in the midst of all this, moving with a silent, gliding step, a napkin over his arm, and a smile upon his face—a smile that was a mere muscular contraction, having no connection whatever with his heart—was Augustus Toppam. He carried the soup; he presented the fish; he filled the glasses; he received the commands of the very class of men among whom he had lately moved, and to whose remarks he now listened with a patient, wooden impassivity. He saw the golden half-sovereigns given to the porters, and he, Augustus Toppam, the late dispenser of such largesse, fetched the boots and carried up the carpet-bags.

He had one especial tormentor, in the shape of a stout, red-faced, consequential gentleman from the North, named Mr. Grimesby, who travelled on oil, and who patronised the Grand Monarch as if he had built it himself. Mr. Grimesby was a man of loud voice, loud waistcoats, and loud opinions; he bellowed for his dinner as if the hotel were on fire, and he found fault with a perseverance and a minuteness of detail that would have done honour to a Chancellor of the Exchequer.

‘Waiter!’ roared Mr. Grimesby, one evening, thumping the table till the glasses rang. ‘This turbot is cold, sir! Cold as a stone! And where’s the melted butter? D’ye think I can eat turbot without melted butter, sir? Bring it instantly, or I’ll report you to the manager!’

‘Yes, sir. Instantly, sir,’ said Augustus, with a low bend, and a smile of the most unimpeachable meekness. He glided away, and returned with the melted butter. Mr. Grimesby tasted it, and declared it was rancid. Augustus bowed, and fetched fresh. Mr. Grimesby then pronounced the bread stale. Augustus bowed again, and fetched new. There was no end to Mr. Grimesby’s caprices; he was a perfect volcano of dissatisfaction, and Augustus was the patient cinder-heap upon which he poured his lava.

But if Augustus suffered from Mr. Grimesby, he had his consolations. There was one guest—a mild, benevolent, white-haired old gentleman, from the country, who came up to London twice a year, and who always put up at the Grand Monarch. His name was Mr. Lilywhite; he was a retired hop-merchant, and he had a way of looking at the waiters, when he gave his orders, that was almost paternal. He was exceedingly particular about his tea; he liked it strong, and he liked it hot, and he liked it made in a little brown earthen pot, which he carried about with him in a shawl, as if it were a precious infant.

‘Ah, thank’ee, thank’ee!’ he would say, when Augustus brought it. ‘You’re the man that understands it, I see. You’re the man that knows what a cup of tea is, and what a body feels after a journey. What’s your name, my man?’

‘Toppam, sir,’ Augustus would reply, with a strange flutter at his heart, for he had a dim recollection that his mother had once spoken of a Mr. Lilywhite, who had been kind to his father.

‘Toppam—Toppam—’ repeated the old gentleman, musingly. ‘I knew a Toppam once. A Suffolk man. A good man. But that’s a long while ago. You’re not a Suffolk man, I suppose?’

Augustus’s face flushed, and then paled. ‘No, sir,’ he said, in a low voice. ‘No, sir. I am not.’ And he turned away, with the empty tray under his arm, and a bitter smile upon his lip, to answer the imperious summons of Mr. Grimesby, who was roaring for his bill.

But the hour of his humiliation was not yet complete. There came a day—a wet, disagreeable, squalid day in November—when the Grand Monarch was crowded to excess; the trains had brought up shoals of travellers, and the coffee-room was a very Babel of tongues and a very pandemonium of knives and forks. Augustus was tired, footsore, and dispirited; he had been on his legs since seven in the morning, and it was now nine at night. He had carried trays up-stairs, and trays down-stairs; he had been scolded by Mr. Grimesby, and he had neglected, for one brief moment, the paternal teapot of Mr. Lilywhite. He felt that he could bear no more; he felt that he must sink down upon the crimson carpet, and let the trampling world go by.

And then, as he stood, for a moment, in the shadow of the great staircase, his eye fell upon a figure that made his heart stand still. It was a woman—a woman in a black cloak and a plain bonnet, with a small umbrella, and a large bandbox; a woman with a pale, anxious face, and eyes that wandered wistfully from face to face, as if seeking someone they could not find. She was looking about her, in the bewildered, helpless manner of a countrywoman in a crowd; and she was holding in her hand a leathern wallet—a worn, old, leathern wallet, that Augustus knew as well as he knew his own right hand.

It was his mother.

She had come up to London, by Parliamentary, to find her Gussie. She had heard nothing from him for three months; the letters had ceased; the bright, hopeful, loving letters, full of the grand acquaintances he was making, and the great things he was doing. She had borne it as long as she could; and then, with a mother’s instinct, she had taken the thirty remaining sovereigns from the tin box, and had come up to town.
