The letter from Mr. Ellsworth came upon a Tuesday, and I remember that the rain fell without ceasing against the windows of our little house in Cheyne Walk, as though the heavens themselves conspired to deepen the gloom which that cruel epistle was to cast upon my spirit. My brother had waited for it with the feverish impatience of a man who has staked his all upon a single cast, and when at length the servant placed it in his hands, he bore it to the window and broke the seal with fingers that trembled so violently he could scarce accomplish the task. I watched him from my seat by the fire, and I saw the colour drain from his countenance as he read, and then a dreadful stillness came upon him, more terrible than any outcry, for it seemed as though the very springs of life had frozen within him.

“Edmund?” I said, and my voice sounded strange and hollow in my own ears. “What does he say?”

He turned to me, and in his eyes there was a look I had never seen before—a look of such utter desolation, such profound and hopeless misery, that my heart clenched within my breast as though a hand of ice had seized it. “He says,” my brother answered, and his voice was low and measured, as though he read from some ancient chronicle of death, “that my verses are the idle vapourings of a mind too weak to conceive a genuine thought, too shallow to sound the depths of human feeling. He says that I mistake mere facility of language for poetry, and that my images, which I had thought so fresh and luminous, are but the faded borrowings of greater men. He says—” here his voice faltered, and he pressed his hand against his forehead as though to still some tempest within—“he says that I should turn my attention to some honest trade, for nature never intended me for a poet.”

I rose and would have gone to him, but he motioned me back with a gesture so commanding that I stood rooted to the spot. “Do not,” he said. “Do not speak to me of consolation. There is no consolation. The oracle has spoken, and I am nothing.”

He stood there by the window, and the grey light of that melancholy afternoon fell upon his face, revealing lines which I had not marked before—lines of suffering and of bitter disappointment—and I thought that he looked suddenly older, as though the years had rushed upon him in a single moment and claimed their due. Then he turned and quitted the room without another word, and I heard his footsteps ascending the stairs, slow and heavy, like the tread of one who follows a funeral.

All that night I sat alone, and the rain beat against the panes, and the wind moaned in the chimney like a creature in torment. I thought of the long years of his labour, of the sleepless nights he had devoted to his art, of the hopes he had cherished and the dreams he had woven, and I wept for him as though he had died. For indeed something had died within him—that bright and ardent spirit which had animated his youth, which had made him the companion of my happiest hours, which had filled our little home with music and with light.

At dawn I went to his chamber, but the door was locked, and when I called to him he answered not. I waited in an agony of apprehension, and at length, when the sun had risen high and still he gave no sign, I summoned the servant and bade him force the door. We found my brother seated at his writing-table, pale and haggard, with the ashes of his manuscripts scattered about him like the relics of some burnt offering. He had destroyed them all—every poem, every fragment, every line that he had ever written—and the fire in the grate had consumed them utterly, so that nothing remained but a fine grey dust which the draught from the open window scattered through the room.

“Edmund,” I cried, “what have you done?”

He looked at me with eyes that seemed to see me not, but rather to gaze upon some vision of his own desolation. “I have done what I should have done long since,” he said. “I have burned my ships. There is no retreat now. I am no poet—the world has told me so, and the world is right. Henceforth I shall devote myself to science, to the pursuit of truth, to the accumulation of facts. The imagination is a snare and a delusion; it promises what it cannot perform, and leads only to disappointment and despair.”

I would have spoken, but he silenced me with a gesture. “Say nothing, Clara. Let me alone. I have much to do—much to undo. The past is dead, and I must bury it.”

From that day forward, my brother became a changed man. He abandoned his poetry utterly, and applied himself with a grim and joyless industry to the study of chemistry and natural philosophy. He rose before dawn and laboured far into the night, filling his journals with observations and experiments, and when I ventured to speak to him of the beauty of the sunset, or the music of the birds, or any of those things which had once delighted him, he would answer me with a coldness that chilled my very soul. “These are idle fancies, Clara,” he would say. “They profit nothing. The man of science has no time for such vanities.”

And yet—and this is the secret which I have kept locked within my breast, and which I shall keep until the grave claims me—I had saved his verses. When first I perceived the direction of his thoughts, and suspected that he meditated their destruction, I had secretly made copies of all that he had written, and these I concealed in a place which I knew he would never search. Often, in the silence of the night, when he was buried in his calculations and his experiments, I would take them out and read them, and weep over them, and wonder whether the world would ever know what it had lost. For I knew—I knew with a certainty that no critic’s verdict could shake—that these were not the idle vapourings of a shallow mind, but the true utterances of a poet’s soul, and that some day, when the bitterness of this hour had passed, they would find their proper place among the treasures of our literature.

But I spoke no word of this to Edmund. I dared not. The wound was yet too fresh, the disappointment too keen, and I feared lest, by revealing what I had done, I should only deepen his anguish and confirm him in his resolution. And so I held my peace, and watched him day by day grow more reserved, more solitary, more absorbed in those dark and dangerous studies which seemed to afford him the only consolation he could find.

Yet sometimes, when the evening light fell softly through the windows, and the labours of the day were done, I would see him pause at his desk and gaze out upon the fading sky, and a look would come into his eyes—a look of longing and of loss unspeakable—which told me that the poet was not dead within him, but only sleeping, and that perhaps, in some happier hour, he might yet awaken to claim his heritage. And I would pray, with all the fervour of a sister’s love, that such an hour might come, and that the verses which I had saved might prove, not a monument to his failure, but a prophecy of his triumph.
