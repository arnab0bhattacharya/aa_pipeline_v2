The light in the library was not one light but a thousand; a grey, watery substance that lay in the tall windows and fell upon the leather backs of the books, and upon Miss Vane’s hand as it moved, and upon the dust motes which were the only other inhabitants of the room. It was a substance, this light, rather than a quality, and it seemed to have soaked through the great stone walls of Alderthorpe Hall, to have been distilled by the Northamptonshire rain and the damp of the winter earth, until it possessed the very air she breathed. And in it, time did not pass but lay, like the dust, suspended.

Her work, which Mr. Ashworth—the new owner, a man of brisk, modern tastes who had bought the house as you might buy a motor-car—had called ‘a simple inventory’, was in fact an excavation. Her fingers, stained with the fine black dust of centuries, travelled along the spines, reading the titles not with her eyes but with their tips: the cracked morocco, the smooth calf, the frayed linen. *Sermons of Dr. Donne. The Compleat Angler. Paradise Lost.* But the books themselves were merely the outer shell of the thing. The real library, the one that mattered, was written in the margins.

She had found the girl first. A pale, spidery script in a volume of Herrick, pencilled so lightly it was almost a breath on the page. Beside *‘Gather ye rosebuds while ye may’*, the words: *But the roses are all gone, and I am seventeen, and it rains.* The pencil had pressed harder then, leaving a small, dark wound in the paper. Elara. The name had come to her unbidden, as names do, and it seemed to fit the delicate, disappointed hand. She felt she knew her; knew the way she must have stood at this very window, watching the same rain streak the same panes of glass, her thoughts turning, turning, like the slow, inevitable water in the ditch beyond the lawn. She knew the particular quality of her boredom, which was not the dull ache of inactivity but a sharp, fretted thing, a desire for a world beyond the hedges of Alderthorpe.

Then, the soldier. A different hand entirely, pressed into the flyleaf of a book of military history. *E.M.V. 1917. Mud, rain, and the same damned list of kings.* It was not a complaint, but a statement of fact, written with a blunt, certain stroke. She could feel the cold of the trench in the words, see the boy—Edward, his name would be—sitting with a book for a moment’s forgetfulness, only to find his own life echoed in the endless, grey chronicle of battles. He had not written again. Perhaps the book had been sent back to this quiet room, a ghost returned to its shelf, while he remained out there in the mud and the rain of his own making.

And then, the mother. This was the most potent of all, for it was not in a book of poetry or history but in the endpapers of a heavy medical tome, *The Diseases of Women and Children.* A list, written in a firm, maternal hand, the ink faded to the colour of a bruise. *Edward, b. 1895. Measles, 1899. Anna, b. 1898. Whooping-cough, 1902. Elara, b. 1901. Fevers, 1906, 1908. Anna. Scarlet fever, 1911.* The names were her children. The illnesses were the geography of her love, the small, terrifying battles she had fought in the dark of the nursery, her hand on a hot forehead, her ear to a struggling chest. She knew this woman better than she knew her own sister. She knew the weight of her vigil, the sharp relief of a fever breaking in the night.

The sound of a motor-car grinding on the gravel outside was an intrusion, a stone thrown into the still pond of the afternoon. Mr. Ashworth. He had come to inspect her progress. She heard his voice in the hall, clipped and confident, and felt a sudden, strange protectiveness for the ghosts that surrounded her. He entered the library with a stride that seemed to disturb the settled dust.

“Miss Vane. How does the work proceed?” He did not wait for an answer, his eyes scanning the shelves as if measuring them for demolition. “A vast quantity of paper, isn’t it? Still, one must go through it all before the sale.”

“It is nearly done, sir.”

“Good, good.” He picked up a slim volume of poetry from the table beside her. He opened it, and his thumb, thick and pink, rested for a moment on the page where Elara had written of the rain. Miss Vane felt a pang, as if he had touched a bruise.

“Anything of value?” he asked, his tone suggesting there could not be.

“There are some notes, sir. In the margins.”

He glanced at the page, his eyes passing over the faint pencil marks without seeing them. “Scribbles. The family were always writing in books. Damned bad habit. Makes them worthless.” He snapped the book shut. The sound was a small violence in the quiet room. “My grandfather, you know. A decent enough man, but no head for business. Let the whole estate go to pot. My father had to sell the hunting lodge. Now, well, one must be practical.”

He said ‘the family’ as one might say ‘the previous tenants’, a temporary inconvenience whose habits were a matter for faint annoyance. He saw the books as objects, assets to be weighed and valued. He did not see the girl at the window, or the soldier in the rain, or the mother counting the illnesses of her children in the dark. He did not feel the life that pulsed in these quiet pages, the faint, persistent echo of their joys and their fears. She looked at his solid, well-fed face, and then at her own hand, resting on the open page of the medical book, and she knew, with a certainty that was both a triumph and a sorrow, that she was the true heir to this house. Not of its stone and its land and its title deeds, but of its story. She possessed it all, held it in the silent, perfect world of her mind, while he, who had the right of it, owned nothing at all but the furniture. He turned to leave, and the light in the room seemed to close over the space where he had stood, leaving only the dust, the books, and the deep, unbroken silence.
