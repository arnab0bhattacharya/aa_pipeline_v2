It is a curious thing to consider the relative nature of terror. My father, the Reverend Elijah Vane, has spent thirty years among the Kitharo people of the high Altai, where the avalanches thunder down the gullies and the mountain leopards scream in the night. He has faced famine, frostbite, and the implacable hostility of the glacier-gods with nothing but a stern Presbyterian resolve. Yet I find myself wondering how he would fare in the Strand, where I took my midday meal yesterday, confronted not by the wrath of nature but by the mechanical vomiting of an omnibus. 

I am writing this in my cramped lodging in Bloomsbury, a district of relentless, soot-stained brick. The windowpanes rattle continually in their frames, vibrating to the passage of the subterranean railway—a train that runs entirely in the dark, like a blind metallic worm, devouring and discharging thousands of pale, anxious humans every few minutes. When I first arrived from the coast, I stood on the platform at Charing Cross and was immediately struck by a profound physical panic. The noise was a physical weight, a suffocating miasma of iron and shouting. I had spent my boyhood in a valley where the loudest sound was the cracking of the winter ice on the river; here, the very air seemed bruised and battered by the ceaseless percussion of hooves, wheels, and human panic.

My father sent me to England to receive a scientific education at the University of London. I am to lodge with a distant relative, a Mr. Henfrey, who manufactures surgical instruments in a manufactory near the river. It is a vast, cavernous place, filled with lathes and grinding wheels driven by a gigantic steam engine. The heat is infernal, and the smell of oil and hot metal reminds me vividly of the volcanic springs in the lower Altai. The men who work there are singularly grimy, their faces streaked with black grease, moving with an automatic, rhythmic precision that is deeply unsettling. They remind me of the Kitharo devil-dancers, who paint themselves with soot and ash before their ritual frenzies. 

Mr. Henfrey is a man of abrupt movements and a perpetually irritated complexion. He is, I believe, what the English call a "practical man," which seems to mean a man entirely devoid of wonder. When I attempted to explain to him the Kitharo belief that the thunder is the voice of the mountain spirits, he merely snorted and tapped the brass casing of his steam engine. "That is where your power comes from," he said, loudly, over the clatter of the lathes. "Coal and steam. None of your heathen mumbo-jumbo." 

I find this English obsession with practical machinery to be a form of savage idolatry itself. They speak of their engines with the same hushed reverence my father’s converts reserved for the Holy Communion. The locomotive is their sacred beast, a fire-breathing behemoth to which they daily sacrifice their time, their comfort, and occasionally their limbs, with a cheerful fatalism that would appall a South Sea islander. The Kitharo, at least, only sacrificed their enemies. The English sacrifice themselves to their own inventions.

I had my first real encounter with the savagery of London society last Thursday evening. Mr. Henfrey, deciding I was too melancholy, dragged me to a public house in Whitechapel, a low-roofed, gas-lit cavern thick with the reek of stale tobacco and spilled gin. The noise was, if anything, more concentrated than in the street. A woman was playing a battered piano with a violent, hammering intensity, and a group of men were singing a song with a great many lewd gestures and bellowing choruses. 

We sat at a wooden table with three of Henfrey’s acquaintances. They were working men, with rough hands and a distinct aroma of the tanneries. I was struck by the elaborate ritual of their drinking. They did not merely consume the liquid; they engaged in a complex series of toasts and challenges. If a man failed to drain his glass when commanded, he was subjected to a chorus of mocking laughter and forced to purchase another round. It was a system of social coercion as rigid and unforgiving as any taboo I encountered in the mountains.

One of the men, a brawny fellow with a red face and a singularly broken nose, turned his attention to me. "You’re the missionary’s whelp, ain’t you?" he bawled, peering at me with small, bloodshot eyes. "Come to teach us how to be saved, have you?"

I told him, as calmly as I could, that I had come to study natural philosophy. He found this immensely amusing. "Natural philosophy!" he repeated, slapping the table so hard that the glasses jumped. "Listen to him! Natural philosophy! You won’t find much of that down Wapping way, mate. We deals in unnatural philosophy down there. Rats and river mud, that’s our natural history."

I was attempting to formulate a reply when a fight broke out near the piano. The cause was trivial—a spilled glass, a jostled elbow—but the violence was instantaneous. Two men closed, grappling and cursing, and rolled onto the floor, knocking over tables and sending glasses shattering. The rest of the patrons did not intervene; they formed a ring, cheering and laying bets with a callous excitement that made my blood run cold. It was exactly like the Kitharo bear-baiting, save that the animals were human.

Mr. Henfrey, seeing my distress, pulled me out into the street. The cool, foggy air was a relief after the stifling heat of the public house. "You mustn’t mind them, Vane," he said, though he looked rather amused himself. "They’re only blowing off steam. It’s the only way they know how."

I walked back to Bloomsbury alone, my mind in a tumult. The gas lamps flared and sputtered in the damp air, casting long, grotesque shadows that leaped and twisted against the brickwork. I passed a knot of ragged children, sleeping in a doorway, huddled together for warmth like a litter of puppies. A policeman walked by, his heavy boots striking the pavement with a measured, mechanical tread, his face impassive and unreadable beneath his helmet. He looked at the children, looked at me, and walked on. 

I am beginning to understand that the English are not a civilized people. They are merely a technologically advanced tribe, living in a vast, smoky encampment, governed by the same brutal instincts and superstitious fears as any other. They have simply replaced the stone axe with the steam hammer, and the witch-doctor with the politician. The Kitharo, at least, have the decency to be afraid of their gods. The English worship their machines with a thoughtless, everyday devotion, entirely oblivious to the fact that those machines are slowly grinding them to dust.

I must close now. The clock in the hall is striking eleven, a harsh, metallic clanging that sets my teeth on edge. I must sleep, if the ceaseless rumbling of the cabs will permit it. Tomorrow I am to attend my first lecture at the University. I am told the professor will speak on the laws of thermodynamics. I hope, for my father’s sake, that it will make more sense than the ritual of the public house. But I confess, as I sit here listening to the siren of a river tug wailing through the fog, I have my doubts. The savagery of London is far more complex, and far more terrifying, than anything I left behind in the mountains.
