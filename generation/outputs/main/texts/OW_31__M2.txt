Eleanor sat before the mirror, the glass reflecting a face that had forgotten how to look surprised. It was a lovely face, or it had been, before the last three days had etched into its smooth porcelain a certain brittle, brittle elegance, like a vase that has been dropped but not yet broken. She was selecting her jewels. The diamonds were too cold; the emeralds, too vulgarly green. She settled on a strand of pearls, simple, lustrous, and utterly devoid of sentiment. It was the only accessory that matched the mood of the evening.

The house was quiet. Philip had left an hour ago, dressed in his black tie, his expression set in that mask of polite indifference he wore whenever he was trying to be truthful without actually saying anything. He had not explained. He had not denied. He had simply poured himself a brandy, looked at the clock, and said, "You are going to the Ashcombe Ball, are you, my dear?"

"I am going to the Ashcombe Ball," she had replied.

"Then you had better not be late. It is a tiresome sort of affair, but one must not be late at a tiresome affair."

That was all. No apology. No excuse. Just the brutal, efficient silence of a man who knows that the truth is a weapon that cuts both ways, and he has chosen to let the blade fall.

Eleanor knew the story, or what the world called the story. It was not difficult to piece together. The debts of Mrs. Vane, that woman who lived in the West End with a reputation as thin as the paper on which her letters were written, had been paid. Every penny. The creditors, those vultures who circled above respectable society, had been satisfied. And Philip had done it. Philip, who could not afford to buy a new coat for himself, had bought his wife’s rival her freedom. Or perhaps her silence. It did not matter. The act was one of such intimate generosity that it could only be interpreted as passion.

She picked up her fan. It was made of ivory, painted with a scene from *The Tempest*, but she looked at it as one looks at a relic. She was not going to the ball to dance. She was going to the ball to watch. To see the woman who had displaced her. To look into her eyes and see if there was the look of a conqueror there, or merely the tiredness of a creature who has been bought.

The carriage rattled over the cobblestones, the wheels striking up a discordant rhythm that seemed to match the ticking of her watch. London at night was a beast of many heads, snarling in the fog. The gas lamps flickered, casting long, wavering shadows that looked like fingers reaching for something. Eleanor watched the city slide past the window, a blur of brick and shadow. She thought about the nature of betrayal. It was not the act that hurt; it was the silence that followed. A loud betrayal is like a fire; it consumes, but it also illuminates. A silent betrayal is like a frost; it kills everything slowly, and you do not know which root has withered until the flower has fallen.

They arrived at the Ashcombe house. The doors were open. The music drifted out, a waltz, something light and airy, utterly inappropriate for the gravity of the situation. Eleanor stepped out of the carriage. She wore a gown of midnight blue silk, cut low in the back, and her hair was arranged in the severe, elegant style of the day. She looked like a statue of justice, if justice had ever been fashionable.

Inside, the ballroom was a riot of color. Pink, yellow, white. The women were dressed in feathers and tulle, their faces painted to look like porcelain dolls. The men were dressed in black, their faces hidden behind the masks of politeness. Eleanor moved through the crowd. She did not look at Philip. She knew where he was. He was standing near the bar, talking to a man she did not know. He looked bored. He looked safe.

She found Mrs. Vane. She was sitting alone in a corner, holding a glass of champagne she did not drink. She was younger than Eleanor, and prettier, in a careless, unpolished way. Her hair was dark, her eyes large and dark, and she looked tired. Not the tiredness of a woman who has been dancing, but the tiredness of a woman who has been waiting.

Eleanor sat down beside her.

"You are not Mrs. Ashcombe," Eleanor said.

"No," said Mrs. Vane. "I am Mrs. Vane."

"Philip told me you were here," Eleanor said.

"He did?"

"He did not tell me why he paid your debts."

Mrs. Vane turned to look at her. Her eyes were clear. "Did he not tell you?"

"No."

"Then you must be a very patient woman, or a very foolish one."

"I am neither," Eleanor said. "I am merely curious."

"Curiosity is a vice," said Mrs. Vane. "But it is a fashionable one."

"Who are you to him?" Eleanor asked.

"Who am I to him?" Mrs. Vane smiled. It was a sad smile. "I am a debt. I am a responsibility. I am the thing he could not bear to see ruined. That is all."

"He paid you."

"He paid for my silence. I have a scandal. A large one. If it had come out, it would have destroyed him. Not because I was his mistress, but because I was his friend. A friend who was in trouble. He did not pay me for my body, Eleanor. He paid me for his soul. And it is very expensive to keep a soul clean in this city."

Eleanor felt a strange sensation in her chest. It was not relief. It was not anger. It was something colder. It was the realization that she had been fighting a ghost. She had been imagining a rival, a lover, a woman who had stolen her husband’s heart. But the truth was much more mundane, and therefore much more terrible. It was not a matter of passion. It was a matter of duty. And duty was a poor lover.

"Why did you not tell him to stop?" Eleanor asked.

"Because he would have stopped. And then the scandal would have come out. And he would have been ruined. I would have been ruined. And we would both have been free. But we are not free. We are bound by the things we do for each other. And that is a stronger chain than love."

Eleanor looked at the woman. She saw the fear in her eyes. She saw the exhaustion. She saw the woman who had been saved, and who now owed a debt that could never be repaid.

"Philip is a good man," Eleanor said.

"He is a good man," Mrs. Vane agreed. "But he is not a happy man. Good men are never happy. They are too busy being good."

Eleanor stood up. She adjusted her pearls. She felt lighter, but also emptier. She had come to confront a rival, and she had found a confidante. She had come to fight a war, and she had found a truce.

"Good night, Mrs. Vane," Eleanor said.

"Good night," said Mrs. Vane.

Eleanor walked through the ballroom. She passed Philip. He was watching her. He looked relieved. He looked guilty. He looked like a man who has just been caught with his hand in the till.

Eleanor smiled at him. It was a small, cold smile.

"Was it worth it?" she asked.

"What?" he asked.

"Your soul," she said.

He did not answer. He could not. The music swelled around them, the waltz reaching its crescendo, a whirl of silk and shadow. Eleanor turned and walked toward the door. She did not look back. She knew that the truth was a mirror, and she had seen her reflection in it. And she did not like what she saw. She went out into the night, into the fog, into the silence. And for the first time in three days, she felt that she was alone. And that was a luxury.
