The thing happened on a Tuesday in October, in the year nineteen-hundred-and-four, and it happened with a completeness that left no room for any of those vague, half-superstitious explanations that country people are so fond of attaching to machinery. It was not a gradual failure. There was no spluttering, no diminution of power, no preliminary coughing in the carburettor. At precisely half-past nine in the morning, every internal combustion engine in the parish of Crowthorne, and indeed, as we later discovered, across the whole breadth of England, simply ceased to be an engine.

I was standing in the yard of the Midland Motor Works, which I had established some three years before with a capital scraped together from my own savings and a heavy mortgage on my uncle’s farm. I had staked everything on the horseless carriage. I had two dozen machines in the show-shed, and another half-dozen in various stages of assembly. We had just run a new four-cylinder Darracq chassis out into the daylight for Mr. Voss, the brewer, to inspect. My chief mechanic, a certain Bunting, a man of immense practical resource and a perpetual grievance against fate, was at the crank. He gave the handle a vigorous half-turn. There was a dull, dead resistance, as if the flywheel had been set in solid concrete. Bunting staggered, swore heartily, and tried again. The engine did not fire. It did not even turn over. It was, to all intents and purposes, a solid block of iron and steel.

"It’s seized, sir," said Bunting, rubbing his elbow.

"Nonsense," said I, and I went to examine it. But it was not seized in the ordinary mechanical sense. The parts moved freely enough against one another when disconnected, but the essential principle of the thing—the explosive impulse that is the very soul of the petrol motor—was utterly gone. We tried the others in the yard. We tried Mr. Voss’s, which he had driven up in high gaiety not an hour before. We drew petrol direct from the cask and applied a match. The spirit burned with a thin, rather spiritless blue flame, but it utterly refused to explode in the cylinders. It was exactly as if some invisible, capricious hand had reached down and tampered with the very nature of combustion itself.

By noon the full extent of the calamity had filtered through to the general consciousness of the town. The Royal Mail van stood rigid at the corner of the High Street. Dr. Haddon’s new Wolseley was a useless obstruction outside the surgery. A sort of stunned incredulity gave way, by three o’clock, to a very ugly and practical panic. For it seemed this paralysis was universal. The telegraph wires were working, but they only conveyed the maddening intelligence that London was in chaos, that the military could not move its transport, and that no one in authority had the faintest conception of what had occurred or when it might end.

And for me, Thomas Pell, the motor-dealer, it meant absolute and unmitigated ruin. I had bills falling due on the fifteenth. I had a show-room full of expensive metal coffins. I had a bank manager in Reading who was, in the best of times, a man of icy and unsympathetic commercial rectitude.

It was Bunting who brought the matter to a head. He came into my office, where I was sitting staring at a list of my creditors, with a grimy piece of paper in his hand.

"It’s Mrs. Gribble, sir," he said. "And the two Miss Peakes from the almshouses."

"Well?" said I.

"They’re in the shop, sir. They want to know if you’ll be wanting the money for the two ton of coal you had delivered last week. They’re walking over to Bracknell, sir, to fetch Dr. Warner. The old lady’s had a stroke, seemingly, and our Dr. Haddon’s away in Wokingham. They can’t get no cart from the livery stables till Thursday, the blacksmith’s horse being lame. They say they’ll pay you next month, if you’ll wait for the coal."

I remember staring at Bunting with a sudden, quite disproportionate intensity. The sheer, grotesque absurdity of the situation struck me like a physical blow. Here was I, a man of science and progress, a disciple of the new age of mechanical locomotion, sitting ruined among a score of useless engines, while two elderly women proposed to walk six miles across the heath to fetch a doctor for a dying woman, because the entire fabric of modern transport had dissolved like a dream.

"Tell them to come in, Bunting," I said.

They came in, very timid and apologetic, those two faded, respectable figures. Mrs. Gribble had a shawl over her head, and the elder Miss Peake was leaning heavily on a malacca cane. They were quite prepared to be refused. They were the sort of people who are always prepared to be refused. They began a halting explanation of their difficulties, their voices overlapping in a rather painful duet of self-justification.

I let them finish. Then I said, "You are not going to walk to Bracknell."

They looked at me, startled.

"The road across the heath is heavy going," I said, "and it will be dark before you get there. Bunting, take the two-seater from the back shed."

Bunting stared. "The two-seater, sir? But it won’t go, sir!"

"It will go," said I, with a sudden, fierce conviction that surprised even myself. "It will go if you push it. We will push it. We are going to fetch Dr. Warner."

There was a moment of bewilderment. Then Bunting, who was a man of rare intelligence beneath his grime, began to laugh. He saw it. And I, who had been weighing the relative merits of jumping the night train to Southampton and leaving my debts to the bailiffs, saw it too. It was a choice between skulking away from the wreckage of my fortunes or doing something definite and human.

We got the two-seater out. It was a light car, built for a lady, and with Bunting at the steering-wheel and myself pushing, we trundled it down the yard and out into the street. Mrs. Gribble and the Miss Peakes were installed upon the velvet cushions, looking for all the world like a pair of bewildered queens in a triumphal car, and we set off.

It was an extraordinary progress. The road to Bracknell was littered with the stalled wrecks of the new era. We passed Mr. Voss’s great Daimler, standing like a sulky monument in the middle of the road; we passed a baker’s cart, its horse gone to grass, the loaves still warm inside; we passed a party of young men from the cycling club, pushing their machines up the hill with a sort of sulky, bewildered energy. And we, pushing our dead car, were the only moving thing in the landscape. It gave one a curious sense of power. We were not defeated. We had adapted ourselves. We were using the very symbol of our disaster as a means of conveyance.

It was hard work, of course. The road was uphill for the first two miles, and the sweat ran down my face and blinded me. But there was a comradeship in it that I had not known since my undergraduate days. Bunting and I, we were no longer employer and employed; we were two men pitted against a common enemy, the inertia of matter. We talked as we pushed, of the strange caprice of nature, of the mysteries of combustion, of the probable effect of the catastrophe on the share price of the various motor companies. And I found myself, in the midst of my ruin, experiencing a quite illogical exhilaration.

We fetched Dr. Warner, a gruff, kindly man who was vastly amused by our conveyance, and who insisted on taking his turn at the pushing on the way back. We got the old lady her medicine, and she lived, I am glad to say, to plague her neighbours for another five years.

As for me, I did not go to Southampton. I stayed in Crowthorne. The motors never went again, of course. The thing was a mystery that science has never satisfactorily explained; a sort of cosmic interruption in the chain of physical causation. I was bankrupt, and my show-room became a bicycle shop. But I have never regretted that walk to Bracknell. It taught me, in a way that no amount of scientific theorizing could have done, that the essential thing in life is not the machine, but the man; not the engine, but the will that drives it. And that, I think, is a lesson worth learning, even at the price of a fortune.
