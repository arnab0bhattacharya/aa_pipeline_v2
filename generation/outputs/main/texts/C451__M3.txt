The chalk dust hung in the late-June light, suspended like something dormant, though Edwin knew it was merely the absence of a breeze. The tall windows of the Lower Fourth classroom were open to their fullest, but the Kentish air outside sat thick and still, carrying only the distant, rhythmic thud of a cricket ball against willow from the far pitch. It was the final day of the summer term, 1923, and Edwin’s forty-second year at Stonebridge School was drawing to its close with the same quiet, unremarkable exhalation that had ended every other. The corridors had emptied an hour ago, the shouts and the scuffling of boots fading into the triumphant roar of the departing trains at Haversham station. He was alone in the room, seated in his high-backed oak chair, his hands folded over the lap of his threadbare tweed trousers.

He had not yet begun to clear his desk. He was looking instead at the rows of empty desks before him, the tops scarred by four decades of restless compass points, penknives, and bitten nails. It was a palimpsest of boyhood, a record of fleeting obsessions and idle cruelties. He lowered his gaze to the desk immediately in his line of sight, the second row from the front. There, carved with a steadiness that suggested a steady hand, were the initials A.H. and a date: 1913. Arthur Hadleigh. Edwin remembered Arthur with a painful clarity. A heavy, blotchy boy, perpetually out of breath, who had struggled immensely with his Latin declensions but could recite the batting averages of the Surrey team with absolute, joyous accuracy. He had been kind, in the way that only the ungainly can truly be kind, always stepping aside to let the others pass.

Edwin traced the grooves of the letters with the pad of his thumb. Arthur had not gone to France. His lungs had been weak since a bout of influenza in his final year, and the army would not take him. He had gone into his father’s accounting firm in Maidstone instead. It was Arthur who had written to Edwin in 1918, a polite, conscientious letter, to inform him that young Thomas Rourke, who had sat directly behind Arthur, had been killed at Épehy. Arthur was alive, enduring, still likely reciting averages. Yet, sitting in the empty classroom, Edwin felt the ghost of Arthur Hadleigh as a kind of mourning. The boy had survived, but the world he belonged to had been obliterated, and he was left to keep its accounts.

Edwin stood, his knees cracking in the quiet, and walked slowly down the aisle. He let his fingers trail along the wooden lips of the desks. He stopped at the back row, where the wood was most deeply gouged. Here sat the boys who had been too tall, too restless, too entirely bored by the Peloponnesian War to care about the marks they left. He found the name he was looking for, etched deeply in capital letters: P. ASHWORTH. Philip Ashworth. He had been a golden boy, though not in the way the phrase usually implied. Philip was sardonic, handsome, and possessed of a sharp, cruel wit that Edwin had secretly admired. Philip had taken great delight in mocking Edwin’s stammer, which had been much worse in those days. Once, during a translation of Cicero, Philip had mimicked Edwin’s halting breath so perfectly that the entire class had erupted in laughter. Edwin had caned him for it, feeling the sting of the cane in his own palm, feeling the hot flush of his own humiliation. 

He looked at the name now, the letters jagged where the knife had slipped. Philip had gone to France in the autumn of 1914, an officer in the Royal West Kents. He had survived the Somme, survived Passchendaele, only to be killed by a stray sniper’s bullet in the quiet days of November 1918, a week before the armistice. Edwin had read the notice in the Old Boys’ gazette and had felt a complicated, terrible surge of relief that he would never again have to face that mocking smile. The guilt of that relief had settled into his bones and stayed there, a dull ache that flared up in the damp.

He moved to the window and looked out over the playing fields. The grass was yellowing at the edges, parched by the unusually warm spring. He tried to summon the faces of the boys who had run across that grass, but they blurred together into a single, faceless mass of white shirts and straw boaters. He remembered the collective noise, the sheer volume of fifty boys confined to a room, the squeak of nibs on slate, the smell of pencil shavings and damp wool. But the individual features were fading. He pulled the Old Boys’ roll from his memory, the list of the dead he read silently every November. There were forty-seven names. He could see the first fifteen clearly—Ashworth, Rourke, Davies, the Carmichael twins. But toward the end, the names became just sounds, hollow syllables stripped of their human weight. 

He turned back to the desks, suddenly desperate to anchor the forgetting. He searched the wood for a name he had lost. There was a boy, a quiet boy with dark hair, who used to sit by the door. He had a habit of drawing ships in the margins of his exercise books. He had died in the navy, Edwin was certain, somewhere in the North Sea. But what was his name? It began with an M, or perhaps a V. He scanned the desktops, his eyes stinging. Vance? Morrison? The boy had been there, had carved his name into the wood, had existed in this very room, and now Edwin could not even summon his first name. 

He sank into a desk at the back of the room, the wood groaning under his weight. He felt a sudden, overwhelming tiredness. He had spent his life teaching these boys the glories of Rome, the rhetoric of Demosthenes, the stoicism of Marcus Aurelius. He had taught them that duty was the highest calling, that a civilized man meets his end with dignity and without complaint. And then he had sent them out, one by one, into a world that had chewed them up in the mud of Ypres and Arras. He had armed them with Latin tags and sent them to walk into machine-gun fire. What good had his lessons been? What use was the perfect pluperfect to a boy drowning in a gas-filled trench?

He rested his head in his hands, the silence of the room pressing in on him. It was a heavy silence, thick with the absence of forty-seven voices. He thought of the boys who had come after, the ones who sat in these desks now. They were a different breed, quieter, more fragile, looking at the world with a wary, disillusioned eye. They did not carve their names into the wood. They did not believe in the glory of empire or the nobility of sacrifice. They had learned that lesson too well, taught by their older brothers’ absences.

Edwin opened his eyes and looked once more at the desk in front of him. There, half-hidden beneath a deep gouge, was a faint, almost illegible name. T. VANCE. He stared at it, the breath catching in his throat. Thomas Vance. The dark-haired boy who drew the ships. He had been at Jutland. Edwin remembered now, with a sharp, clarifying pang, the way Thomas used to look out the window during lessons, his eyes tracing the clouds as if they were the sails of his imaginary armada. He had been nineteen when he went down with his ship. 

Edwin reached out and placed his palm flat over the carved letters, as if he could press some warmth back into the wood, some life back into the memory. He could not bring them back. He could not undo the lessons he had taught, nor the world that had shattered them. All he could do was remember, and in remembering, bear witness to the quiet, unremarkable courage of boys who had sat in this room and stared at the chalk dust floating in the light, dreaming of a future they would never see. 

He stood up slowly, his joints aching, and walked back to his desk. He opened the drawer and took out a soft cloth. He wiped the chalk dust from his hands, folded the cloth neatly, and placed it back inside. The room was empty. The term was over. He picked up his hat, walked to the door, and closed it softly behind him, leaving the carved names to their long, silent vigil in the afternoon sun.
