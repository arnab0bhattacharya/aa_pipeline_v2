The December wind swept across the valley with a cutting, sullen roar, driving clouds of soot from the tall chimneys of Brookfield Mill against the grimy windowpanes of Ashworth House. I stood at the nursery lattice, pressing my forehead against the freezing glass, and looked out upon the steep, cobbled lane that mounted from the works to our door. Below, in the hollow, the mill-stream, swollen by recent rains, thundered with a sound like muffled artillery; above, the Yorkshire moors stretched away, black and desolate, under a sky of cold iron. I had no business to be standing idle at the casement. The lesson-books lay open on the table, and my two pupils, Maria and Susan Ashworth, were already clamouring to resume their interrupted tasks. But I was not mistress of myself; I was listening—listening, with a painful, unaccountable eagerness, for a certain firm, heavy tread in the hall below.

I was only the nursery-governess. Let me not forget that. I was Agnes Lloyd, an orphan, a dependent, paid twenty pounds a year to drum the rudiments of grammar and geography into the minds of the manufacturer's younger daughters. And he was Rowland Ashworth, the eldest son of the house, the heir to the mill and the fortune. I had been nearly a year in this house, and in that time I had learned my own place with a severity that left no room for illusion. I had also learned, with a bitterness that was daily bread, that I could not look upon Mr. Rowland without a strange trouble at my heart. He was not handsome—his features were rugged, almost harsh—but he had a keen, dark eye, a resolute bearing, and a certain abrupt, imperious sincerity that I could not withstand. When he spoke to me, which was seldom, his voice had a deep, manly music that thrilled me; when he passed me in the passage with a distant nod, I felt a sudden, piercing chill, as if a door had been shut against my face.

That morning, however, I had overheard what I was never meant to hear. I had come down to the housekeeper's room for Maria's neglected atlas, and the door of the parlour had stood ajar. Mrs. Ashworth's voice reached me, high and satisfied: "So it is settled at last. Rowland is to marry Miss Greenwood, of Northgate. A most eligible connection; her father's works are worth forty thousand pounds. A true blessing for us all."

I had stood rooted to the spot, the atlas forgotten under my arm. The words had entered my ear and passed into my heart like a sword. I did not faint; I did not weep. I turned slowly and went back up the stairs to the nursery, and sat down in my chair by the window, and folded my hands on my lap, and felt that the world was suddenly stripped bare of every leaf and blossom, and that I stood alone on a wide heath, with a cold wind blowing over me from the grave.

Miss Greenwood! I had seen her once at the chapel—a tall, fair girl, richly dressed, with a calm, assured brow, and the unmistakable air of one who has never known want or humiliation. She would make a fitting mistress of Ashworth House. She would sit at the head of the table where I was not allowed to sit even at the foot. She would wear silks and velvets, and drive in a carriage, and be called "ma'am" by servants who barely called me anything at all. And Rowland would stand beside her, and look at her as I had sometimes, in my wilder, weaker moments, imagined he might look at me.

But he had never looked at me so. He had never spoken a word of tenderness. He was merely civil, distantly civil, as a man of his station is civil to an inferior. I knew this. I told myself this. And yet the knowledge did not kill the hope that had lived in me, secretly, stubbornly, like a green shoot in a crevice of stone. Now the boot had descended and crushed it.

"Miss Lloyd, I cannot find my place!" cried Maria, pulling at my gown. "You are not attending; you are looking out of the window."

I turned from the lattice and took the book. "Forgive me, Maria. We will begin the geography."

I set them to their copies and bent over the slates, correcting, guiding, while the wind shook the casement and the mill roared below. But my hand was unsteady, and my voice had a note in it that made Susan look up once or twice with childish wonder. I forced myself to composure. I would not betray myself. I would not let any eye in that house read the wound that was bleeding within. What right had I to bleed? What claim had I upon Rowland Ashworth? None—less than none. I was a paid servant; he was the master's son. The Scripture saith, and saith truly, that the servant is not greater than his lord. I would be the servant, and I would serve faithfully, and I would bury this thing in the deepest cell of my heart, and no one should ever know.

At dinner, I sat at the end of the long table in the housekeeper's room, eating my cold mutton in silence. The housekeeper, Mrs. Bland, talked of the approaching marriage as a matter already settled and universally approved.

"It will be a grand wedding, I'll be bound," she said, buttering her bread. "Mrs. Ashworth is beside herself with satisfaction. And Mr. Rowland seems very well pleased, though he is not one to show his feelings."

"Does he love her?" The question broke from me before I could stop it. I flushed crimson, and thrust my fork into a potato.

Mrs. Bland stared. "Love her? What a question, Miss Lloyd! He is marrying her because she is a fitting match, and because her father's capital will help to put in the new looms. Love is all very well for poets and such-like, but in a manufacturing family, we look to substance."

I said no more. I finished my dinner in a silence that was like a gag drawn tight across my mouth. When I rose to leave, Mrs. Bland called after me: "The family dines early to-day, Miss Lloyd, as Mr. Rowland and Miss Greenwood are expected at four. The children are to be dressed in their best pinafores to receive her."

I went back to the nursery and dressed the children with a trembling, furious energy. I tied Maria's sash with a jerk that made her cry out, and then I knelt down and begged her pardon, and kissed her, and felt the tears very near the surface. But I would not let them fall. I would not.

At four o'clock the bell rang. I heard voices in the hall—Mrs. Ashworth's, full of cordial welcome; a lighter, sweeter voice that I knew to be Miss Greenwood's; and then, low and measured, his. I stood in the nursery and listened as one listens to a sentence of banishment. The children ran down to be admired, and I was left alone. I went again to the window. The December afternoon was already darkening; the mill lights were beginning to prick out in the valley below, and the wind had risen to a wail. I pressed my hand hard against my breast, as if to hold down by force the tumult that was there.

I did not hate Miss Greenwood. I could not. She had done me no wrong. But I felt, with a terrible, Scriptural clearness, that I was as one cast out into outer darkness, where there is weeping and gnashing of teeth—not because I had sinned greatly, but because I had been born poor and obscure, and had dared, without knowing it, to lift my eyes to a star that hung forever above my reach.

The children came back presently, full of the visitor's finery. "She has a silk pelisse, Miss Lloyd, and a velvet bonnet with feathers!" cried Susan. "And Mr. Rowland smiled at her—did you ever see him smile, Miss Lloyd? He looked quite handsome!"

"Yes," I said. I turned to the fire and poked it viciously, sending a shower of sparks up the chimney. "Yes, I have seen him smile. Come now, it is time for your sewing."

They sat down, and I sat down, and the lesson went on. The room was warm, the fire burned bright, and the children's needles clicked softly. Outside, the wind moaned about the house, and the mill roared on, tireless and indifferent. I held my peace, and set my face like a flint, and told myself that I felt nothing—nothing at all.
