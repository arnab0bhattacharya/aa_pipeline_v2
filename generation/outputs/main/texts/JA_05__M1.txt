Miss Sophia Hargreave, on arriving in London under the protection of her aunt, Mrs. Perceval, had imagined that the trials of a first season would consist chiefly of a difficulty in distinguishing one handsome face from another, and a certain shyness in the presence of gentlemen. She had not conceived of friendship as an expense.

Yet it was in the matter of friendship that her little fortune began to suffer its first, and most painful, diminution. The Misses Bellingham—Caroline, Augusta, and the youngest, who was known simply as Letty—had taken her up with an enthusiasm which, at first, Sophia found most gratifying. They were creatures of the highest fashion, moving through the drawing-rooms of Mayfair with a grace that made Sophia feel herself a plucked wildflower placed beside hot-house roses. Their friendship was a series of engagements: a drive in the Park at five, where one must not be seen to be merely looking, but to be looked at; a morning at the shops in Bond Street, where the mere act of looking at a bonnet in a certain window was understood to be a statement of one’s intentions; and, most fatally, a subscription to a circulating library in Albemarle Street, where the choice of one’s novels was a matter of the greatest social consequence.

The first time the account was presented, Sophia had paid it with a flush of pride, seeing it as her entry into their world. The second time, she had paid with a cheque drawn on her own modest allowance, an act that required a week of strict economy in her personal expenses. By the third month, she found herself in the dreadful position of having to explain to Mrs. Perceval that her pin-money was exhausted.

“My dear Sophia,” said Mrs. Perceval, without looking up from her embroidery, “one does not keep an account of friendship. If the Misses Bellingham are kind enough to include you in their parties, you must learn to keep pace. It is a lesson in consequence, and one cannot put a price on that.”

But the price, Sophia was learning, was a very particular one. It was the price of a new silk pelisse, which she was expected to purchase to match the one Augusta had acquired; it was the price of a ticket to a concert at Vauxhall, where the Bellinghams’ party required a certain style of refreshment; it was the price of being seen to be indifferent to cost. To be poor in such a set was not merely to be without funds; it was to be without spirit.

It was during a morning call at the Bellinghams’ residence in Grosvenor Square that Sophia’s eyes were opened to a second, and perhaps more serious, deficiency. Mrs. Bellingham, a lady of formidable bosom and sharper eyes, had received them with a civility that was chillingly precise. She spoke of the weather, of the Prince Regent’s latest indiscretion, and then, with a suddenness that made Sophia catch her breath, of Mrs. Perceval’s own circle.

“I trust, Mrs. Perceval,” she said, her gaze resting on Sophia’s aunt with a look of polite inquiry, “that you find the society in town much as you remembered it? It is so difficult, is it not, to keep up with the changes in connexion? One hears of so many new faces, and one is never quite sure of the old ones.”

Mrs. Perceval, who had been smiling with her usual air of settled confidence, seemed to stiffen. “I find London, as ever, a delightful mixture of the established and the emergent, Mrs. Bellingham. It is the very essence of its charm.”

“Indeed,” said Mrs. Bellingham. “And I am sure your friends in—where was it?—Shropshire, must be quite green with envy at your being here. Though I suppose, in the country, one’s reputation is a simpler thing. Less to maintain, perhaps.”

Sophia felt a sudden, uncomfortable heat in her cheeks. She saw, in a flash, the way the Bellingham girls—Caroline and Augusta—had exchanged a look of such transparent meaning that it might as well have been spoken aloud. It was a look that placed Mrs. Perceval not at the centre of London society, but at its periphery; a lady of some means, certainly, but one whose claims to fashion were of a provincial and slightly faded variety. The friendship offered to Sophia, she now understood, was not a tribute to her own charm, but a curiosity about a new specimen, and a gentle exercise of power over a relation of a woman they considered a social inferior.

That evening, as they sat in the quiet of their drawing-room in Upper Brook Street, the silence was heavy with unspoken things. Mrs. Perceval was unusually subdued, her hands lying idle in her lap.

“The Bellinghams are a very particular family,” she said at last, her voice lacking its customary assurance. “One must be careful to meet their standard.”

“I believe,” said Sophia, with a courage she did not know she possessed, “that their standard is one I can no longer afford to meet. Nor, perhaps, do I wish to.”

Mrs. Perceval looked at her niece with an expression of mingled surprise and relief. “My dear, you have been observing. It is a dangerous habit in a young lady, but a necessary one. The truth is, my standing here is not what it was. A misunderstanding about a certain investment, a few words spoken in haste years ago… in the country, such things are forgotten. In town, they are merely dormant. The Bellinghams know it. They enjoy knowing it.”

She gave a small, weary sigh. “They took you up to see if you would be as foolish as they hoped. And I, I am afraid, encouraged it, thinking it might secure us a better place. It was a miscalculation. Friendship, like a bank, requires a deposit of character as well as coin. We are rather low in both.”

Sophia reached out and took her aunt’s hand. The warmth of it was a comfort, and the honesty a greater one. “Then we must learn a new economy,” she said. “One of fewer engagements, and truer ones.”

Mrs. Perceval smiled, a genuine smile this time. “You are a sensible girl, Sophia. We shall retire from the lists for a while. We shall read our own books, and walk in the Park to enjoy the air, not to be seen enjoying it. And we shall see who remains our friend when the subscription to the library is cancelled.”

It was a resolution that cost Sophia a pang. She would miss the sparkle of the Bellinghams’ company, the thrill of being at the heart of things. But as she lay awake that night, listening to the distant rattle of a carriage, she understood that she had acquired a different kind of polish. It was the quiet gleam of self-knowledge, a finish that did not wash off in the first rain, and which, she suspected, would serve her far better in the long run than the most fashionable friendship in Grosvenor Square.
