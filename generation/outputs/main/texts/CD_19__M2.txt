The arches of the Bermondsey Viaduct were not merely a passage for the iron horse, but a cavernous cathedral of soot and shadow, where the air hung heavy with the scent of damp brick, coal smoke, and the peculiar, metallic tang of the rails. It was here, in the deepest gloom where the gaslight from the street above could not penetrate, that the colony of the Lost Boys held court. They were a ragged, shivering tribe, led by a boy of fifteen named Silas, whose eyes were as sharp and cold as the winter wind that whistled through the gaps in the masonry. They had a new charge, a creature of such pitiful smallness and fragility that it seemed less a child and more a stray kitten, half-frozen and entirely bewildered. She had no name, for she could not speak it; only a single, trembling syllable, "Lily," which she uttered when she thought of flowers she had never seen.

Silas, who had known the taste of every crust and the sting of every policeman’s truncheon, sat upon an overturned barrel, his legs crossed with the air of a general inspecting his troops. He looked at the girl, who was huddled in a corner, wrapped in a coat three sizes too large and smelling faintly of lavender and something else—something clean, and dangerous.

"She’s a burden," grunted Tibbs, a thick-set fellow with a nose like a beetroot, who was currently gnawing on a stolen apple core. "A liability. We can’t carry her to the workhouse, and we can’t feed her on air. She eats more than I do, and I have the stomach of a horse."

"She eats what we give her," Silas said, his voice low and dangerous. "And she is quiet. Quiet is a virtue, Tibbs. Noise brings the watch. Noise brings the magistrates. Noise brings the end of us." He paused, looking at the girl’s face, which was pale as the moon that shone through the high, grimy windows of the viaduct. "Besides, she is the only one of us who does not look like a criminal. She looks like a lady. That is useful."

It was a lie, of course. They all knew it. The girl was not useful; she was a ghost in their lives, a reminder of a world they had been cast out of. They fed her by day, stealing tins of condensed milk from the wharf, or pinching oranges from the carts that rumbled down the muddy roads. They washed her in the stagnant water of the canal, scrubbing the grime from her skin until she shone like a porcelain doll, and then they hid her in the deepest recess of the arch, where the rats were too shy to approach.

But the peace was broken one morning by the sound of a carriage, a thing of such majesty and polish that it seemed to have descended from a different planet. It stopped at the foot of the arches, and from it stepped a woman of such extraordinary dignity that the very mud seemed to recoil from her shoes. She wore a dress of deep blue velvet, trimmed with sable, and her face was a mask of composed, imperious anxiety.

"Is there a child here?" she asked, her voice carrying the crisp, clear tone of the upper classes, a sound that was as alien to the arches as the sun is to the deep sea. "A girl. Small. With light hair. She was taken from me last night."

Silas felt a cold knot form in his stomach. He watched the woman, who had begun to look up into the shadows, her eyes searching the darkness with a ferocity that was almost frightening. The other boys froze, their breaths caught in their throats. Tibbs, whose heart was not made for such suspense, slipped on a piece of wet cardboard and crashed into a pile of bricks. The sound was like a cannon shot in the silent cave.

The woman’s head snapped toward the noise. Her eyes, large and dark, locked onto Silas.

"Well?" she said, stepping forward, her skirts brushing the filth of the ground. "Come out. I know you are there. I have been looking for her for two days. She is my daughter. My only child."

Silas stood up. He was tall for his age, and he straightened his back, trying to look as if he had not just been crouching in the dirt. He looked at the girl, who was peering out from behind a wooden crate, her eyes wide with a terror that was not for herself, but for them. She tugged at Silas’s sleeve, a silent plea.

*Give me up,* her eyes said. *Save yourself.*

Silas looked at the woman. He saw the desperation in her face, the wealth in her clothes, the power in her voice. He saw a future where he might be bought, or sold, or simply disappeared into the machinery of the law. And then he looked at the girl, who was shivering, not from cold, but from the fear of being taken away from the only people who had ever held her hand.

"No," Silas said.

The woman blinked. "No?"

"She is not here," Silas said, his voice steady, though his hands were shaking inside his pockets. "We are vagrants, madam. We have no children. We have no place to keep them. You will find your daughter in the workhouse, or in the river, or in the hands of the police. But not here."

The woman’s face hardened. She reached into her bag and pulled out a purse, heavy with gold. "I am not asking for a bribe, boy. I am asking for my child. I will pay you. I will pay you whatever you name. But I will not leave until I have seen her."

Silas looked at the gold. It glinted in the dim light, a cold, bright star. He thought of the warm soup they could buy, the new boots, the safety of a room with a lock. He thought of the girl’s face, and the way she had smiled at him yesterday when he had given her a piece of stale bread. It was a small smile, but it was real. It was the only real thing in the arches.

"Go away, madam," Silas said. "We are not thieves. We are just... lost."

The woman stared at him for a long moment. Then, with a sigh that seemed to age her ten years, she turned and walked away. The carriage rumbled away, its wheels disappearing into the distance, leaving behind only the smell of exhaust and the echo of its horn.

Silas went back to the girl. He sat down beside her, and she leaned against him, her head resting on his shoulder. She did not speak. She did not need to. They were both lost, but they were lost together. And in the dark, cold arches of Bermondsey, that was enough.
