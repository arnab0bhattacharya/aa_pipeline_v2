It is a common error, perhaps, among those who have never felt the pressure of an unshared intellectual life, to suppose that the yearning for such communion is a mere fancy, superadded to the more robust necessities of our nature, and easily subdued by the presence of warm hearths and a kindly disposition. We are apt to think that a good temper and a liberal table will make up for the absence of any subtle sympathy of thought. But the mind, like the body, has its appetite, and to sit down daily to a feast where the meat is abundant but the salt of understanding is lacking, is a privation that touches the very quick of a woman’s moral fibre.

Such, at least, were the reflections that began to beset Mrs. Helena Torrington in the early spring of 1834, as she sat in the oak-panelled dining-room of Beechcroft Priory, watching her husband convey a generous portion of cold roast beef to his plate. It was little more than eight months since she had come to the Priory as a bride, bringing with her a small but cherished collection of the Greek and Roman classics, a few modern poets, and a heart full of vague, half-formed expectations of a companionship that should be at once affectionate and enlightened. She had been educated at a school in Bath, where the mistress had been a woman of more than ordinary cultivation, and where Helena’s naturally quick intelligence had been fed on history and philosophy, until she had come to look upon mental acquisition as the chief good of life. Squire Torrington, on the other hand, had been educated at Harrow and Oxford, but the university had done little for him beyond confirming his native taste for sport and giving him a certain sturdy, unquestioning English loyalty to the institutions of his country. He was a man of excellent morals, kind-hearted, and just; but his mind moved in a narrow circle, bounded by the weather, the condition of his crops, the breed of his horses, and the management of his tenantry. He read the newspaper with regularity, but a book was to him a thing to be avoided as one avoids a dull day in the hunting-field.

“I’m thinking of riding over to Deepden after breakfast, Helena,” he said, in his hearty, cheerful voice, which had in it something of the bluff frankness of a man who has never had occasion to conceal a thought. “I want to see what old Chawner is doing with that new-fangled drill he talked of at the last quarter-sessions. I told him he was a fool for his pains, and that the old way was good enough for my father before me; but the fellow’s obstinate as a mule, and I must e’en go and see how the thing works.”

“By all means, Edmund,” said Helena, with a gentle smile. She had learned already that it was useless to attempt to share his interest in agricultural implements, and she had too much sense and too much genuine affection for him to pretend to an interest she did not feel. She had married him, after all, not for his intellect, but for his honest, manly character, and for the shelter and position he could give her. She had believed, in the innocence of her heart, that these things would be enough. She was beginning to perceive that they were not.

When he was gone, she went to the morning-room, where her books were kept, and took down a volume of Plato. It was the *Phaedo*, and she had been reading it the evening before, but had been interrupted by the entrance of her husband, who had looked at the book with a kind of good-natured wonder, and had said, “I say, Helena, that’s rather heavy reading for a woman, isn’t it? I should have thought you’d have been better employed in looking over the housekeeping books. Mrs. Birtle’s been complaining about the price of butter, and I shouldn’t wonder if the woman’s cheating us. You’d better see to it.”

She had put the book away then, with a sigh that was almost involuntary, and had applied herself to the household accounts. But this morning, alone in the morning-room, with the sun streaming in through the diamond-paned window, and the birds singing in the old yew-tree outside, she felt the old hunger return, and she opened the book with a eagerness that was itself a confession of weakness.

For an hour she read, and the world of ideas opened out before her like a wide, fair landscape. The arguments for the immortality of the soul, the noble death of Socrates, the grand, serene philosophy that looked upon the body as a prison-house—all these things moved her to the depths of her being, and she was conscious of a delight that was almost pain. But by and by the delight faded, and a sense of loneliness came over her. What was the use of all this thinking, if there was no one to share it with? What was the good of climbing to the mountain-top, if one must sit there alone, with no companion but the clouds and the stars? She had read that morning of Socrates discoursing with his friends in the prison, and the contrast between that noble fellowship and her own isolation struck her with a force that brought the tears to her eyes.

She was still sitting with the book on her lap when the butler, Primmer, brought in the letters. There was one from her old schoolfellow, Mary Acton, and Helena opened it eagerly. Mary had married a dissenting minister in the north, and her letters were full of the simple, earnest life she and her husband led, of their studies together, of the sermons they read and discussed, of the little improvements they made in their cottage. It was a narrow life, no doubt, compared with that of the Beechcroft Priory, but it had the one thing that Helena’s life lacked—companionship of thought.

“I sometimes think,” Mary wrote, “that the best part of marriage is not the love, but the understanding. Love is a good thing, but it is like the sunshine; it warms us, but it does not make us grow unless the soil is right. And the soil is the common interest, the common purpose, the willingness to learn and to teach. Without that, marriage is but a friendly truce, a sort of armed neutrality, in which each side holds its own ground, but neither advances.”

Helena laid down the letter and looked out of the window. The words had touched the very core of her trouble. She loved Edmund Torrington, but she was beginning to perceive that love without intellectual sympathy is a plant that may strike root, but will never flower. And the question that pressed upon her was this: should she go on reading and thinking alone, widening the gulf between herself and her husband by every hour she gave to the life of the mind, or should she put away her books and give herself up wholly to the duties of her station, becoming the pattern of a country squire’s wife, but resigning all hope of that higher companionship which she had once dreamed of?

It was a hard question, and one that she was not likely to answer in a day. She was too young, and too much under the influence of her present mood, to see that there might be a middle course—that the cultivation of her own mind need not necessarily estrange her from her husband, and that the renunciation of her intellectual life would not necessarily make her a better wife. She did not yet know that the true secret of marriage is not identity of interest, but mutual respect, and that a man may be a good husband without being a good philosopher.

She sat for a long time, with the book and the letter before her, and the conflict in her heart was sharp. On the one side was the drawing of a strong, inherited tendency, the love of knowledge for its own sake, the desire to grow, to expand, to reach out towards the unseen and the eternal. On the other was the appeal of duty, the claims of affection, the fear of becoming isolated and eccentric, the dread of losing her husband’s confidence, and the vision of a quiet, useful, uneventful life, spent in the service of others, but barren of all that made life beautiful to her.

She was still debating when the door opened, and Edmund himself came in, flushed with exercise, and carrying in his hand a specimen of the new drill.

“Well, Helena,” he cried, “I’ve been to see Chawner’s machine, and I’m bound to say it works uncommon well. I’ve brought back a bit of the gearing to show to Merton. It’s a queer-looking contrivance, but it sows the seed as regular as clockwork, and saves a power of labour. I shouldn’t wonder if we have it on the home farm next year.”

He laid the piece of iron on the table, and looked at her with the simple, good-natured satisfaction of a man who has made a discovery and wishes to share it. Helena looked at the gearing, and then at her husband’s face, and something in the honest, boyish pleasure of his expression touched her. She put out her hand and took up the piece of iron, examining it with an attention that was not wholly feigned.

“It is very ingenious,” she said. “But does it not require a good deal of skill to manage?”

“Not a bit of it,” said Edmund. “Any fool could manage it. But I say, Helena,” he added, catching sight of the book on the table, “you’ve not been at that old Greek fellow again, have you? I thought you’d given him up.”

“I was reading a little while you were gone,” she said, and there was a slight tremor in her voice.

“Well, well,” he said, good-naturedly, “I suppose women must have their fancies, like men theirs. Only don’t overdo it, you know. A little learning’s a dangerous thing, as the poet says. Come, let’s have lunch, and I’ll tell you all about Chawner’s drill.”

He went out, humming a tune, and Helena stood for a moment looking at the book and the letter, and the piece of iron gearing that lay between them. She knew now what the struggle meant. It was not a struggle between duty and inclination, but between two selves—the self that loved knowledge and the self that loved her husband. And she knew, with a clearness that was almost painful, that she could not satisfy both.

She put the book back on the shelf, and the letter into her desk. Then she went in to lunch, and listened with exemplary patience to Edmund’s account of the new drill. But that night, when the house was quiet, she took the book down again, and read by candlelight, with a sense of guilt and a sense of joy that were inextricably mingled. The life of the mind was too strong for her; she could not give it up. She would go on, as wisely and as moderately as she could, cultivating her own intelligence, and striving, not without hope, to make it a source of strength rather than of division. But the renunciation was there, and the loneliness, and the consciousness that, in the great battle of life, she was fighting, and must fight, alone.
