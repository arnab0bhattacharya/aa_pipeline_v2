The heat lay upon the mountain like a great, soft paw, pressing them, as they mounted the zig-zag path, into a common perspiration, a common subservience to the air, which tasted of crushed thyme and ancient, baked dust. Eleanor, stopping to wipe her neck with a handkerchief already dark with damp, felt the sun not merely shine but strike, boring through her grey cotton blouse into the very marrow of her bones. It was an annihilating heat, yet it did not destroy; rather, it seemed to scoop them out, leaving them hollow, translucent, so that their thoughts rang in their heads with the clarity of bells. Below them, the Bay of San Stefano had hardened into a sheet of hammered copper, and the town, with its brown roofs and prickly cypresses, resembled a shaken-out cloth, dotted, frayed at the edges where the white houses straggled into the scrub.

Cyril, a little way ahead, struck his stick against a stone, and the sharp crack roused Gerald, who had been lagging, his hands in his pockets, staring with a kind of sulky intensity at a lizard that darted across the white dust.

“We shall never get there before evening,” Gerald said, breathing hard, his face flushed to a dark, congested red. He addressed the remark to the air, but it was meant for Lucy, who was walking just behind him, carrying her sketchbook under one arm. She had tied a lilac scarf beneath her chin, and the ends of it blew out, brushing his shoulder.

“What does it matter?” Lucy said, her voice light, detached, as if she were speaking from the deck of a moving ship. She was looking upwards, where the ruins of the watchtower jutted against a sky of bleached, blinding white. “If we don’t get there, we don’t. It’s the walking—” she paused, sucking in her breath as her boot caught on a root, “—it’s the walking that is—”

“Exquisite,” Cyril supplied, turning his head to give her a smile that showed his long, yellow teeth. He was a man of forty, bald, with a stoop, but his eyes were youthful, mocking, and perpetually amused. He had the trick of making whatever he said sound slightly ironical, as though the whole fabric of life were a rather poor joke which he was too polite to laugh at.

Gerald scowled. He hated irony; he liked facts, solid, heavy, respectable, like the furniture in his father’s house in Surrey. And he hated Cyril, partly because Cyril was ironical, and partly because Lucy looked at him when he spoke, narrowing her eyes, as if she were trying to catch the exact shade of his meaning.

“You always say that,” Gerald burst out, stopping dead. “You always say it doesn’t matter. But it does matter. We started at two, expressly to see the view before the haze came up. Now it’s half-past four, and we’re not half-way. If we’d walked instead of sitting about in that wretched olive-grove—”

“Oh, the olive-grove!” Eleanor murmured. She had fallen behind, and stood now, a little apart, looking down the hillside. The words stirred something in her; a memory, not of this olive-grove but of another, years ago, in Italy, when she had been young, and a man had picked up a leaf and said—what had he said? The words had gone; only the leaf remained, grey-green, silky, with a little bend in the stalk. She saw it as clearly as if it lay in her palm. Life was like that, she thought; the events, the people, the very words, dissolved, leaving only leaves, colours, the shapes of things. She sighed, a long, soft sigh, and moved forward to rejoin the others.

Cyril had sat down on a boulder. He was taking off his boots.

“What are you doing?” Gerald demanded, outraged.

“Resting my feet. They’re on fire. I suggest you all do the same. There’s a perfectly good patch of shade under that rock. And look—there’s a stream, or at least a trickle. Lucy, come and put your feet in the water.”

Lucy laughed, a little uncertainly, and glanced at Gerald. But Gerald had turned away, and was staring fixedly at the bay, his back rigid, his hands clenched. She hesitated, then went over to Cyril, and sat down on the grass beside the trickle of water. It was barely a stream, only a thread of moisture sliding over the smooth, grey stones, but it was cool, and she dipped her fingers in it, and then pressed her wet fingers to her wrists.

“It’s delicious,” she said.

“Isn’t it?” Cyril said. He had stretched out his legs, and was leaning back on his elbows, his face tilted up to the sky. “I don’t care if I never reach the top. I don’t care if I never see the view. I’m perfectly happy here. Gerald can go on alone if he wants to.”

“Don’t,” Lucy said, in a low voice. “You know he won’t go alone.”

“Why not? He’s a grown man. He has two legs. He can walk.”

“You know what he’s like. He thinks it’s—well, a question of principle. We said we’d do it, so we must do it.”

“Principle!” Cyril snorted. “The most murderous word in the English language. More people have been killed by principle than by all the diseases put together. However, I surrender. I’ll put my boots on. But first—” he turned and looked at her, and his eyes, under their heavy lids, were suddenly serious, without a trace of irony— “first, tell me something. Why did you come?”

Lucy looked down at the water. It was very difficult to answer him, because she did not know the answer, or rather, she knew it, but it was made of so many layers, so many conflicting impulses, that to peel them all away would take hours, and the core, when reached, might be nothing at all—a mere wisp of feeling, a fancy. She had come because Eleanor had asked her; because the house was stuffy; because she had wanted, desperately, to get away from the drawing-room, where the piano stood open and the music, Chopin’s Nocturnes, hung in the air like a scent that one has loved and now finds cloying. She had come because of Gerald, and because of Cyril, and because of the mountain itself, which she had seen from her window every morning, blue, remote, inviting, and had thought, I must go up there, I must see what lies on the other side.

“I don’t know,” she said. “Do you ever know why you do things?”

“Rarely,” Cyril said. “But I thought you might. You seem so—so clear.”

“Clear!” She laughed. “I’m the muddiest person alive.”

“No. You’re not muddy. You’re—” he paused, picking up a pebble and turning it over in his fingers— “you’re like a glass with water in it. One can see right through to the bottom. But the bottom is just sand, and a few pebbles. There’s nothing there.”

She felt, suddenly, that he had seen something; not the real her, for there was no real her, but the shape of her, the outline, which was all that anyone could ever see. And she felt, too, a tenderness for him, because he had looked, and because his voice, for once, was without mockery.

Eleanor, watching them from the path, saw them sitting together, Cyril’s dark head and Lucy’s lilac scarf, and thought, How beautiful they look, like a picture, a small, bright group against the grey rock. She had no jealousy; she was past that. She felt only a mild, maternal pleasure in their youth, their intensity, their seriousness, which seemed to her both touching and absurd. They were so eager, so agonizingly in earnest, about nothing at all. Or rather, about everything, which was the same thing.

Gerald had not moved. He stood like a monument, his back to them, staring at the view. But he was not seeing the view; he was seeing Lucy’s hair, which had escaped from under the scarf and was blowing in little dark curls against her neck. He wanted to go to her, to push Cyril aside, to say, “Look at me, Lucy, look at me, not him.” But he could not. He was held by a paralysis of pride, of shyness, of some deep, inarticulate desire that he could not name. He felt that if he moved, something would break, something irrevocable.

They mounted the last slope in silence. The path was steeper now, loose with shale, and the sun, sinking westward, threw long, violet shadows across the rock. The air had cooled a little, and there was a smell of wild marjoram, strong, pungent, medicinal. Cyril had put his boots on, and walked ahead, whistling softly through his teeth. Lucy followed, her sketchbook forgotten under her arm. Eleanor came last, moving slowly, carefully, her eyes on the ground.

At the top, they stopped. The view burst upon them, not gradually but all at once, like an explosion of light and colour. The island lay below them, spread out like a map, but a map transformed, transfigured, by the evening light into something not of earth. The sea was no longer copper but deep, luminous violet, shading to rose where the sun touched it, and the mountains on the far horizon were not solid but made of mist, blue, ethereal, like the mountains in a Chinese painting. The town was a cluster of toy blocks, brown and white, with the cypresses sticking up like dark pins. And away to the east, the plain stretched out, fertile, checkered with green and gold, fading into a haze of silver.

Nobody spoke. They stood there, four isolated figures on a bare rock, and the silence was not the absence of sound but a positive thing, a presence, enveloping them, drawing them together, then holding them apart. Gerald felt the silence like a physical pressure; he wanted to cry out, to shatter it, to say, “This is what I meant, this is what I wanted.” But he could not. The words were too small, too trivial, for the vastness that pressed upon him. Lucy felt her eyes fill with tears; she did not know why; it was the beauty, she thought, but it was not only the beauty, it was the pain of beauty, the sense that it would pass, that it was already passing, that she was standing in the middle of something which she could not hold. Eleanor, looking at the sea, thought of the leaf, the grey-green leaf, and of all the things that had passed and were passing, and would pass, and she felt a quiet, almost joyful acceptance, as if she were a leaf herself, drifting on a current, without weight, without resistance.

Cyril, alone, seemed unmoved. He stood with his hands in his pockets, his head thrown back, looking at the sky. But his face, in the evening light, was strangely softened, and the lines round his mouth were deeper, as if he had been suffering.

They began the descent. The light was going fast; the violet had deepened to purple, and the first stars were pricking through the sky.
