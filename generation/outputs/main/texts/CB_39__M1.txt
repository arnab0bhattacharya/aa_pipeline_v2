Captain Maitland stood at the window of the inn parlour, watching the smoke rise from the tall chimneys of Marshfold. The day was dull and heavy; a south-westerly wind drove the rain in slanting sheets against the glass, blurring the view of the mill-yard and the huddled rows of weavers’ cottages. It was a dreary scene—a place of soot and stone, where the very air tasted of coal-dust and damp wool. Yet there was a fierce vitality in the place, a restless energy that Maitland could feel even through the walls. He had been billeted here a fortnight, sent to keep order among a population that was half-starved and wholly rebellious. The Luddites were abroad, smashing frames and threatening masters, and the magistrates had grown nervous.

He turned from the window as the door opened. It was his sister, Clara, her bonnet dripping, her cheeks flushed with the raw wind.

"You are wet through," said Maitland, taking her cloak. "Was the school dismissed early?"

"No, the children were kept late to learn their catechism. But, Edward, I must speak to you. I have seen something that troubles me."

Maitland looked at her keenly. Clara was young, gentle, and possessed of a heart that was too easily moved. "What is it? Has anyone insulted you? If the weavers have been insolent—"

"No, no. It is Miss Arden, the teacher. You know her—the quiet girl who reads the lessons with such distinctness. She was weeping today, Edward. She tried to hide it, but I saw her tears fall upon the Bible. And she was trembling when she left."

Maitland frowned. "Miss Arden? The weaver’s daughter? I have noticed her. She has a proud look, but she is poor enough."

"It is her father," Clara whispered, glancing towards the door as if the walls had ears. "They say he is to be arrested. Is it true?"

Maitland’s face hardened. The softness vanished from his eyes, replaced by the iron discipline of the soldier. "It is true. I have the warrant in my pocket. Thomas Arden is a seditious rascal. He has been preaching rebellion to the men at the mill, inciting them to burn the frames. The magistrates demand his arrest."

"But, Edward, he is an old man! And he is ill. To drag him to York Gaol in this weather—it will be his death."

"Justice knows no weather, Clara. And sedition is a disease that must be cut out, lest it infect the whole body of the state. I go to take him within the hour."

Clara wrung her hands. "Oh, that I should have to see Miss Arden again on Sunday, knowing that we have been the instruments of her misery! She is so good, so pious. She teaches the little ones with such patience. To think that her father—"

"Goodness in the daughter does not whitewash the guilt of the father," said Maitland sternly. "Do not let your sentiment blind you to the danger of these times. If the mob rises, it is my duty to suppress them, and it is the duty of the magistrates to punish the ring-leaders. Go to your room, Clara. This is no work for you."

He buckled on his sword and pulled on his heavy greatcoat. The rain was beating harder now, a relentless drumming against the slate roofs of the village. Maitland stepped out into the street, followed by two of his men. The wind howled down the narrow lane, whipping the smoke from the chimneys into a grey fog. They marched past the shuttered shops and the silent mill, their boots splashing in the puddles.

The Arden cottage stood at the end of a row, a low hovel of stone and thatch. Maitland knocked sharply on the door. There was a long pause, and then the sound of a bolt being drawn.

The door opened to reveal the girl, Miss Arden. She stood in the doorway, a small, slight figure, her dark hair pushed back from a face that was pale but composed. She looked at Maitland, and then at the soldiers behind him. She did not flinch, but her eyes—dark and luminous—blazed with a sudden fire.

"Captain Maitland," she said. Her voice was low, but it carried over the noise of the wind. "You have come for my father."

"I have, Miss Arden. I have a warrant for his arrest. Stand aside."

She did not move. "He is very ill. He is in bed. Have you no mercy?"

"I have my orders," said Maitland. He felt a strange unease under her gaze. She was not afraid of him; she was judging him. "I must do my duty."

"Duty?" She laughed, a short, bitter sound. "Is it duty to crush the starving? Is it duty to serve a master who grinds the faces of the poor? You call it law, Captain, but we call it oppression. The scripture says, 'Woe unto them that decree unrighteous decrees.' Do you never read your Bible?"

Maitland felt a flush of anger rise to his face. "I will not bandy texts with you, girl. Let me pass."

"I will not," she said, and she planted her small feet firmly on the threshold. "You shall not take him while I stand here. If you want him, you must strike me down first."

The soldiers shifted uneasily behind him. Maitland looked at her—this frail, passionate creature defying the might of the King’s army. He saw the weaver’s spirit in her, the fierce, unyielding pride of the North. He knew he could brush her aside with one hand, but something held him back. It was not fear, but a sudden, shocking recognition of her righteousness. She was wrong in the law, but she was right in her love.

"Miss Arden," he said, his voice harder than he intended, "do not be a fool. Resistance is useless. If you force me to use violence, it will go hard with you."

"Use it then," she cried, her eyes filling with tears that she refused to let fall. "Show the world what sort of man you are. Beat a sick old man and his daughter in the rain. That is the glory of the militia, is it not?"

The wind roared through the lane, rattling the shutters. The rain fell in torrents, soaking Maitland’s coat, plastering the girl’s hair to her forehead. For a long moment, the two stood locked in a silent battle of wills. Maitland felt the weight of his commission, the stern voice of the law in his ear, but he heard, too, the thunder of his own heart.

"Let him come," said a voice from within the cottage. It was a weak, rasping sound, barely audible above the storm. "Let him come, Mary. Do not shame us by begging."

The girl, Mary, turned her head. "Father!"

"Let him in, child. The Lord’s will be done."

Mary stepped back, her resistance broken not by Maitland’s force, but by her father’s command. She looked at Maitland with a look of such profound contempt that he felt it like a physical blow.

"Go in," she said. "Do your work. But remember this, Captain Maitland: there is a Judge higher than your magistrates, and He keeps a strict account."

Maitland entered the cottage, the soldiers at his heels. The room was dark and cold, the fire burnt low. The old weaver lay in a truckle bed, his face grey with illness, his chest heaving. He looked at Maitland with eyes that were clear and steady.

"I am ready," he said.

Maitland nodded to his men. They lifted the old man from the bed. Mary stood by the hearth, her hands clasped, her lips moving in silent prayer. She did not look at Maitland again.

As they carried the prisoner out into the storm, Maitland paused at the door. He looked back at the girl standing alone in the gloom. The firelight caught her face, illuminating the tears that now streamed freely down her cheeks. She was weeping for her father, for her people, for the injustice of the world.

He turned away and stepped out into the rain, the wind tearing at his coat, the mud sucking at his boots. He felt a sudden, violent distaste for his profession. He had done his duty, but he felt like a thief in the night. The words she had spoken echoed in his mind, mixing with the roar of the wind and the beat of the rain. *Woe unto them that decree unrighteous decrees.*

"March," he ordered his men, his voice rough. "Back to the inn."

As they walked through the flooded streets of Marshfold, the lights of the mill glowed yellow and sinister in the darkness. The machinery was still turning, grinding out profit from the misery of men like Thomas Arden. Maitland walked on, his head bowed against the storm, feeling the chill of the Yorkshire night penetrate to his very bones. He was the instrument of order, the guardian of peace, but as he looked at the bleak, rain-swept landscape, he wondered if he was not merely the jailer of a people who had been condemned without a crime. The wind howled around him, a wild, mournful sound, like the cry of a soul in torment.
