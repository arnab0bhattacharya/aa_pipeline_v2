The wedding was fixed for the Saturday, and on the Friday afternoon, by a kind of tacit indulgence of the firm, Miss Ada Pemberton went up to the City for the last time to clear out the little drawer of her desk and to say her goodbyes. It was a raw, yellow-grey afternoon, the sort of afternoon when the City seems all chimney-pots and draughts, and the clerks stand at the corners of the courts with their hands deep in their pockets, staring at nothing in particular. She had come in her good coat and her hat with the wing in it, and she felt the strangeness of walking up Ludgate Hill at an hour when she was usually at her typewriter, with the great roar of the printing presses coming up through the pavement and the omnibuses grinding past the church.

Ten years, she thought, as she went in under the archway of Messrs. Craddock and Vane’s, and up the stone stairs that smelt of dust and paraffin. Ten years of that stair, and the brass plate with the name worn at the corner where a thousand hands had polished it. She had come at seventeen, a raw girl from Wandsworth, to be junior shorthand-typist at twelve shillings a week, and she had watched the firm change and the partners die and the ledgers grow dusty, and now she was thirty-one and the head of the typing room, and she was giving it up.

Mr. Craddock was in his room, and he put down his pen and looked at her over his spectacles with the queer, shy kindness that had always been his way with her. “So it is tomorrow, Miss Pemberton,” he said. “Well, well. We shall miss you. I don’t know how the correspondence will get on without you. There is nobody else who can read my handwriting, I am sure.”

She laughed and said it was only a matter of practice, and he said, “No, no, it is a gift,” and then there was a little pause, and he cleared his throat and said that Mr. Harbottle was a very fortunate man, and that she must let them know if ever she wanted anything, and that her salary would be paid to the end of the month, which was more than the articles allowed. She thanked him, and her voice was not quite steady, and then she went into the typing room, where the girls had got a little bunch of chrysanthemums for her and had tied it up with a ribbon, and she kissed them all round and promised to come and see them, and she knew perfectly well that she would not come, and so did they.

When they had gone back to their machines she sat down at her own desk by the window and began to sort out the drawer. It was extraordinary what had accumulated in ten years. There were pencils worn down to stubs, and a pen-wiper, and a tin of throat lozenges, and a timetable of the South-Western Railway of 1898, and a programme of a concert at the Crystal Palace, and a photograph of a young man in a boater who had been engaged to her once and had gone to South Africa and had died of enteric, and she looked at it a moment and then put it in the waste-paper basket, not without a certain tenderness, for she was not a hard woman, but she had no intention of carrying that particular piece of the past into her new life. And there were the letters, of course—the letters she had copied in her beautiful clear hand, the invoices and the bills of lading and the dull, interminable business of Craddock and Vane, wholesale druggists and drysalters, and she thought how strange it was that all those words, so many millions of them, had gone out of her fingers into the world and had done their work and vanished, and nobody would ever know that it was Ada Pemberton who had made them legible.

She was folding up her apron when the door opened and old Mr. Vane came in, the junior partner, a stout, wheezy, red-faced man who had been kind to her in his blunt fashion for a decade. “Ah, Miss Pemberton,” he said. “Thought I should catch you. Going to be married tomorrow, eh? Well, I hope you’ll be very happy. Harbottle’s a good sort of man, I believe—retired from the tea trade, isn’t he? Yes. Well, you’ll have a nice little home at Streatham, I dare say, and a servant, and all the rest of it. That’s the proper thing for a woman. I always say the proper place for a woman is her home. No offence, Miss Pemberton, but I do.”

“None taken, Mr. Vane,” she said, smiling.

“You’ve been a good girl,” he went on, “and a clever one, and I don’t mind telling you that when you go we shall have to get two girls to do your work, and they won’t do it as well. But there it is. A woman ought to be married. It’s nature. And Harbottle’s a steady man with a good balance at his bank, and you’ll be very comfortable. Very comfortable indeed.”

He shook her hand and went out, wheezing, and she stood by the window with her parcel of little things under her arm and looked down into the court. A van was unloading at the warehouse opposite, and a boy was sweeping the mud into the gutter, and a cat sat on a window-sill washing its face with a slow, deliberate paw. It was all exactly as it had been for ten years, and tomorrow it would go on exactly the same, and she would not be there. That was the extraordinary thing—that the world should be so indifferent. She had a sudden, absurd vision of herself as a little clock that had been ticking in a corner of that office for ten years, and now they were taking the clock away and putting it on a shelf at Streatham, and it would go on ticking there, but nobody would listen to it.

She went down the stairs for the last time, and out into the yellow afternoon, and stood a moment on the pavement, and the roar of the City came round her like a sea. She thought of her lodgings in Bloomsbury—the two rooms with the gas-fire and the little balcony where she grew nasturtiums in the summer, and the landlady who was a dragon and the cat that was a comfort, and the evenings when she came home tired and made herself a cup of tea and read a novel from the circulating library, and nobody asked her where she had been or what she had done. She thought of her wage—thirty-five shillings a week now, and her own to spend, and the little bank-book with the money in it that was hers, that she had earned, that nobody had given her. And she thought of Mr. Harbottle, who was fifty-five and kind and clean and had a house at Streatham with a garden and a greenhouse, and who had said to her, not unkindly, “Of course, my dear, you’ll give up all that typewriting nonsense. I couldn’t have my wife working in an office. It wouldn’t be respectable.”

She had said yes, because she was fond of him and because it was the sensible thing and because all the world said so, and because she was thirty-one and tired of lodgings and landladies, and because a woman must look to her future. But standing there on the pavement, with the chrysanthemums in her hand and the roar of the City in her ears, she had a moment of clear, cold vision, and she saw that she was about to exchange a small, real independence for a large, comfortable dependence, and that the bargain was not so obviously good as Mr. Vane supposed. She would have a house, and a servant, and a garden, and a husband who was kind to her; and she would have no wage of her own, and no desk of her own, and no door that she could shut, and no evening that was hers, and no name in the world but Mrs. Harbottle. She would be a clock on a shelf.

Then the moment passed, as such moments do, and she smiled at her own folly, and thought that she was a lucky woman to have a good man and a home, and that she was tired of the City and the fog and the typewriter, and that it would be very pleasant to have a garden. She gathered up her parcel and her chrysanthemums and went down Ludgate Hill to the station, and the omnibuses ground past her, and the presses roared, and the clerks went home in their thousands, and the yellow afternoon closed down over the City, and nobody in all that multitude knew or cared that a little clock had stopped ticking in Craddock and Vane’s.
