It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a clergyman in possession of a small income and a large library, must be in want of a pupil to read to him. Mr. Arthur Penrose, the Reverend gentleman of the modest parsonage of Little Wickham in Hampshire, had long possessed the former, and was now, through the benevolent intercession of his neighbor, Mr. Hallam, suddenly provided with the latter.

Mr. Hallam was a man of considerable estate and moderate wit, who had recently lost his wife and found his remaining daughter, Miss Clara Hallam, to be of a disposition so idle and her appetites for intellectual diversion so unbounded, that he deemed it necessary to engage a tutor. He had selected Mr. Penrose not for his pedagogical prowess, which was untested, but for his reputation as a man of serious habits and unblemished character. Mr. Penrose, who had spent the previous three years in a state of quiet, solitary observation of Miss Hallam’s figure as she walked across the common, accepted the appointment with a trembling hand and a heart that beat with a rapidity he was convinced could only be attributed to the sudden influx of cold air into his study.

The first lesson was appointed for the following Tuesday. Mr. Penrose spent the intervening days in a state of agitation that bordered on the pathological. He read his own books, only to find them devoid of meaning. He walked his garden, only to find the roses intrusive. He had prepared a list of texts suitable for a young lady of refinement, ranging from the lightest novels of the season to the more substantial works of moral philosophy, and had already discarded half of them as either too frivolous or too dense. He had settled, with great satisfaction, on a selection of essays by Mrs. Barbauld, believing them to offer the perfect balance of amusement and edification.

On the morning of the appointed day, Mr. Penrose dressed with a care that was unusual for him. He checked his reflection in the mirror of the parlor, ensuring that his cravat was tied with the precise symmetry that denoted a man of taste, and that his hair was powdered with a dignity that did not suggest vanity. He arrived at Hallam House precisely at ten o’clock, neither a minute before nor a minute after, for punctuality, he had decided, was the first step toward self-respect.

Miss Hallam was in the drawing room, seated before a fire that was burning too brightly. She looked up as he entered, and Mr. Penrose felt a sudden, inexplicable chill. She was wearing a gown of pale blue muslin, which seemed to him, in his disordered state, to be of a color so vivid as to be offensive to the eyes.

"Mr. Penrose," she said, her voice clear and steady. "My father has mentioned that you are to assist me with my reading. I trust you have brought something suitable."

Mr. Penrose felt his throat constrict. He reached for the book he had placed on the side table, his fingers brushing against the leather binding with a sensitivity that was almost painful. "I have selected a few works," he managed to say, his voice sounding thin and distant to his own ears. "I thought the essays of Mrs. Barbauld might provide an agreeable start."

Miss Hallam nodded, a gesture so slight it might have been an accident. "Mrs. Barbauld is a fine writer," she said. "Though I confess I find her tone somewhat severe. I prefer something lighter. Something that does not demand so much of the mind."

Mr. Penrose was taken aback. He had expected a request for a novel, perhaps, or a collection of poetry. The preference for lightness was not entirely unexpected, but the manner in which she expressed it, with such detached indifference, gave him pause.

"We may begin with the first essay," he said, opening the book. "It is on the subject of moral responsibility."

Miss Hallam settled into her chair, crossing her legs with a grace that seemed to mock his own stiffness. She opened the book to the indicated page, but did not look at it. Instead, she watched him over the rim of the pages, her eyes fixed on his face with an intensity that made him wish he were anywhere else in the world.

"Shall I read?" she asked.

"You are welcome to read," Mr. Penrose said, though he had intended to read aloud himself. The idea of listening to her voice recite the dry, abstract language of moral philosophy was, to his mind, a form of torture. "Or I may read to you, if you prefer."

"I prefer to read myself," she said. "It helps me to concentrate."

And so the lesson began. Mr. Penrose sat in the chair opposite her, his hands folded in his lap, his eyes fixed on the back of the book in his hands. He could not look at her. To look at her was to acknowledge the absurdity of the situation, the ridiculousness of his own desire, the sheer impossibility of his position. He was a clergyman, a man of reason and duty, and he was sitting in a drawing room with the object of his affection, listening to her read about the nature of conscience.

The minutes passed with a slowness that was agonizing. Miss Hallam read with a clear, musical voice, but the words seemed to drift through the air without landing. Mr. Penrose found himself analyzing the angle of her shadow on the wall, the dust motes dancing in the light of the fire, the very texture of the carpet under his feet. He tried to listen to the meaning of the text, but his mind was elsewhere, wandering through the corridors of his own embarrassment.

There was a silence that was not empty, but full. It was full of the sound of their breathing, of the crackling of the fire, of the rustle of the pages. It was a silence that spoke volumes, and yet said nothing. Mr. Penrose felt a strange kind of peace in it, a peace that was born of the realization that he was safe. She was not looking at him. She was not judging him. She was simply reading, and he was simply sitting.

When the essay was finished, Miss Hallam closed the book. "That was quite interesting," she said. "Though I am not sure I agree with all of it. I think she is too hard on human nature."

Mr. Penrose opened his mouth to reply, but no words came. He had prepared a response, a thoughtful comment on the nuances of moral agency, but it had evaporated in the heat of the moment.

"Shall we continue?" he asked, his voice rough.

Miss Hallam looked at him, and for a moment, he thought he saw a flicker of amusement in her eyes. But then it was gone, replaced by a polite, distant smile.

"I think we have accomplished enough for today," she said. "My head is beginning to ache."

Mr. Penrose stood up, his legs unsteady. He gathered the book and walked toward the door, his movements stiff and mechanical. As he reached the threshold, he turned back.

"Miss Hallam," he said.

She looked up, her expression neutral.

"Thank you," he said. "For your patience."

She inclined her head slightly. "You are welcome, Mr. Penrose. I look forward to our next lesson."

He left the room, closing the door behind him with a soft click that echoed in his mind. He walked back to the parsonage in the fresh morning air, feeling a strange mixture of relief and disappointment. He had survived the lesson. He had not said anything foolish. He had not fainted. He had maintained his dignity.

And yet, as he sat down at his desk, he found himself reaching for the book of essays, only to realize that he had left it at Hallam House. He would have to return it at the next lesson. He would have to see her again. He would have to sit in that drawing room, in that silence, and listen to her read.

He picked up his pen, intending to write a letter to his brother, but the words would not come. Instead, he stared at the blank page, and for the first time in his life, he wished that he were ill.
