It was in the dusk of a November afternoon, when the gas-lamps were already flaring in the foggy street below, that I discovered the letter. The pensionnat was silent; the day’s hum of recitation and the clash of piano-keys had ceased, and only the heavy tread of the portress echoed in the stone corridor. I had returned to the classroom to fetch a volume I had left upon my desk, but finding the room occupied, I paused in the shadow of the doorway.

Agatha Vane stood by the window, her face turned towards the glass, though she saw nothing of the wet pavement or the hurrying figures of the citizens of Ghent. Her hands were trembling as she held a folded paper, and as I watched, she pressed it to her lips with a fervour that was not the calm affection of a daughter or a sister. It was the desperate, consuming fire of a lover.

I stepped forward. My footfall on the oaken floor startled her; she turned with a sharp cry, crumpling the paper against her breast. Her eyes, usually so dull and heavy with the boredom of conjugations and arithmetic, were wide with a terror that went beyond the fear of punishment.

"Mademoiselle Vane," I said, my voice hard, for I had learned that in this house of rigid discipline, softness was often mistaken for weakness. "What is this?"

"Nothing, Mademoiselle Elwood," she gasped, retreating until her back met the cold wall. "It is nothing—a letter from my mother."

"Liar," I said. The word fell from my lips like a stone. I advanced and held out my hand. "Give it to me. You know the regulations. No correspondence is permitted save through the hands of Madame Héloïse. And that is not a letter from England."

She hesitated, her pale lips quivering. Then, seeing the futility of resistance, she placed the crumpled ball into my palm. I smoothed it out. It was written in a bold, masculine hand—blots of ink here and there where the writer had pressed too hard, betraying a passion as ungovernable as her own. The signature was "Julian." The words were feverish, speaking of meetings in the garden, of a flight to Antwerp, of love that would not be denied by stone walls or iron grates.

I folded the letter and slipped it into my pocket. "You are to come with me," I said. "Madame Héloïse must know of this."

At the mention of the Directrice’s name, Agatha Vane sank to her knees. She did not weep; her grief was too dry and parched for tears. She only looked up at me, and in her gaze I saw a reflection of a desolation I knew well.

"She will send me away," she whispered. "She will write to my father. He will beat me. Or worse—he will lock me in the convent at Bruges. Oh, Mademoiselle, have mercy! I am so lonely here. Is it a sin to love? Is it a sin to wish to be seen?"

Her words struck me with a force that winded me. The room seemed to sway, and for a moment, the gaslight flickered, and I was no longer in Ghent, but back in the drafty schoolroom of my own girlhood in Yorkshire. I remembered the biting wind that swept the moors, and the ache in my chest that came not from cold, but from a hunger for warmth—for a voice that would speak to *me*, and not merely to the pupil. I remembered the terror of being young, and female, and utterly at the mercy of those who held the keys.

I looked down at her. She was plain, this girl—sallow-skinned and thin, with the awkwardness of seventeen. But in her eyes burned a spirit that refused to be extinguished. To crush that spirit, to hand her over to the mercies of Madame Héloïse, who was as cold and unyielding as the marble statues in the vestibule, seemed to me suddenly a crime greater than the breaking of a school rule.

Yet, what was I to do? I was a governess, a dependent. My own position was a precarious raft in a stormy sea. To be dismissed for shielding a rule-breaker would be to cast myself out into the winter of poverty, to face the workhouse or the cold charity of strangers.

"Stand up," I said, my voice trembling despite my effort to control it. "Stand up, Agatha."

She rose, swaying. I walked to the stove, where the coals glowed a dull red. I took the letter from my pocket and held it over the iron door.

"Look," I said. "I am going to burn this. But you must listen to me. You must listen as if your life depended on it, for perhaps it does."

I dropped the paper. It curled, blackened, and vanished into the flames. The destruction of the evidence felt like a liberation, a severing of a chain.

"If I report you," I said, turning to face her, "you will be ruined. This Julian—if he is a man of honour, he will wait; if he is not, you are well rid of him. But you cannot write to him again. Not here. Not while you are under this roof. Do you understand?"

"But I love him," she cried. "He is the only thing that makes this place bearable!"

"Love!" I exclaimed, the word bursting from me with a bitterness I could not suppress. "Do not speak to me of love as if it were a sweetmeat to be enjoyed at leisure. Love is a fire, child, and in this world, it consumes the woman while the man merely warms his hands. You must be stronger than your heart. You must endure."

I gripped her shoulders. They were bird-like, fragile beneath the grey wool of her dress. "I will not betray you," I said. "But you must give me your word. No more letters. No more meetings in the garden. You must be silent as the grave until you leave this school. Can you do that?"

She looked at me, searching my face for the trick, the trap. Finding none, she nodded slowly. "I swear it, Mademoiselle."

"Good," I said. I felt suddenly very old, and very tired. The adrenaline of the moment had ebbed, leaving a hollow ache. "Go now. Wash your face. You look as though you have seen a ghost."

She hesitated, then reached out and touched my hand—a fleeting, warm pressure—before fleeing the room.

I stood alone in the silence. The stove ticked as the metal cooled. Outside, the wind howled against the glass, rattling the panes in their lead frames. I had committed a folly. I had jeopardized my bread and butter for the sake of a girl I barely knew, driven by a phantom of my own past.

Madame Héloïse would expect the report in the morning. If I did not give it, and the truth came out by other means, I was ruined. But as I looked at the grey, swirling fog that pressed against the window, I felt a fierce, illicit surge of triumph. I had defied the order of things. I had chosen mercy over law.

"Vengeance is mine, saith the Lord," I whispered to the empty room, "I will repay." But perhaps, I thought, there are times when He requires us to be His instruments of grace, rather than His ministers of wrath. I picked up my book, blew out the lamp, and walked out into the darkness, carrying my secret like a coal against my heart.
