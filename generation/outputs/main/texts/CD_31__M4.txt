The name of the young man was Nicholas Wren, and if you had beheld him on a certain raw November morning, standing upon the windswept platform of the Bletchley Junction station of the Grand Midland Railway, you would have seen a slender, pale-faced fellow of some two-and-twenty years, in a threadbare greatcoat buttoned to the chin, with a countenance in which eagerness and dread were so strangely mingled that the very porter who swept the cinders from the waiting-room fire paused in his labour to observe him. For Nicholas had come upon a secret—a secret that had lain buried these fifteen years beneath the weight of a lie, and which, having been discovered but three days since, had set his whole world tottering like a house of cards.

His aunt—the formidable Miss Tabitha Wren, proprietress of a lodging-house in a narrow by-street off the Strand, a woman of iron principles and a voice that could penetrate two floors of solid brick—had raised him from infancy upon a diet of cold mutton, colder looks, and the unvarying assurance that his father, one Ambrose Wren, had perished at sea when Nicholas was but seven years old. It was a tale she told with a certain grim satisfaction, as though the Almighty had shown a commendable sense of propriety in removing a ne'er-do-well brother before he could contaminate his offspring. But three days ago, in the dusty recesses of an old bureau, Nicholas had found a bundle of letters—letters in a hand he did not know, addressed to his aunt, and speaking of a man named Ambrose Wren who was not dead at all, but living, and working, and asking only for news of his son.

The shock had been prodigious. The world, which had seemed so solid, had turned to quicksilver beneath his feet. And so here he stood, upon this provincial platform, with the wind cutting through his thin coat and the smoke of a departing engine rolling in grey wreaths about his ankles, waiting to see the face of a father he had mourned as dead.

The station was a busy, clattering place, full of the din of shunting engines and the cries of porters and the hiss of steam; and amid it all, at a little wooden booth beside the booking-office, sat an elderly man in a faded uniform, whose business it was to punch the tickets of the second-class passengers. He was a spare, stooping figure, with hair the colour of wood-ash and a face deeply lined, and there was something in the patient droop of his shoulders that spoke of long years of waiting and of hopes deferred. Nicholas, observing him from a distance, felt his heart contract within him; for there was a something in the set of the man's head, a something in the way he held his ticket-punch as though it were an instrument of solemn import, that stirred a chord of memory so deep and so remote that it seemed to echo from another life.

He approached the booth. The old man looked up, and his eyes—grey eyes, thoughtful and sad—met those of the young man, and for a moment there was silence between them.

"Ticket, sir?" said the old man, in a voice that trembled slightly, as though it were rusty from disuse.

Nicholas fumbled in his pocket and produced a bit of pasteboard—a ticket for a destination he had no intention of reaching—and handed it through the little window. The old man took it, examined it, and raised his punch to mark it; but his hand shook so violently that the instrument clattered against the wood.

"You are not well," said Nicholas, in a voice he scarcely recognised as his own.

"It is nothing, sir," replied the old man. "A touch of the old trouble—the rheumatism, sir, the rheumatism. It comes and goes. I shall be right enough presently."

"You have been long in this employment?"

"Fifteen years, sir. Fifteen years come Michaelmas."

"And before that?"

The old man was silent for a moment. He turned the ticket over in his fingers, and when he spoke again his voice was very low.

"Before that, sir, I was—I was otherwise situated. I had a—a family, sir. A wife, and a little son. But the wife died, and the son—the son was taken from me. I have not seen him these fifteen years."

Nicholas felt the blood rush to his temples. "Taken from you? How taken?"

The old man looked up sharply, as though the question had struck a chord he had long thought silent. "It is a long story, sir, and a sad one, and I doubt whether it would interest a young gentleman like you. But if you would do me the kindness to step inside—for the wind is bitter, sir, and you look as though you had known better days yourself—I will tell it you, such as it is."

Nicholas stepped through the little door and stood within the booth, and the old man motioned him to a stool, and then, with many a pause and many a struggle for composure, he told his tale. He told of a stern sister who had never approved of his marriage; of a wife who had died in childbed; of a business that had failed; of debts that had overwhelmed him; and of a sister who had taken the child and sworn that he should never see it more unless he could provide for it a home worthy of its name. He told of years of struggle and poverty, of letters unanswered, of a heart that had grown sick with hope deferred; and as he spoke, Nicholas saw the tears standing in the old man's eyes, and felt his own throat tighten with a grief too great for words.

"And the child?" he asked at last, in a whisper. "What became of the child?"

The old man shook his head. "I do not know, sir. I have written, and written, and written again; but no answer comes. My sister is a hard woman, sir—a hard woman. She has her virtues, but they are not the virtues of the heart. And so I sit here, day after day, and punch the tickets, and wonder whether my boy is alive or dead, and whether he thinks of me at all, or whether he has been taught to curse my name."

Nicholas rose from the stool. His hands were shaking, and his heart was beating so loudly that he fancied the whole station must hear it. He stood before the old man, and looked into his face, and saw there the features he had studied in the only miniature that had survived the wreck of his father's fortunes—the features he had been taught to believe belonged to a dead man.

"My name," he said, "is Nicholas Wren. And I think—I think, sir, that you are my father."

The old man stared at him. His face went white, then red, then white again. He rose from his chair, and his hands went out as though to grasp the young man's arm, and then fell trembling at his side.

"Nicholas?" he whispered. "My Nicholas? My little Nick?"

"The same, sir."

For a moment there was no sound but the roar of an express train thundering through the station, and the shriek of its whistle, and the clatter of its wheels upon the rails. Then the old man sank back into his chair, and covered his face with his hands, and wept—wept as Nicholas had never seen a man weep before, with great shuddering sobs that seemed to tear themselves from the very depths of his being. And Nicholas, kneeling beside him, put his arms about the old man's shoulders, and held him close, and wept too, for the years that had been lost, and for the years that might yet be saved.

It was some time before either of them could speak. The station clock ticked solemnly upon the wall; the porters shouted; the trains came and went; but in the little ticket-booth there was silence, save for the sound of two hearts that had been long parted and were now, by some strange mercy of Providence, united.

"You will come with me," said Nicholas at last, rising and wiping his eyes. "You will leave this place, and come to London, and we will find a room together—a better room than my aunt's, I promise you—and we will be father and son, as we should have been these fifteen years."

The old man shook his head. "I have no money, Nicholas. I have nothing to offer you but my poverty and my grey hairs."

"And what have I to offer you," cried Nicholas, "but a heart that has hungered for a father's love these fifteen years? Come, sir. Come. The past is past. Let us make what amends we can for the future."

And so it was that, upon that raw November morning, the ticket-collector of Bletchley Junction resigned his post, and walked out of the station arm-in-arm with his son; and if the porters stared to see the old man go, and if the station-master shook his head and muttered something about "queer customers," they were not the less happy for it. For there are reunions, in this world of ours, that make amends for many partings; and there are tears that are better than laughter; and there are moments—rare, brief, and precious—when the long account of human sorrow seems, for an instant, to be balanced by a single act of love.
