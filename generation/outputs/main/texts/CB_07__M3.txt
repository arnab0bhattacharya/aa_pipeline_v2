The soot of Slackcote settled on my white cravat as I walked the rutted lane from the church to the school. It was a grim deposit, the very breath of the valley, where the mills stood like brick cathedrals to Mammon, vomiting smoke into a sullen sky. I was the new curate, sent to shepherd a flock that despised the sight of me. To the operatives, I was the master's man—a sleek, black-coated spy sent to mollify their starvation with scripture. To Mr. Garside and the mill-owners, I was a poor parson, tolerated only to keep the dying from cursing God over loudly on their deathbeds. I belonged nowhere; I was a cipher in a parish of furnaces.

I pushed open the heavy oak door of the schoolroom. The day was raw, and a biting damp crept through the ill-fitting windows. Mrs. Thorne stood at the hearth, endeavoring to coax a flame from a few damp sticks. She was a widow, spare, stern, and pale. Her garments were of plain stuff, but scrupulously neat. She turned as I entered, and her grey eyes met mine. They were searching, unyielding eyes.

"You are early, Mr. Locke," she said. "The children will not be here for half an hour."

"I came to escape the wind, Mrs. Thorne," I replied. "It howls through the vicarage like a banshee."

"It howls louder through the cellars of Marsh Fold," she retorted, turning back to her futile fire. "But they have not the leisure to escape it. They must spin the wool while the damp rots their lungs."

I felt a familiar sting of irritation. Mrs. Thorne had a way of pricking the conscience that felt akin to an affront. I was not the author of their misery. I was merely a man striving to do my duty in a hard world.

"You are severe," I said. "I cannot alter the conditions of the manufacturing district. I can only offer them the bread of life."

"The bread of life is well enough," she answered, "but it is poor sustenance when the belly is empty. You preach submission to the Garsides, Mr. Locke. You tell the weary it is God's will they should toil fourteen hours. Do you think God is deaf to the clatter of those looms?"

"He is deaf to nothing," I returned, my voice rising. "But He works through the established order. I am here to save souls, not to turn the world upside down."

She looked at me then, a long, deliberate look that stripped away my clerical armor. "You are here to salve your own conscience," she said, "and to earn your thirty pounds. But conscience, sir, is a stubborn thing. It will not be quieted by a reading of the Liturgy."

I made no answer. I turned and left the schoolroom, my heart beating thick with anger and a sudden, unwelcome shame. I walked the lane back to my lodgings, the mill-sirens wailing over the moor.

That night, a great storm broke over Slackcote. The rain lashed the panes, and the wind tore at the slates. I sat in my study, a single candle burning low. I tried to read, but the words swam. I saw only Mrs. Thorne's pale, reproachful face. I saw the children of the mill, their faces streaked with grime, their eyes hollow. I thought of the text I had chosen for Sunday: "The poor always ye have with you." It had seemed a simple acknowledgment of fact. Now it sounded like a taunt.

I was roused from my miserable reverie by a knocking at the door. It was a boy, soaked to the bone. "Come to Marsh Fold," he cried. "My mother. She's dying. She'll have no parson."

I rose instantly. I had no umbrella, and my greatcoat was thin. I followed the boy into the tempest. The rain drove against us like needles. We descended the steep, cobbled streets to the mill-workers' cottages. The boy led me to a low door. I stooped and entered.

The room was dark, lit only by a flickering rushlight. The air was thick with the smell of damp wool and sickness. On a pallet in the corner lay a woman. She was young, but the life had been ground out of her. I knelt beside her and took her hand. It was cold. I spoke the words of comfort, the prayers for the dying. She opened her eyes, looked at me, and whispered, "Is it morning? I must be at the loom."

"It is night," I said. "You need not go to the loom."

A spasm crossed her face. "If I am late," she whispered, "they will fine me."

I gripped her hand tighter. "God will not fine you," I said. "You are going to a place where there is no more labor."

She smiled, a faint, heartbreaking smile, and then she was gone. I closed her eyes and rose. In the doorway stood Mrs. Thorne. She had come to sit with the dead. Our eyes met. I expected a word of cold reproof. She said nothing. She only looked at me, and in her eyes I saw not anger, but a profound, sorrowful knowledge.

I turned and walked out into the storm. The rain had ceased, and a wild wind swept the sky. A break in the clouds revealed a single star. I stood there, shivering, and I swore rancorously to myself. I would not be the master's man. I would not be a parson of excuses. I would stand with the broken, or I would break myself. The established order be damned.

I returned to my room and sat down at my desk. I took up my pen and began to write. I did not write a sermon on submission. I wrote a letter to Mr. Garside. I demanded better ventilation, shorter hours, an end to the fines. I knew it would cost me my living. I did not care. I was done with half-measures. The fire in the grate had gone out, but a fiercer fire burned in my breast. I would speak the truth, and let the fires of Slackcote consume me if they would.
