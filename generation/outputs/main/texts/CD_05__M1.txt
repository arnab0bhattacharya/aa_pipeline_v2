It is a truth which the moralists of our own happy island are but too apt to overlook, that a man may be sent upon an errand of the greatest delicacy and the most profound importance, and yet find himself, upon its very first stage, plunged into a sea of such squalid and perplexing troubles as would make the heart of a philosopher sink within him, and that of a practical man (which is a far more valuable organ) turn to stone with indignation. Such was the case with Mr. Arthur Penhaligon, a junior clerk of the firm of Thistlewood, Finch, & Vane, who was dispatched, in the autumn of the year of our Lord 1789, to the wild and agitated shores of France, with a commission that seemed, to his superiors, no more than a simple journey of a few days’ duration, but which was, in fact, as fraught with peril and moral ambiguity as any pilgrimage ever undertaken by a knight of olden time.

Mr. Penhaligon’s charge was to proceed to the Chateau de Verfeuille, in the province of Picardy, there to take possession of certain deeds, securities, and bags of gold coin belonging to the noble house of that name—a family which, having found the atmosphere of their native land becoming rather too warm for comfort, had prudently decamped to more tranquil climes, leaving their worldly goods in the care of their steward, a certain Monsieur Dubois. The journey itself was a trial of the spirit; the roads were a disgrace to any civilized nation, the inns were dens of suspicion and fleas, and the very air, which should have been filled with the simple scents of the country, was thick with the sour smell of fear and the bitter tang of resentment. The villages through which he passed were not the cheerful, bustling communities of his English imagination, but sullen clusters of hovels, where gaunt-faced men stood in their doorways, their eyes following his carriage with a silent, hungry intensity that was far more unnerving than any open outcry.

At length, having rattled and bumped over miles of rutted track, Mr. Penhaligon found himself before the great iron gates of the Chateau de Verfeuille. But what a spectacle met his eyes! The gates, which should have been flung wide in welcome, were shut fast and hung with weeds; the long avenue of elms was choked with fallen leaves; and the chateau itself, a vast pile of stone that had once, no doubt, been the pride of the province, stood like a wounded giant, its windows dark and vacant. It was a picture of profound neglect, of a grandeur that had not so much faded as been actively strangled.

He was admitted, after a considerable delay and a great deal of rattling of chains, not by a liveried servant, but by a scowling fellow in a greasy waistcoat, who led him through a hall where the marble floor was cracked and the portraits of the Verfeuille ancestors looked down from the walls with an air of faded, dusty despair. Here he was met by the steward, Monsieur Dubois. This gentleman was a person of a most singular appearance; he was a man of perhaps fifty years, with a face as round and smooth as a sucking-pig, but with eyes that were small, sharp, and restless, like two black beads set in dough. He was dressed in a coat of a startlingly fine cut, which sat upon his person with the air of a costume rather than a garment, and he wore an enormous diamond pin in his cravat that glittered with a vulgarity that was quite shocking to Mr. Penhaligon’s modest sensibilities.

“Monsieur Penhaligon!” cried this worthy, spreading his hands in a gesture of welcome that was too expansive to be sincere. “You are welcome, welcome! But, ah, what a day, what a country! It is a wilderness of troubles, is it not? You have seen the wolves at our gates, I do not doubt. But come, come, you are safe with me. You are safe with Monsieur Dubois!”

He led Mr. Penhaligon not into a counting-house or a library, but into a vast dining-hall, where a fire of enormous proportions roared in a cavernous fireplace. The table was laid for one, and upon it was spread a feast that would have done credit to a Parisian epicure: a silver tureen of soup, a roast fowl glistening with butter, a pyramid of jellies, and a decanter of wine that was the colour of a ruby. Monsieur Dubois gestured for his guest to be seated. “You see,” he said, with a confidential air, as he carved the fowl with an expert hand, “I maintain the standards. I keep the flag flying. The good family, they are gone, it is true, but the Chateau de Verfeuille, she must not fall! Not while I am here. It is a sacred duty.”

Mr. Penhaligon, who had not eaten anything more substantial than a piece of black bread for two days, found the sight of the food both tempting and profoundly disturbing. “Monsieur Dubois,” he began, endeavouring to keep to the point of his mission, “my instructions are to secure the property of the house of Verfeuille. The deeds, the moneys—”

“Property!” cried Dubois, waving a drumstick dismissively. “Property is a word for lawyers and for men who see nothing but ledgers. We are men of the world, are we not? We see the larger picture. The family is absent. The people, they are like children—hungry, foolish, they see the chateau and they say, ‘Ah, that is where the bread is!’ They do not understand that one must maintain order. One must show them that the world does not change so easily. It is for their own good, you see. It is for their protection.”

As he spoke these last words, a strange thing happened. A great crash was heard from the courtyard, followed by the sound of angry voices. Dubois did not startle; he merely paused, a morsel of fowl halfway to his mouth, and a slow, reptilian smile spread across his plump face. “You hear?” he said softly. “The children at the gate. They are impatient. They do not understand that the larder of the chateau is not the larder of the province.”

Mr. Penhaligon rose and went to the window. He saw not a mob of ruffians, but a collection of villagers, their faces pale and thin, their clothes ragged. They were led by an old man with a white beard, who was pointing up at the chateau and speaking with a desperate passion. They were not armed with pikes or muskets, but with empty sacks and baskets. They were hungry. And as Mr. Penhaligon looked from their desperate faces to the glittering diamond pin in Dubois’s cravat, and from the untouched feast upon the table to the cracked and empty portraits on the wall, a terrible clarity seized him. He had been sent to protect the property of the absent nobles; but the man who now held that property was a parasite, a usurper who was fattening himself upon the carcass of his masters’ estate while the people starved. The gold in the vaults, the deeds in the strongbox—were they the property of a family that had fled its duties? Or were they the last hope of the hungry souls at the gate? The question settled upon his soul not as a matter of law, but as a matter of conscience, and it was a weight far heavier than any bag of gold.
