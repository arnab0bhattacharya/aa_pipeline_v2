The trouble began, as such troubles will, not with the bones themselves but with the tea. Professor Henry Gadsby, of the Marshall Institute, had been three weeks in the Kizil-Kum, and the tea—a black, bitter brew that the caravan men called *chai* and that Gadsby had come to regard as the one civilised institution in an otherwise entirely hostile creation—had just been poured when his assistant, young Frobisher, came clattering down the rocky slope into the camp with his hat in his hand and his face the colour of the desert at noon.

“Professor,” he said, and then stopped, and swallowed, and began again. “Professor, you’d better come. There’s a skull.”

Gadsby set down his tin cup with the deliberation of a man who has learned that the desert punishes haste. “We have found skulls before, Frobisher. The strata are full of them. You have yourself extracted, I believe, some fourteen.”

“Not like this one,” said Frobisher. “It’s not a lizard, sir. It’s not a mammal of the ordinary sort. It’s—” He gestured helplessly with his hat. “It’s *upright*.”

The site lay in a shallow basin of red sandstone some half-mile from the camp, where the wind had scoured away the overburden and left a shelf of older rock exposed. Gadsby went down on his knees in the rubble and looked, and for a long time he said nothing at all. The skull was embedded in the matrix at an angle, but the vault was unmistakable—high, rounded, capacious. The brow was not the low ridge of the ape; it was a true forehead, though heavy, and beneath it the orbits looked out at the professor with the empty patience of a hundred thousand centuries. The jaw was human in its curve, though the teeth were large and the chin receding. And below the skull, where the wind had worried at the stone, a long thigh-bone lay half-revealed, and its articulation was not that of a creature that walked upon four legs. It had walked, that creature. It had stood erect and looked out across this same red waste, and it had done so in a stratum that Gadsby’s own reading of the rocks placed, at the most conservative estimate, a quarter of a million years before the accepted antiquity of man.

“Frobisher,” said Gadsby at last, “we will need the plaster and the linen. And you will say nothing of this to the men. Nothing at all.”

But the men knew. They always know. By nightfall the whole camp was aware that the professor had found a man who was not a man, and the cook, a Tartar of gloomy disposition, had begun to speak of djinns. Gadsby sat in his tent with a candle and wrote a careful letter to the sponsor, Mr. Aubrey Vane, of the Vane Mineral and Development Syndicate, who had financed the expedition on the understanding that it would locate certain copper-bearing seams and that any antiquities discovered would be reported to him first and to the world second, or not at all.

The reply came back by the next caravan, six weeks later, and it was everything that Gadsby had feared. Vane was coming in person. He arrived in a cloud of dust and a solar topee, a large, ruddy, genial man who shook hands with everyone and remembered the cook’s name, and who spent the first evening talking pleasantly of copper and the second evening talking pleasantly of nothing at all, and on the third evening asked to see the bones.

They were laid out now in the larger tent, cleaned and catalogued, and beside them, in a shallow tray of cotton wool, lay the tools. There were four of them: a chopper of quartzite, rudely but deliberately flaked; a scraper; a pointed implement that might have been a awl or a weapon; and a curious flat stone with a hole worn through it, which Gadsby believed had been a weight for a digging-stick or a ceremonial object of some sort. They were not the work of any beast. They were the work of a hand.

Vane looked at the skull for a long time, and then at the tools, and then he lit a cigar and sat down on a camp-stool and looked at Gadsby.

“Well, Professor,” he said, “this is a very remarkable thing.”

“It is the most remarkable thing,” said Gadsby, “that has ever been found. It rewrites the history of the human race. It pushes back the dawn of intelligence—of tool-making, of upright carriage, of everything that separates us from the brutes—by an interval that staggers the imagination. When this is published, Vane, it will be the sensation of the century.”

“Yes,” said Vane, and drew on his cigar. “That’s the trouble.”

“Trouble?”

“My dear fellow. The Syndicate has spent eleven thousand pounds on this expedition. We have found no copper. The seams are there, I don’t doubt it, but they are deep and the transport is impossible and the political situation in this part of the world is—well. You know the political situation as well as I do. The Russians are moving south. The Persians are restless. The whole country is a powder-barrel, and the one thing that would bring every newspaperman in Asia down upon us like a plague of locusts is the announcement that we have dug up a man who is older than Adam.”

“But that is precisely the point,” said Gadsby, his voice rising. “That is precisely why it must be published. The scientific world must be told. The evidence must be examined, corroborated, argued over. That is how knowledge advances, Vane. That is how the truth is established.”

“The truth,” said Vane, “is a very fine thing, and I am as fond of it as any man. But the truth about this particular skull is going to be established by a great many people who have never seen it, and some of them will say it is a hoax, and some will say it is a fraud, and some will say it is an ape, and the newspapers will say all three in the same edition, and the Syndicate will be a laughing-stock, and the concession will be cancelled, and the eleven thousand pounds will be gone. And in the meantime the Russians will have moved another fifty miles south, and there will be a war, and the skull will be in a museum in St. Petersburg with a label on it in a language you cannot read.”

Gadsby was silent for a moment. The candle guttered. Outside, the wind made its long, dry, patient sound across the sand.

“What do you propose?” he said at last.

“I propose,” said Vane, “that we pack the bones in a crate and send them down to the coast, and that we say nothing at all until the political situation is settled and the concession is secure. A year, perhaps two. Then you may publish whatever you like, and I will pay for the monograph myself.”

“And if I refuse?”

Vane spread his hands. “You are a scientist, Professor. I am a businessman. I do not threaten you. I merely observe that the Syndicate owns the concession, and the concession covers this entire basin, and without the concession you cannot dig, and without digging you cannot find anything more. The bones you have are safe. The bones you might find are not. Think it over.”

Gadsby thought it over for three days. On the fourth day he called Frobisher into his tent and told him what Vane had said, and Frobisher, who was young and hot-headed, said that it was a damned shame and that they should wire the Royal Society at once. And Gadsby said that he had considered that, and that he had also considered the fact that the Royal Society had no authority in the Kizil-Kum and that Vane had the camels and the money and the concession. And Frobisher said that in that case they should at least make a full photographic record and a careful description, and send a sealed copy to the Marshall Institute, so that if anything happened to the bones the truth would not be lost. And Gadsby looked at him for a long moment and then said, “Frobisher, you are a better man than I am.”

So they did that. They photographed the skull from every angle, and the tools, and the thigh-bone in situ; they made drawings and measurements and a full descriptive memoir in Gadsby’s neat, cramped hand; and they sealed the whole in a tin box and addressed it to the Secretary of the Marshall Institute, and gave it to a caravan man named Yusuf, who was known to be honest and who was going down to the coast with the next shipment of specimens. And then they packed the bones in a crate of straw and nailed it shut, and Vane took it away with him when he left, and the camp was very quiet, and the wind went on making its long, dry, patient sound across the sand.

Gadsby stayed another two months and found nothing more. The strata were barren. The copper was not there, or not where they could reach it. And in the spring, when the expedition was wound up and the tents were struck and the men were paid off, Gadsby rode down to the coast with his notebooks and his photographs and his memories, and he did not look back.

The tin box reached the Marshall Institute in due course. The memoir was read, and discussed, and filed away, and the photographs were examined by three Fellows, one of whom said that the skull was probably a pathological modern specimen and the tools were certainly intrusive, and another of whom said that the whole thing was very interesting but that without the actual bones it was impossible to say anything definite, and the third of whom said nothing at all. And in the end the matter was allowed to drop, and the war came, and the Russians moved south, and the concession was cancelled, and the Syndicate was wound up, and Aubrey Vane died in 1916 of a complication following an operation, and the crate of bones was never seen again.

Gadsby lived to be eighty-one. He never published the memoir. But in his old age, when he was asked about the great discoveries of his career, he would sometimes fall silent, and look out of the window at the grey English rain, and say that the greatest discovery he had ever made was the one he had been unable to prove. And then he would smile, a little wryly, and add that the desert, after all, keeps its own counsel, and that perhaps it is just as well.
