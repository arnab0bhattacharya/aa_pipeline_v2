The frost had not broken for three weeks. It lay upon the roofs of Marrowby like a hand of iron, and the smoke of the mill chimneys went up straight and thin into a sky the colour of slate, as if the very air were frozen and would not suffer it to spread. In the yards the snow was trodden into a grey mortar, and along the lane that led from the mill to the village the ruts were filled with ice so hard that the cart-wheels rang upon them like bells at a funeral.

It was in the second week of January, and the looms of Marrowby had been silent since Michaelmas. The war had taken the ships, and the ships had taken the trade, and the trade had taken the bread out of the mouths of six hundred weavers and their families. There was no work to be had, and there was no charity to be had either; for the parish was burdened beyond bearing, and the gentry, who might have helped, were gone to Harrogate and to Bath, and the manufacturers said that they themselves were ruined, and perhaps they believed it.

The manufacturer of Marrowby was Mr. Elias Thwaite. He was a man of fifty, stout and florid, with a hard eye and a harder mouth, and he lived in the great stone house at the top of the hill, behind the mill, where the wind came off the moor and rattled the windows in their frames. He had built the mill himself; he had raised it out of the earth, as it were, and he was proud of it, and he would not own that it could fail while he had breath in his body to drive it. He had a sister, Miss Ruth Thwaite, who kept his house and poured out his tea and listened to his talk of the war and the blockade and the wickedness of the French, and who said little, but who had eyes that saw more than her brother guessed.

On the morning of the fifteenth of January, a deputation came down from the village. There were five of them, weavers all, and they had walked up the hill in their thin shoes and their patched coats, and they stood in the snow in the yard before the mill door, and asked to see the master. Mr. Thwaite came out to them in his great-coat and his hat, and stood upon the step with his hands behind him.

“Well, lads,” said he, “what is it now?”

The eldest of them, one Joseph Hartley, a man with a grey face and a cough that shook him, took off his hat and held it in both hands.

“We’ve come about work, master,” said he. “We’ve come to ask if there’s any chance of the looms starting again.”

“There is no chance,” said Mr. Thwaite. “I told you so at Christmas, and I tell you so now. The war is the war, and the blockade is the blockade, and I can no more make trade out of nothing than you can make cloth out of air.”

“We’ve had no work since September, master,” said Hartley. “We’ve sold the clock and the bed, and the children are crying for bread. There’s them in the village that hasn’t eaten a hot meal since the frost set in.”

“And what would you have me do?” cried Mr. Thwaite, and his voice rose as if he were the injured party. “Would you have me open the mill and set the looms going at my own cost, and sell the cloth at a loss, and go bankrupt for the sake of feeding you? I have my own family to think of. I have my sister to think of. I am not a charity, I am a manufacturer.”

“We’re not asking for charity, master,” said Hartley, with a kind of dignity that sat strangely on his hunger. “We’re asking for work. We’ll work for less than the usual wage. We’ll work for bread and nothing more, if it please you.”

“And what good would that do me?” said Mr. Thwaite. “If I cannot sell the cloth, I cannot pay the wage, whether it be bread or money. You do not understand these things. You are weavers, not merchants. Go home and be thankful you have a roof over your heads, for there are those in this parish that have not even that.”

The men looked at one another, and the snow fell upon their shoulders, and the youngest of them, a boy of nineteen, with a face like a ghost, began to cough as if his chest would split. Mr. Thwaite turned upon his heel.

“I have said my say,” he said. “I will hear no more. Go home, and do not come here again, for it is no use.”

And he went in and shut the door, and the five men stood in the yard in the falling snow, and then they turned and went down the hill again, and the boy coughed all the way.

Now Miss Ruth Thwaite had been standing at the window of the parlour, which looked out upon the yard, and she had heard every word that passed, for the window was old and the glass was thin. She was a woman of forty, spare and quiet, with a face that had once been handsome and was now worn to a kind of patient sharpness, and she had a way of folding her hands together and looking at the fire when she was thinking, which her brother took for submission and which was in truth something very different. She had kept his house for twenty years, and in those twenty years she had learned that there are two ways of managing a man: the one is to argue with him, and the other is to let him think he has his way and then do as you please. She had tried the first when she was young, and it had brought her nothing but a headache and a quarrel; she had tried the second ever since, and it had brought her peace and a certain quiet power which she did not misuse.

When the deputation had gone down the hill, she went into the kitchen, where the cook was making the pastry for the master’s dinner, and she stood by the table and looked at the flour and the butter and the eggs, and she said:

“Martha, how much bread is there in the house?”

“There’s the two loaves for the master’s table, ma’am, and the flour in the bin, and the meal in the barrel.”

“And how much meat?”

“There’s the joint for Sunday, and the bacon in the chimney, and the cold fowl in the larder.”

Miss Thwaite was silent a moment. Then she said:

“Martha, I want you to bake me a dozen loaves this afternoon, and to boil the bacon, and to make a great pot of broth with the bones and the vegetables. And I want you to do it quietly, and not to speak of it to the master.”

Martha looked at her with round eyes.

“And who is it for, ma’am?”

“For the village,” said Miss Thwaite. “For the weavers and their children. There is no work to be had, and there is no charity to be had, and I will not sit in a warm room and eat my dinner while the children cry for bread. My brother is a hard man, and he will not help them, and I cannot make him; but I can help them myself, and I will, and he need not know.”

“And if he finds out, ma’am?”

“Then I will bear it,” said Miss Thwaite, and her voice was as quiet as ever, but there was a light in her eye that Martha had not seen there for many a year. “I have borne worse things than his anger, Martha, and I am not afraid of it. Now go and set the dough, for there is no time to lose.”

That night, when the mill-house was dark and the master had gone to bed with his pipe and his newspaper, Miss Thwaite and Martha and the little kitchen-maid went out into the lane with baskets on their arms, and they went from cottage to cottage in the village, and they left a loaf and a piece of bacon and a jug of broth at every door where there was a child crying or a man out of work. And the snow fell upon them, and the wind cut through their shawls, but they did not feel it, for they were warm with the warmth of the thing they did.

And in the morning, when Mr. Thwaite sat down to his breakfast and found no bacon and no bread but a dry crust and a cup of tea, he frowned and said:

“What is this, Ruth? Where is my breakfast?”

“There is no bacon, brother,” said Miss Thwaite, pouring out his tea with a steady hand. “There is no bread. The village was hungry, and I gave it to them.”

Mr. Thwaite stared at her as if she had struck him.

“You gave it?” he cried. “You gave my bacon and my bread to those idle weavers? You had no right—you had no business—I forbid it, do you hear? I forbid it!”

“You may forbid it,” said Miss Thwaite, and she set down the teapot and looked him in the face, and her eyes were not the eyes of a submissive woman. “You may forbid it, Elias, and I will not argue with you, for I know you too well. But I will tell you this: while there is bread in this house, no child in Marrowby shall starve if I can help it. You may turn me out of doors if you will, and I will go, and I will not ask you for a penny. But I will not sit at your table and eat while the weavers’ children cry for hunger in the lane. I have done it too long already.”

Mr. Thwaite opened his mouth and shut it again. He was not accustomed to be spoken to in this manner, and he did not know what to say. He looked at his sister, and she looked at him, and there was a silence in the room that was louder than any words. Then he pushed back his chair and rose and went to the window and stood with his back to her, looking out at the mill and the snow and the grey sky.

“You will ruin me,” he said at last, but his voice was not so loud as it had been.

“I will not ruin you,” said Miss Thwaite. “I will save you, if you will let me. There is a way, Elias, if you would only see it. The war will not last forever. The ships will come back, and the trade will come back, and the looms will start again. But if you let the weavers starve now, there will be no weavers left to work for you when the trade returns. You will have a mill and no hands, and then you will be ruined indeed.”

Mr. Thwaite said nothing. He stood at the window a long time, and the snow fell, and the wind rattled the frames, and his sister sat at the table with her hands folded, waiting. And at last he turned round, and his face was not so hard as it had been.

“You are a strange woman, Ruth,” he said. “I do not understand you.”

“No,” said Miss Thwaite, “you do not. But you will let me have my way?”

He grunted, which she took for consent, and she rose and went out to the kitchen to see about the baking of more bread. And that afternoon the looms of Marrowby were not started, and the war was not ended, and the blockade was not broken; but there was a loaf in every cottage in the village, and the boy who coughed had broth for his supper, and Miss Ruth Thwaite went about her house
