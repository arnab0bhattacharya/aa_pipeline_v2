The offer arrived in a letter of heavy, cream-colored paper, the seal broken with the careful, deliberate touch of a man who had long since ceased to be surprised by the resistance of the world, and was now merely accustomed to the friction. Arthur Penhaligon stood by the window of his modest lodgings in Kensington, holding the communication as if it were a specimen of some rare and potentially venomous insect, turning it over in his fingers with a mixture of horror and a cold, analytical curiosity. It was from Mr. Josiah Vane, the patron of the lucrative living of St. Jude’s-on-the-Hill, a church in Mayfair whose pews were upholstered in velvet the color of dried blood, and whose congregation had acquired a distinct taste for sermons that were as soothing as a warm bath and as devoid of moral friction as a well-polished mahogany table.

Vane’s letter was short, yet it possessed that terrible weight which comes from the unspoken assumption of ownership. It reminded Arthur that his father’s debts had been cleared by Vane’s grandfather, a debt of gratitude which, in the eyes of the old family, constituted a perpetual lease on the son’s soul. It suggested, with a delicacy that was itself a kind of cruelty, that Arthur’s recent publications on the moral necessity of social reform were “interesting speculations” but perhaps ill-suited to the “delicate sensibilities” of a metropolitan congregation that preferred their theology to be seasoned with comfort rather than salted with truth. The living, the letter concluded, was his by right of inheritance, provided he would “modify his tone.”

Arthur felt a physical chill, as if the air in the room had suddenly dropped several degrees. He was twenty-six, with the lean, intense look of a man who has spent more time thinking than eating, and his mind, though not yet fully formed into the rigid architecture of a dogmatist, had already begun to trace the intricate, often painful, lines of causality that bound the rich to the poor. He had been raised in a vicarage in the north, in a parish so destitute that the church roof leaked in winter, and the smell of damp wool and poverty was a constant companion. There, he had learned that the soul is not a separate entity to be polished for heaven, but a part of the whole human condition, inextricably bound to the material circumstances of the body. To preach to Vane’s congregation of silk and satin, to tell them that their wealth was a blessing from God without mentioning the hands that wove their clothes, would be a lie of such magnitude that it would shatter the very foundation of his faith.

Yet, the alternative was also laid before him, not in a letter, but in the memory of a visit he had made three weeks prior to the parish of Ashby-on-the-Wold, a small, bleak place in the north where the coal seams were thin and the winters long. The incumbent there, a broken man named Mr. Harker, had shown him the church, a crumbling stone structure that seemed to be sinking into the earth, and had spoken of the parishioners with a weary, defeated tenderness. They were not the wicked, Harker had said, but the neglected; they were people whose lives had been ground down by the indifference of a society that viewed their labor as a commodity rather than a human activity. If Arthur accepted this living, he would be trading the security of a London stipend and the prestige of a fashionable church for the daily grind of a poverty that was both spiritual and material. He would be responsible for souls that were already battered by the world, and he would have to find the strength to lift them without having the strength himself.

The choice was not, as Arthur had initially thought, a simple one between comfort and sacrifice. It was a complex negotiation between his own needs for security and his higher needs for integrity. He knew that if he accepted Vane’s offer, he would be safe, respected, and financially secure. He would be a man of the world, a clergyman who could discuss the price of stocks and the health of the horses at the same time as he discussed the price of souls and the health of the church. He would be comfortable, and comfort is a powerful sedative. But he also knew that he would be betraying the only truth he had found, the truth that justice is not a distant ideal but a present duty, and that to ignore the suffering of the poor is to participate in it.

He looked out the window. The London street was bustling with life, a river of carriages and pedestrians, all moving with a purpose that seemed to be directed by the invisible hand of profit. He could see the faces of the workers, pale and tired, and he thought of the hands that had built the city, the hands that had laid the bricks and hauled the stones, and he felt a surge of anger that was quickly tempered by a deep, sorrowful understanding. He realized that he could not serve two masters, and that the master of comfort was a master who demanded a price that he was not willing to pay.

He sat down at his desk and began to write. His hand moved slowly, carefully, as if he were carving words into stone. He did not write a letter of acceptance to Mr. Vane. Instead, he wrote to Mr. Harker, to the Bishop, and to his own conscience. He wrote that he had made his choice, and that he would go to Ashby-on-the-Wold. He wrote that he believed in the power of the gospel to transform not only the individual soul but also the social order, and that he was willing to test that belief in the most difficult and unglamorous of settings. He knew that he would be alone, that he would be poor, and that he would be misunderstood. But he also knew that he would be true, and that truth, though it is often a harsh master, is the only master that does not enslave.

As he sealed the letter, he felt a sense of relief that was mixed with a profound sadness. He was leaving the world of security and comfort, and entering the world of struggle and uncertainty. He was leaving the city, with its noise and its light, and going to the north, with its silence and its dark. But he was also leaving behind the part of himself that was afraid, and embracing the part of himself that was brave. He knew that the road ahead would be long and hard, and that he would often be tempted to turn back. But he also knew that he had made the right choice, and that rightness, even in the face of suffering, is a kind of joy that no amount of comfort can buy.

He walked out into the street, the letter in his pocket, and felt the cold air on his face. The city was still moving, but he felt that he was moving in a different direction, away from the current, and towards something that was deeper and more real. He did not look back. He knew that the past was behind him, and that the future was ahead, and that the only thing that mattered was the present, and the present was a choice that he had made, and a choice that he was ready to keep.
