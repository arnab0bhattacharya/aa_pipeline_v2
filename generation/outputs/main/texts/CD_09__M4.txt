In the ancient and respectable market-town of Crumbleford, in the county of Kent, where the cathedral spire, the corn exchange, and the gasworks vie for pre-eminence in the affections of the inhabitants, there dwelt, in the year eighteen hundred and thirty-six, a young man by the name of Obadiah Pettingell. He was apprenticed—bound, indentured, and otherwise legally fettered—to Mr. Abel Gudgeon, coffin-maker and undertaker of this town, a man of grave aspect and graver business, whose whole life was a long and solemn rehearsal for the funeral that should one day be his own.

Now, the shop of Mr. Gudgeon stood in a narrow lane behind the market, a lane so perpetually shaded that the sun seemed to enter it only on sufferance, and so pervaded by the odour of varnish and new-sawn elm that the very cats of the neighbourhood went about with an air of melancholy resignation. In this shop Obadiah worked from six in the morning until eight at night, measuring, planing, joining, and polishing; and as he worked, he dreamed. For the soul of Obadiah Pettingell was not the soul of a coffin-maker, but the soul of a tragedian; and while his hands were busy with the mortal husk, his spirit was strutting upon the boards of Drury Lane, bowing to thunders of applause, and dying nightly in the fifth act to the tears of a thousand spectators.

It was his habit, when the master's back was turned, to rehearse before the looking-glass that hung behind the shop door—a looking-glass in which many a departed Crumbleford worthy had been accustomed to survey himself for the last time before the lid was screwed down—and there to declaim, with gestures of a truly alarming nature, such passages of Shakespeare as he had by heart. “To be, or not to be,” he would murmur, with his hand upon a half-finished coffin-lid; or “Is this a dagger which I see before me?” with a plane in his grasp and his eye fixed upon the ghost of nothing at all. And at such moments his countenance would assume an expression of such terrific grandeur that the apprentice-boy who swept the shavings would drop his broom and flee into the street.

It chanced, in the autumn of that year, that a company of travelling players came to Crumbleford, and set up their booth in the yard of the Bull and Bell, and announced, in a playbill of a most gorgeous and inflammatory description, that they would present, for one night only, the tragedy of “The Miser’s Doom; or, The Avenging Shadow,” together with a comic song by Mr. Frederick Fizzgig, and a hornpipe by Miss Araminta Dobbs. Obadiah, who had never in his life beheld a play, went to the yard of the Bull and Bell as a pilgrim goes to Mecca; and when the curtain rose, and the Miser appeared in a green coat and a black wig, and began to rave and stamp and clutch his bosom, Obadiah felt that he had found his home.

He sought out the manager next morning—a portly, red-faced, perspiring gentleman named Mr. Horatio Bumbleby, who wore a white hat, a blue coat, and a perpetual air of having just escaped from a fire—and he offered himself, with many blushes and stammerings, as a candidate for the stage. Mr. Bumbleby looked him up and down, heard him recite a speech from “Macbeth,” and pronounced, with the decision of a man who had seen a thousand such aspirants and had been one himself, that the young man had “the making of a very pretty villain.” He offered him, accordingly, the part of the Second Assassin in “The Miser’s Doom,” with a salary of five shillings a week and the privilege of sleeping in the property-room; and Obadiah, in a transport of joy, accepted on the spot.

But when he returned to the shop and announced his intention to Mr. Gudgeon, that gentleman’s face assumed an expression which, had it been carved in oak, would have been a warning to all apprentices for ever. “You will do what?” said he, laying down his rule with a clatter that echoed through the lane. “You will go upon the stage? You will be a play-actor? You will make a fool of yourself in a painted booth, with a tin sword and a paper crown, while the honest trade that has fed you and clothed you and taught you to handle a plane—the trade of your father before you, and his father before him—is left to go to the dogs?”

“But, sir,” pleaded Obadiah, “I have no genius for coffins.”

“Genius!” cried Mr. Gudgeon, with a bitter laugh. “Genius! And what, pray, has genius to do with it? Do you think I have a genius for coffins? Do you think my father had a genius for coffins? Do you think the world is made of geniuses? No, sir; the world is made of men who do their duty, and pay their debts, and measure a corpse correctly, and don’t go capering about in a barn with a feather in their hats. You will stay here, and you will finish that oak coffin for Mrs. Pemberton’s husband, and you will speak no more of this tomfoolery.”

And then his mother wept. Mrs. Pettingell, a small, pale, anxious woman, who had never in her life contradicted anybody, came to the shop with her handkerchief in her hand and her eyes red with crying, and besought her son, in the name of all the saints, to give up his wicked design. “O Obadiah,” said she, “do you not see that it will break my heart? To think of my boy—my only boy—standing up before a lot of people in a painted dress, and saying things that were never in the Bible! What would your poor father have said? He was a respectable man, Obadiah, and he never went to a play in his life, except once, and he was ill for a week after. O, my dear, dear boy, do not do it!”

And then, worse than all, came Miss Sophronia Larkins, the daughter of the baker in the High Street, with her curls and her dimples and her little white apron, and she laughed at him. “La, Obadiah,” said she, “you a play-actor! Why, you would be ridiculous! You would be the laughing-stock of the whole town. Fancy you, with your long face and your solemn eyes, strutting about and making speeches! You had much better stay with Mr. Gudgeon and make coffins, for you look like a coffin-maker, and you will never look like anything else.”

Now, whether it was that the tears of his mother, or the scorn of Sophronia, or the stern decree of Mr. Gudgeon, had most power upon the mind of Obadiah, I cannot undertake to say; but certain it is that he went to bed that night in a state of the most miserable indecision, and that he rose in the morning with his mind made up. He would go to Mr. Bumbleby, he resolved, and tell him that he could not accept the part; that he was born to the smell of varnish and the feel of shavings; that he was a coffin-maker, and a coffin-maker he must remain. And having so resolved, he put on his hat and walked slowly down the lane, and there, at the corner, whom should he meet but Mr. Bumbleby himself, who seized him by the button-hole and cried, “My dear fellow, I have been looking for you everywhere! The Second Assassin has run off with the Miser’s daughter, and we have no one to play the part to-night. You must be ready at six. Here is the book. ‘Enter Second Assassin, with a dark lantern.’ You have only to look grim and say ‘Die, villain!’ and it is all over.”

And Obadiah Pettingell, standing at the corner of the lane, with the book in his hand and the smell of the bakery in his nostrils and the sound of Mr. Gudgeon’s plane in his ears, felt his resolution ooze out of him at every pore. He looked at the book; he looked at the lane; he looked at Mr. Bumbleby’s white hat; and then he thought of Sophronia’s laugh, and his mother’s tears, and the oak coffin for Mrs. Pemberton’s husband, and he made a desperate plunge. “I will do it,” said he; “I will be the Second Assassin.”

And so, at six o’clock that evening, while Mr. Gudgeon sat in his shop in the gathering dusk, wondering what had become of his apprentice, and Mrs. Pettingell wept over her tea, and Miss Sophronia Larkins walked in the High Street with a new ribbon in her bonnet, Obadiah Pettingell, with a dark lantern in his hand and his heart in his mouth, stood behind a painted curtain in the yard of the Bull and Bell, waiting for his cue. And when it came, and he strode upon the boards, and the audience—which consisted of a dozen sailors, three old women, and a dog—set up a feeble cheer, he felt for one brief moment that he was indeed a tragedian; and then he opened his mouth, and the words “Die, villain!” came out in a squeak so small and so pitiful that the dog barked, the sailors roared, and the Second Assassin, dropping his dark lantern, fled from the stage in confusion, and was seen no more in Crumbleford for many a long day.
