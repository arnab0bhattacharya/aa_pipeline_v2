In the city of Velitria, which is built upon a hundred bridges of white and rose-coloured stone and is therefore perpetually suspended between sky and water, there lived a lace-maker named Elara. She was poor, as those who make beautiful things for the rich often are, and she sold her work at the Palace of the Serene Republic, where the nobles passed by in a scent of orange-blossom and scandal. Her lace was a marvel of patience; it possessed that lightness which is the most difficult of all virtues to achieve, and which, like innocence, is only appreciated after it is lost. To see her white fingers moving over the pillow was to believe that the air itself was being embroidered.

One afternoon, as the sun was setting in a wash of apricot and gold, the Duchess of Valois—a woman whose reputation for cruelty was exceeded only by her reputation for elegance—stopped her chair at Elara’s humble stall. She was a creature of exquisite and terrible beauty, with hair the colour of dead gold and eyes that held the cold, clear indifference of a winter sky. She looked at the lace, and then at the lace-maker, with that detached curiosity one might feel for a peculiar insect.

“This is very pretty,” said the Duchess, her voice sounding like the tinkling of small, expensive bells. “But it is only fit for the ankles of a waiting-woman. I require something that is not merely a fabric, but a philosophy.”

Elara bowed her head, her heart fluttering like a trapped bird. “I have no philosophy, Your Grace. I have only thread and time.”

“Time,” mused the Duchess, picking up a scrap of net and letting it fall. “How vulgar. I want a veil. A veil that is so fine it cannot be seen, yet so dark it hides the face. A veil of shadow spun into silk. If you can make me a veil finer than any ever made in Velitria, I will give you a purse of gold that will keep you from hunger for a year. If you fail, I shall have your hands cut off, for they will have been proven useless.”

The crowd gasped, but the Duchess smiled, a smile that did not reach her eyes. It was a delightful proposition; it had the structure of a good tragedy.

“I shall make it,” said Elara, though her voice trembled.

For a year, Elara worked. The city of Velitria changed its seasons around her, the canals freezing and thawing, the festivals coming and going with their noise and fire, but in her small room, there was only the silence of the work. She used a thread so thin that it was visible only by the light of the moon, and she worked by the moon because the sun was too harsh for such delicate labour. She wove into the pattern all the things she saw and all the things she felt: the weeping of the bridges, the coldness of the water, the distant laughter of the courtiers.

As the months passed, the beauty of the lace grew monstrous. It was too perfect. It seemed to drink the light from the room. And as the veil grew, Elara’s eyesight grew dim. She was trading her vision for the Duchess’s vanity, pouring the liquid of her own sight into the mesh of the silk. By the time the year was up, the veil was finished, but Elara was blind. She could see nothing but a faint, grey mist, and she knew the world only by the touch of the thread.

She went to the palace, led by a small boy. The Duchess received her in a salon of turquoise velvet and gilded mirrors. Elara held up the veil. It was a phantom; it was a breath held too long. When she laid it over her hand, the hand disappeared.

“It is perfect,” said the Duchess, taking it. She held it up to the light. “It is the colour of a secret. It is lighter than a lie and heavier than a truth. You have done well, little spider.”

“And the gold?” asked Elara, her empty eyes fixed on the sound of the voice.

“Oh, yes,” said the Duchess carelessly. She tossed a heavy purse onto the floor. It landed with a dull, heavy thud, like a heart stopping. “There. Take it. You have earned your bread.”

But the Duchess was already looking away. She put the veil upon her face. It was exquisite. It made her look like a statue of grief, or a queen of the underworld. She turned to her waiting-woman, a girl with a sallow complexion and a bad temper.

“Here,” said the Duchess, taking the veil off almost as soon as she had put it on. “I have seen it now. The novelty is gone. It bores me. Keep it. It may improve your complexion, which is, I fear, past saving.”

She threw the veil at the girl. It fluttered down like a dying moth.

Elara stood still. She could not see the gesture, but she could feel the change in the air, the sudden drop in temperature that accompanies a profound act of indifference. She heard the rustle of the fabric as the waiting-woman crumpled it up.

“That is all?” whispered Elara.

“That is all,” said the Duchess, turning to a mirror to examine a diamond in her hair. “The market is glutted with wonders. One must move on. You may go. And do close the door; the draft is ruinous to one’s nerves.”

Elara picked up the purse. It was heavy, but it felt light in her hand. She walked out of the palace, led by the boy, and they crossed the bridges of Velitria. The sun was setting again, painting the water in colours Elara could no longer see. She had sold her sight for a year of life, and the world had accepted the bargain and found the goods wanting.

As she walked, she began to laugh. It was a soft, strange sound, like the snapping of dry threads. The Duchess had wanted a veil to hide her face, but she had given away the only thing that could hide the truth: that beauty is a fleeting accident, while ugliness is a permanent work of art. Elara was blind, yes, but she had seen the vanity of the world woven into a perfect mesh. The Duchess, with her perfect eyes, saw nothing at all but herself. And in the city of bridges, where everything is suspended, it is better to be blind and heavy with gold than to be a Duchess with nothing to look at but a mirror.
