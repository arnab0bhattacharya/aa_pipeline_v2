The rain had not ceased for three days, and Westminster had taken on the aspect of a sodden, grey sponge. Arthur Penhaligon felt the dampness in his very bones as he hurried across the flagstones of the Outer Court, his umbrella a flimsy shield against the persistent drizzle. He was a man made for such weather, in his sensible wool coat and meticulously knotted tie, a creature of schedules and stationery, of ink and order. His world was the quiet, paper-lined office of the Right Honourable Sir Alistair Finch, Minister for the Colonies, a world of meticulously balanced ledgers and carefully worded dispatches to the far-flung corners of the Empire.

He was so lost in thought, mentally drafting a reply to a particularly tedious query from the Governor of Malta, that he almost collided with the figure leaning against the damp stone of a gothic archway.

“Arthur? Arthur Penhaligon, by all that’s holy.”

The voice was a gravelly echo from a past Arthur had spent years packing away. He looked up, blinking rain from his spectacles. The man was thin, almost gaunt, his face sharp and pale under a shock of unruly dark hair. He wore a threadbare coat and boots that had seen far better days, but his eyes were the same. They burned with the same incandescent fire that had once lit up debating societies at Oxford.

“Leo? Leo Vance?” Arthur’s voice was a whisper of disbelief. They had been inseparable once, two boys from provincial towns who had found in each other a shared hunger for knowledge. But their paths had diverged violently after university. Arthur, the pragmatist, had sought the steady, respectable ladder of the Civil Service. Leo, the idealist, had chased the wild, chaotic promise of social revolution, disappearing into the smoky coffee houses and clandestine meetings of the radical fringe.

“The very same,” Leo said, a wry smile touching his lips. He pushed himself off the wall, his movements wiry and restless. “Though I suspect I am not quite the man you remember. And you… you are more so. The very picture of officialdom.”

A flush crept up Arthur’s neck. “It is a living, Leo. A service to the Crown.”

“To the Crown,” Leo repeated, the words dripping with a familiar, bitter irony. “Of course. Which brings me to my purpose, Arthur. I need you.”

Arthur felt a prickle of alarm. Leo Vance was never one for pleasantries. “What is it? If it is money…”

“No, not money. Influence. Access.” Leo’s burning eyes fixed on him. “I need a meeting. With your minister. Sir Alistair Finch.”

Arthur almost laughed. The request was so preposterous, so utterly detached from the rigid hierarchy of Whitehall, that it bordered on the insane. “Leo, you cannot be serious. Sir Alistair does not receive… he does not grant audience to…”

“To anarchists? To troublemakers? To old friends who have lost their way?” Leo’s voice was low, but it carried an edge of steel. “I represent a new movement, Arthur. A voice of the people that is tired of being ignored. We are not bomb-throwers, whatever the rags would have you believe. We are thinkers. We have a proposition for the Minister, a chance at reconciliation, to bridge the chasm between the government and the governed before it becomes a bloody trench. I believe you, of all people, can understand that.”

Reconciliation. The word struck a chord in Arthur’s conscientious soul. He looked at his old friend, at the desperate intensity in his face, and saw not a threat, but a tragic, misplaced passion. He saw the brilliant boy he had once admired, and he felt a surge of paternalistic pity. He could be the bridge. He could bring reason to this wildness, mediate between the fire of Leo’s rhetoric and the cool logic of Sir Alistair’s policy. It would be a coup, a private act of statesmanship that no one would ever know of, but which would bring a measure of order to a chaotic world.

“I… I can try,” Arthur heard himself say. “I cannot promise anything. Sir Alistair is a very busy man. But I will speak to him. I will present your case.”

Leo’s face broke into a smile, a flash of the old charm that had once made him the most persuasive man in any room. “I knew I could count on you, Arthur. You always were the sensible one. The one who could make things happen.”

The next two days were a torment of indecision for Arthur. He felt like a man who had invited a stray wolf into a lambing pen, convinced he could teach it to eat grass. He broached the subject to Sir Alistair during a lull in the morning’s correspondence. The Minister, a portly man with magnificent mutton-chop whiskers and a mind as sharp as a surgeon’s scalpel, had listened with a mixture of amusement and incredulity.

“An anarchist, Penhaligon? An old school chum of yours? Good heavens. Does he wish to lecture me on the inherent tyranny of the telegraph system?”

“He speaks of reconciliation, Sir Alistair. A dialogue. He claims to represent a significant body of opinion.”

Sir Alistair had steepled his fingers, his eyes twinkling. “Very well, then. Let us have our dialogue. Schedule him for Friday afternoon, after the Cabinet meeting. Let us see what this ‘voice of the people’ has to say for himself. It might be more entertaining than the report from the Postal Service on new stamp gum.”

And so it was arranged. Arthur felt a profound sense of relief, of vindication. He was doing the right thing. He was bringing light to a dark corner, reason to a fevered mind.

Friday arrived, cloaked in the usual London grey. The Cabinet meeting had run over, leaving Sir Alistair in a weary but expansive mood. At precisely four o’clock, Arthur ushered Leo Vance into the grand, book-lined office. The contrast was jarring. Leo, in his worn tweeds, seemed to absorb the light in the room, a smudge of defiance against the polished mahogany and leather-bound volumes.

Sir Alistair rose from behind his vast desk, a benevolent smile on his face. “Mr. Vance. A pleasure. Penhaligon tells me you are a champion of the common man. Please, take a seat.”

Leo remained standing. “I prefer to stand, Sir Alistair. It keeps the blood moving. And I am not a champion. I am merely a mouthpiece.”

“Very well,” the Minister said, his smile tightening slightly. “Then let us hear what this mouthpiece has to say. You have requested this meeting on the grounds of ‘reconciliation’. A noble sentiment. Pray, elaborate.”

“This is not a negotiation, Sir Alistair,” Leo said, his voice suddenly devoid of its earlier warmth. It was cold, hard, and clear. “It is an announcement.”

Arthur felt a cold dread snake up his spine. This was not the script. This was not the reasoned dialogue he had imagined. He saw the change in Leo’s posture, the tension in his shoulders. This was not the pleading friend; this was something else entirely.

“An announcement?” Sir Alistair’s eyebrows rose. “How very dramatic. Go on.”

“For too long,” Leo began, his voice ringing with the cadence of a street-corner orator, “you and your kind have ruled from these gilded halls, speaking of progress and Empire while the people of this city starve in its slums. You speak of order, but it is the order of the boot on the neck. You speak of the Crown, but it is a crown built on the suffering of millions.”

“Mr. Vance, this is highly irregular,” Sir Alistair began, his affability evaporating.

Leo ignored him, his gaze sweeping the room, taking in the symbols of power. “We have offered you dialogue. We have offered you a chance to step back from the precipice. You have treated it as a diversion, a curiosity. So now, we offer you something else. We offer you a demonstration.”

He moved with a speed that took Arthur completely by surprise. He was not reaching for a weapon. He was reaching into his coat. But instead of a pistol or a knife, he pulled out a small, heavy-looking object wrapped in cloth. He unwrapped it on the edge of Sir Alistair’s desk. It was a half-brick, its edges sharp and jagged.

“You see this?” Leo said, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. “This is the voice of the people. It is crude. It is simple. But it is honest.”

Before Arthur could cry out, before Sir Alistair could even rise from his chair, Leo swung the brick. Not at the Minister, but at the most prominent object on the desk: a magnificent, ormolu-mounted marble clock, a gift from the Czar. The impact was sickening. The clock exploded in a shower of glass and cogs, its delicate hands frozen at four minutes past four. The sound of the smash echoed in the stunned silence of the room.

Then, Leo turned. He looked not at the spluttering Minister, but directly at Arthur. His eyes, which had once burned with the fire of shared ideals, were now cold as a winter sea. There was no passion in them, no madness. Only a terrible, calculated clarity.

“Reconciliation is impossible, Arthur,” he said, his voice flat. “You build your world on paper and promises. We will build ours on the rubble of yours.”

He did not run. He simply turned and walked from the room, leaving the door open. For a long moment, neither Arthur nor Sir Alistair moved. The only sound was the frantic ticking of the Minister’s pocket watch. Arthur stared at the wreckage on the desk, at the shattered face of the clock. He had not brought a lamb into the pen. He had not even brought a wolf. He had brought a symbol, a piece of the street, and it had just smashed the illusion of their inviolable world to pieces. He looked at his own hands, trembling slightly, and understood with a sickening finality that he had not been a bridge at all. He had been a tool. And the world he had tried to mend had just been broken beyond repair.
