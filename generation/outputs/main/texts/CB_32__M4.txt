The shop had stood empty for a twelvemonth before I took it: a narrow, dark-fronted place in Wethergate, where the houses lean together as if for warmth, and the sun, when it condescends to visit us at all, comes slantwise and grudging, as though it feared to linger. I had my savings from six years of governessing—a hoard counted and recounted on winter nights in other people’s schoolrooms—and I spent them to the last sovereign on whitewash for the walls, a map of the hemispheres, a clock that ticked with a loud, honest clatter, and twelve deal desks, each with its ink-well, each sanded and scrubbed by my own hands until the grain stood up like silk. I hung no sign; I had no need of one. The neighbours knew me. I was Miss Ellin Ashworth, who had taught the Wethergate children their letters in their own kitchens at ninepence the week, and they came to me now at a shilling, and were glad of it.

Those first months I was happier than I had ever thought to be. The schoolroom smelled of chalk and coal and damp wool drying; the infants stumbled through their tables; the elder girls stitched samplers and copied out the Psalms in hands that wavered like reeds in a current. I rose at five to make the fire, and sat at my high desk till dusk, and when the last child was gone I swept the floor and set the slates in order and was not lonely. I told myself I was not lonely. A woman who has her own door-key and her own bread-and-cheese and her own conscience has no right to use that word.

It was in the third month, when the frost had set in hard and the pump in the yard stood frozen to its iron heart, that the blow fell. I had noticed the workmen, of course—had heard the hammering across the street at the old King’s Head, which had been shut these five years and was now all scaffolded like a broken limb. But I had supposed it a brewery, or a wool-factor’s store. I was in the midst of a lesson on the reign of Elizabeth, and the girls were warm for once, and had begun to argue the matter of the Armada with a heat that did my soul good, when Betsy Thwaite, who had the window seat, cried out that there was a great crowd in the street, and a gentleman in a shovel hat upon the steps of the King’s Head, and a paper being read aloud.

I went to the window. The gentleman was the Reverend Mr. Hesselgrave, whom I knew by sight—a large, pale, handsome man with a voice like a bell and a manner that suggested the Almighty had consulted him personally on the ordering of the parish. The paper he held was a prospectus. The King’s Head was to be a school—the Wethergate Grammar and Commercial Academy for Young Ladies—with a staff of four mistresses, a professor of music and dancing from Leeds, terms most moderate, and the especial patronage of the rector and the principal families of the town. As he read, I saw the mothers of my scholars listening, and nodding, and glancing across the street at my dark little shop-front with a look I knew too well—the look of persons who are glad to have an excuse for a change, and ashamed of their gladness.

I did not cry. I went back to my desk and heard the girls through the reign of Elizabeth to the death of the Queen, and dismissed them at four as usual. Then I sat down in the empty room with the clock clattering at me and the frost creeping on the panes, and I confess to my Maker that for one hour I was as near despair as a proud woman may come without breaking. Four mistresses. A dancing-master. The patronage of the rector. And I—what had I? A map, a clock, twelve desks, and a tongue that had never learned to flatter.

The next day I did a thing that cost me more than the savings had cost. I put on my best bonnet—the black straw with the crape rose—and went to the rectory.

Mr. Hesselgrave received me in his study, among his books and his stuffed birds, and heard me out with his large white hands folded on his waistcoat. I asked him plainly whether the town could support two schools; whether it were not possible that his academy might take the daughters of the gentry, and mine the daughters of the shopkeepers and the small farmers, and so both live. I did not beg. I was careful not to beg. But I heard my own voice grow small in that great room, and I hated it.

He smiled. It was a beautiful smile, and it never reached his eyes. “Miss Ashworth,” said he, “I honour your industry. But you must perceive that an establishment without the countenance of the Church, without a responsible committee, without—if I may say it—a name behind it, can offer no guarantee to parents. The town requires a school of standing. It is not a question of competition. It is a question of fitness.”

“And am I unfit, sir?”

“You are a young woman of no fortune and no connections, conducting a school upon premises licensed for the sale of spirits.” He said it gently, as though he were sorry for me, and that gentleness was the cruellest thing he did. “I would advise you, in all kindness, to seek a situation as a governess in a good family. I could perhaps speak for you.”

I rose. My knees were not steady, but my voice, God be thanked, was. “I have been a governess in good families, Mr. Hesselgrave,” I said. “I have eaten the bread of dependence, and it is bitter, and I have no mind to eat it again. You have the countenance of the Church and the patronage of the gentry. I have twelve desks and a clock that goes. We shall see which of us is standing at Michaelmas.”

He bowed me out with the same beautiful smile. I walked home through the sleet with my bonnet ruined and my heart on fire, and I did not know, that night, whether I had spoken like a fool or a Christian. I know now that I spoke like neither; I spoke like a woman who has been told, all her life, to be grateful, and has discovered that gratitude is a coin that will not pass at the bank.

The winter was hard. Three scholars left me in the first week of the new academy’s opening, and two more after Christmas, and Mrs. Thwaite sent word that Betsy must go where she could learn French and the use of the globes. I lost the Thwaites. I lost the minister’s daughters. I kept the butchers’ and the joiners’ and the small farmers’ children, who could not afford French, and the little half-timers from the mill, who came at six in the evening with their fingers blue and their minds still full of the noise of the looms. For them I lit the second lamp, and taught them their sums and the Ten Commandments and the uses of a noun, and I found that in teaching the poor I had a weapon the rector could not match. He had the countenance of the Church; I had the children of the town in my hand every evening, and their mothers in my debt, and their fathers, who are slow to move but who remember a kindness.

I did not compete. I did not beg. I did not close. I did what I ought to have done from the first, and what I would advise every woman who sets up a school in a Yorkshire town to do before she buys so much as a slate: I made myself necessary. I taught the mill-children free. I lent books to the joiners’ apprentices. I sat up at night with the butcher’s wife when her child had the croup, and I did not mention it in the morning. And when the spring came, and the great academy across the street had its French and its globes and its dancing-master, and its young ladies walked out in their white muslin two by two, I stood at my door and watched them go by, and I did not envy them. I had my own door-key. I had my own conscience. And I had begun to learn the one lesson that no dancing-master can teach—that a woman’s fitness is not a thing to be certified by a rector, but to be proved, day by day, in the fire.
