Mr. Arthur Helstone, of Thornfield Park in Derbyshire, was a young man of excellent understanding and considerable address, who found himself, at the age of six-and-twenty, in the unwelcome position of being thoroughly encumbered. His late father had been a man of more taste than economy, and the estate, though imposing in its aspect and respectable in its acreage, was settled so heavily with debts as to render the present owner little more than a tenant at will upon his own property. Mr. Helstone was therefore under the absolute necessity of marrying a fortune, or of selling the land which he could not afford to keep. He was, upon the whole, more inclined to marry than to sell, being naturally attached to his home, and having that preference for the comforts of rank over the exertions of industry which is frequently found in young men of good family and narrow means.

The fortune he sought was, at that moment, residing within five miles of him. Miss Margaret Bexley, the only daughter of a manufacturer from Leeds whose ironworks had yielded a very splendid income, was staying with her aunt at the neighboring village of Haycroft. Miss Bexley was possessed of twenty thousand pounds, a sum which, if applied to the discharge of the most pressing encumbrances upon Thornfield, would enable Mr. Helstone to retain his estate and to consider himself, in the moderate degree which prudence allowed, an independent gentleman. She was not, in the common acceptation of the word, handsome; but she had a look of sense, a degree of composure in her address, and a readiness of observation which Mr. Helstone found, upon acquaintance, to be rather more formidable than agreeable.

He had paid his respects at Haycroft with regularity for nearly a month, and had advanced in his attentions to that point which is generally understood, by those who watch such matters, to imply a serious design. He walked with her in the shrubbery, he rode with her on the common, and he conversed with her on subjects of literature and improvement with a degree of elegance which he flattered himself was not without its effect. Miss Bexley received his attentions with civility, but without any of that flutter of gratified vanity which such attentions are wont to produce. She listened to his remarks on the picturesque with apparent attention, and replied to them with a precision which occasionally made him feel that his taste had been consulted rather than his judgment. He was not, however, a man to be discouraged by a want of enthusiasm where there was no positive indifference, and he continued his pursuit with the steady resolution of a man who knows that his object is necessary to his welfare.

It was on a fine morning in the latter part of their acquaintance that they found themselves walking together in the gardens of Haycroft, the aunt having withdrawn to attend to some matter of household economy, and the younger sister of Miss Bexley having been dispatched in the carriage to call upon a friend. They were, for the first time, wholly without a third party, and Mr. Helstone, who had been meditating an explicit declaration, perceived that the opportunity was one which he could not, with any regard to his own interest, allow to pass.

He began, as men in his situation are apt to begin, with a general observation upon the pleasantness of the scene, the fineness of the weather, and the satisfaction he derived from the society of Haycroft. Miss Bexley heard him with patience, and when he had concluded his preamble, which was rather longer than his own taste would have approved in another, she turned to him with a look of calm intelligence and said, "You are very obliging, Mr. Helstone. But I believe I can spare you the trouble of further circumlocution. You wish to address me, and you wish me to accept you. Am I correct?"

Mr. Helstone, who had not anticipated so direct an approach, felt himself considerably discomposed. He had prepared a speech of some elegance, in which the warmth of his regard was to be tempered by the moderation of his expectations, and in which the subject of money was to be introduced only through the most delicate of implications. He found himself now, however, in the position of a general whose plan of attack has been anticipated by the enemy, and he could only reply, with an air of less composure than he usually wore, that she was, in the main, correct.

"I am glad of it," said Miss Bexley, "for I should be sorry to waste either your time or my own in a discussion which might be conducted with more frankness. You are, I understand, under the necessity of marrying a fortune. Thornfield is encumbered, and you cannot retain it without an addition to your income which your own resources do not provide. I am, as you are no doubt aware, in possession of twenty thousand pounds. You have paid me attentions which, in a man of your station, can have but one meaning, and I have received them with a degree of willingness which, in a woman of my circumstances, can have but one construction. We understand each other, I think, very well."

Mr. Helstone, who had by this time recovered some portion of his self-command, replied that he was happy to find that she had entered so fully into the nature of his situation, and that he could assure her, whatever might be the motives which had first directed his attention to her, his regard was now founded upon a sincere esteem for her understanding and character.

"I do not doubt it," said Miss Bexley, "but I must observe, Mr. Helstone, that esteem, however sincere, is not the only consideration which will determine my answer. I am not ignorant of the world, nor of the estimation in which the daughter of a manufacturer is held by the county families of Derbyshire. I know that my fortune will be acceptable, and that my connections will not. If I marry you, I shall be received, I suppose, with civility, as the wife of Mr. Helstone of Thornfield, but I shall not be admitted, on equal terms, into that society which regards trade as a species of inferiority. I am well content with this. I do not seek the acquaintance of those who would disdain me, and I am not ambitious of a station which I could only maintain by an appearance of what I am not. But I must have, in return for the sacrifice I make, certain terms."

Mr. Helstone, who had by now entirely abandoned his prepared address, begged to know what those terms might be.

"They are these," said Miss Bexley, stopping near a bench at the turn of the path and looking at him with an expression of perfect seriousness. "First, that the settlement upon me shall be of a nature which secures to me, in the event of your death, an income wholly at my own disposal, and not subject to the claims of your creditors. Second, that my father shall be received at Thornfield with the same attention and respect which would be shown to any other visitor, and that no distinction, either in the order of his reception or in the manner of his treatment, shall be made on account of his profession. Third, that I shall have the entire management of my own household expenses, and that no application shall be made to my private fortune for any purpose of estate improvement without my express consent. And fourth, that you shall not, at any future period, represent to me that I am under an obligation to you for the elevation which my marriage has conferred upon my rank. I know very well what I am, and what I am not. I am the daughter of a manufacturer, and I am not ashamed of it. I shall be the wife of a gentleman, and I shall expect to be treated as such. But I shall not consent to be made to feel, by any implied comparison or indirect allusion, that I have purchased my position with my money, and am therefore bound to be grateful for it."

She concluded, and Mr. Helstone, who had listened with an attention which was mingled with no small degree of astonishment, found himself under the necessity of replying to a proposal of a nature which he had certainly never anticipated. He had expected, if he had expected anything, a modest diffidence, a gentle hesitation, perhaps a modest appeal to his generosity. He had found instead a clear-headed comprehension of the whole affair, a firmness of resolution which left no room for misunderstanding, and a set of terms which, however reasonable they might be in themselves, were delivered with a directness which he was not altogether pleased to ascribe to the habits of a manufacturing family.

He was, however, a man of sense, and he could perceive, upon reflection, that the terms were neither unjust nor exorbitant. They were, indeed, such as a woman of Miss Bexley's discernment might very naturally propose, and such as a man of Mr. Helstone's circumstances could not, with any appearance of reason, refuse. He told her, therefore, after a pause of sufficient length to indicate that he had considered the matter with attention, that he accepted her conditions, and that he was happy to find in her a person so well qualified to assist him in the management of his affairs, and so entirely free from those expectations of romantic sensibility which could only have been productive of disappointment on both sides.

Miss Bexley received this reply with a nod of satisfaction, and observed that she was glad they had settled the matter so expeditiously, as her father was expected at Haycroft on the following day, and she wished to have the arrangement concluded before his arrival, in order that she might present it to him as a fact accomplished, rather than as a subject for discussion.

"I have no doubt," she added, "that my father will be entirely satisfied. He has long wished to see me settled in a respectable station, and he is too sensible a man to object to the terms which I have proposed. He will, I dare say, be rather pleased than otherwise to find that I have conducted the negotiation with so much discretion."

Mr. Helstone could not forbear smiling at the word negotiation, which was certainly a term more frequently applied to the transaction of business than to the conclusion of a marriage, though in the present instance it was perhaps not altogether inapplicable. He ventured to express a hope that she did not regard him merely as a party to a bargain.

"I regard you," said Miss Bexley, "as a gentleman of good character, respectable connexions, and encumbered estate, who has offered me his hand upon terms which I have found it in my interest to accept. I do not regard you as a hero of romance, nor do I expect you to regard me as a heroine. We shall, I have no doubt, live together with as much comfort and as little dissatisfaction as most married couples of our acquaintance, and that, I believe, is all that either of us has any right to expect."

Mr. Helstone made no reply to this observation, for he was not certain that any reply he could make would be altogether agreeable to his own feelings or consistent with the candor which Miss Bexley had displayed. He contented himself with offering his arm, and they returned to the house in a silence which was, on his part, meditative, and on hers, so far as he could judge, perfectly tranquil.
