The heat at the port of San Cristóbal del Mar clung to the skin like a wet cloth, even after the sun had dipped below the horizon. Gas lamps along the wooden quay threw weak, flickering halos onto the dark water, illuminating the barnacled hulls of schooners and steamers swaying gently on the tide. Joaquín Vargas walked his rounds with the measured step of a man who had long ago memorized every splintered plank and rusted cleat on the docks. Twenty-three years he had worn the khaki uniform of the customs service, and in all that time he had never accepted so much as a coin that did not belong to him. His record was known, and it was also known that his record had left him with nothing to show for his honesty but a leaking roof and a daughter who had gone to work as a seamstress at fourteen because he could not afford to keep her in school.

He thought of Elena as he walked. She was at home now, in the narrow house on Calle de los Pescadores, lying on a mattress they had stuffed with straw because the horsehair one had been sold months ago. The fever had come in the autumn and had not left. Dr. Mendoza had told him, with the careful, pitying tone that doctors learn to perfect, that the medicine she needed would cost more than a customs officer earns in half a year. He had not said what would happen if she did not get it. He had not needed to. Joaquín had seen the same look in the doctor's eyes that he had seen in his mother's eyes when she spoke of his father, who had died of something similar, in a similar bed, in a similar state of poverty.

The sound of footsteps on the planks ahead broke his circuit of thought. A man emerged from the shadow between two warehouses, walking with the unhurried confidence of someone who expected to be exactly where he was. He was tall for these parts, dressed in a white linen suit that even in the dim light Joaquín could see was of a quality unavailable in any shop in San Cristóbal. His boots were black and polished. He wore a Panama hat, tilted slightly, and when he stopped a few paces from Joaquín, he smiled in a way that suggested the smile had been decided upon in advance, like a business decision.

"Señor Vargas," the man said. He spoke Spanish with an accent that was not quite Argentine, not quite Spanish, something cultivated and deliberately unclear. "I am Adrián Castellano. I believe you know my name."

Joaquín did. Everyone at the port knew the name Castellano, though no one could say precisely what it was that Señor Castellano did. His ships came and went. His cargo was loaded and unloaded. His papers, when they were presented, were always in order. There were stories, of course. There were always stories. But stories were not evidence, and Joaquín had always been a man who dealt in evidence.

"Señor Castellano," Joaquín said. He did not remove his hat. "You are far from your offices at this hour."

"I am exactly where I need to be." Castellano reached into the breast pocket of his jacket and produced a cigar, which he did not light. He held it between his fingers as one might hold a pen, turning it slowly. "I have a ship arriving tonight. The *Marisol*. She will dock at Pier Three within the hour. There will be loading between midnight and four. I would like your assurance that you will not find it necessary to inspect what is loaded, or to record it in your log."

Joaquín looked at him. The water lapped against the pilings beneath their feet. Somewhere out in the harbor, a bell rang on a buoy, a slow, indifferent tolling.

"That would be a serious dereliction of my duty, Señor Castellano."

"Yes," Castellano agreed, without offense. "It would." He paused, letting the silence gather between them like a third presence. "I am told your wife is unwell."

The words landed with the precision of a blade. Joaquín felt something tighten in his chest, a slow, hot pressure that he recognized as the particular anger that comes not from cruelty but from the sensation of being seen clearly by someone who intends to use what he sees.

"My wife's health is not a matter for the docks."

"No, of course not. Forgive me." Castellano did not sound forgiven, or forgiving. He reached into his jacket again and produced an envelope, thick and cream-colored, sealed with wax. He held it out. "This is five thousand pesos. There will be another five thousand when the *Marisol* sails at dawn. I have also arranged for a physician from Caracas, a specialist in fevers, to visit your home next week. All expenses paid."

Joaquín did not take the envelope. He stared at it. Five thousand pesos. Ten thousand. More money than he would earn in fifteen years of walking these docks. More money than his father had earned in his entire life, breaking his back in the cacao fields until his back broke him instead. The physician from Caracas. Elena, sitting up, the fever broken, the color returning to her face, the years returning to her years.

He thought of the morning two weeks ago when he had come home and found her on the floor beside the bed, too weak to call for help, her lips cracked and her eyes glassy. He thought of his daughter, Margarita, seventeen years old, stitching until her fingers bled, and the quiet, terrible understanding in her eyes when she looked at him, which was not blame, exactly, but something worse, something like the slow erosion of the belief that her father could make things right.

"I am not a wealthy man, Señor Castellano," Joaquín said slowly. "You clearly know that already."

"I know what you are, Señor Vargas. That is precisely why I am here."

"What is being loaded onto the *Marisol*?"

Castellano smiled again, the same rehearsed expression. "That is a question I was hoping you would not ask."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I will find another officer. There are six on the night rotation. Three of them, I believe, are more accommodating than you. The *Marisol* will sail regardless. The only question is whether your wife sees the physician from Caracas."

The silence that followed was the longest Joaquín had ever endured. The buoy bell tolled again. A rat moved along the edge of the pier, quick and purposeful, disappearing into a gap between the boards.

Joaquín took the envelope.

Castellano nodded once, as though confirming a delivery of ordinary merchandise, and turned back toward the warehouses. His footsteps receded, unhurried, confident, swallowed by the dark.

Joaquín stood alone on the pier. The envelope was heavy in his hand, heavier than paper had any right to be. He could feel the seal of wax beneath his thumb, smooth and warm, as though it still held the heat of the hand that had pressed it. He looked out at the harbor, at the lights of the town reflected in the black water, at the distant silhouette of the fort on the headland, built by the Spanish two centuries ago to guard against pirates and now used for nothing at all.

He thought of his father, who had died owing money to three different men and had apologized for it with his last breath. He thought of his mother, who had scrubbed floors in the houses of foreigners so that Joaquín could have shoes for school. He thought of Elena on their wedding day, twenty-four years ago, in the little church of San Roque, wearing a dress that her mother had made from curtains, and how she had laughed when she told him, and how he had not laughed because he had been ashamed, and how she had taken his hand and said, "We will be honest people, Joaquín, and honest people have nothing to be ashamed of."

He opened the envelope and looked inside. The bills were crisp, new, bundled with a band that bore Castellano's mark. He closed it again.

At midnight, the *Marisol* arrived, a dark shape sliding into the harbor with running lights dimmed. The loading began. Men moved crates and trunks from carts on the pier to the ship's hold with practiced, silent efficiency. Joaquín stood at the far end of Pier Three, his logbook under his arm, and watched. He did not approach. He did not inspect. He did not write.

At four o'clock, the loading ceased. The carts rolled away, their wheels creaking on the planks. At dawn, the *Marisol* raised anchor and slipped out of the harbor, her sails catching the first pale light from the east.

Joaquín walked home through the empty streets. The air was cooler now, almost pleasant, carrying the scent of jasmine from the courtyard gardens. He let himself in quietly. Margarita was asleep on a chair beside the bed. Elena lay still, her breathing shallow and uneven. He placed the envelope on the kitchen table and sat down across from it.

The morning light came through the window and fell across the envelope, illuminating the wax seal. He sat there for a long time, watching the light move across the table as the sun climbed, and he understood, with a clarity that was almost physical, that the man who had walked home was not the same man who had walked the docks the night before. Something had been loaded onto the *Marisol* that was not in any crate, and something had been unloaded from Joaquín Vargas that no amount of money could replace. He sat at the table and waited for his daughter to wake, and he did not yet know what he would tell her, or whether he would tell her anything at all.
