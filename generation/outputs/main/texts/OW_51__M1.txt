High above the roofs of the little town of Vinterhaven, in a tower of grey stone that the wind seemed to love and the sun had forgotten, lived Aldric the Miser. His only companions were his gold and his candle. Each night, when the town below had doffed its cap of smoke and settled into a deep, woolly silence, Aldric would draw the bolt upon his door and unroll his velvet cloth upon the table. There, by the light of one thin, yellow flame, he would count his coins. He loved the sound they made—a soft, sibilant whisper, like the rustling of silk in a room where no silk is to be found. It was the only conversation he had ever found entirely satisfactory.

One evening, when the winter had laid its white hand upon the land and the snow fell not in flakes but in a fine, crystalline dust, a sound disturbed the sacred ritual. It was a knocking, so faint and so persistent that at first Aldric took it for the tapping of a frozen branch against the shutter. But the sound continued, a patient, rhythmic plea that would not be denied. With a sigh that ruffled the candle flame, he rose and descended the winding stair, his heart filled with the indignation of a man whose privacy has been violated.

He opened the heavy oak door a crack, and a gust of cold air rushed in, smelling of snow and distance. On his threshold stood a small child, no higher than his knee, wrapped in a threadbare shawl the colour of a faded twilight. Her eyes were as wide and dark as a forest pool, and in her small, blue-tinged hands she held an empty lamp.

“Sir,” she said, her voice as thin as the winter wind, “my mother’s lamp has gone out, and the dark is very cold. Could you spare a single flame?”

Aldric looked down at her, and then at the candle burning in the room behind him. To give away a flame was to give away light, and light was a form of wealth. It was measured in wax, and wax was bought with coin. “A light is not a thing to be given away for nothing,” he said, his voice as sharp as an icicle. “What payment do you offer?”

The child looked up at him, her gaze untroubled by his severity. “I have no money,” she said simply. “But when the spring comes, I will bring you the first snowdrop from our garden. It is the whitest of all flowers.”

The absurdity of the offer struck him as a kind of audacious poetry. A flower! A perishable, useless, beautiful thing. It was the very currency of a world he had long since disowned. He grumbled, but he found himself turning back to the table. He took a single match from a box, struck it against the stone, and held the tiny, flickering star to the wick of her lamp. The flame caught, a warm, golden bud in the heart of the glass.

“There,” he said gruffly. “Now go, before you waste more of my heat.”

“Thank you, sir,” she said, and with a small, solemn curtsey, she turned and was swallowed by the swirling snow.

Aldric bolted the door and returned to his gold. But the coins, when he touched them, felt strangely cold. The light of his candle seemed thin and yellow, a sickly thing compared to the warm, living flame he had just surrendered. He counted his wealth, but his mind was not on the numbers. It was on the darkness the child had carried away with her, and the single, precious point of light he had placed within it. That night, for the first time, the whisper of the gold did not comfort him. It sounded like the rustling of a shroud.

The next evening, he found himself listening for the knock. It did not come. The silence of the tower was no longer peaceful; it was empty. He looked at his velvet cloth, at the neat rows of sovereigns and crowns, and he saw only a hoard of cold, indifferent metal. He thought of the child’s promise: a snowdrop. A single, white flower. It was a ridiculous thing to think of, and yet he could not think of anything else. He pictured it in a small glass on his table, a fragile star of white against the gloom. It would be a foolish, temporary, utterly useless possession. He wanted it more than he had ever wanted a bag of gold.

Days passed, and the snow deepened. Aldric grew restless. He would stand at his window, looking down at the town, a scattering of dark shapes against the white. He was a king in a tower of his own making, but his kingdom was a prison of his own design. The gold was his wall and his moat. But now he had seen a small face at the gate, and the walls no longer felt so secure.

One afternoon, as a pale sun struggled to pierce the grey sky, he saw her. She was walking with her mother in the street below, their forms small and dark against the snow. She was pointing up at his tower. Aldric drew back from the window, his heart thumping against his ribs like a trapped bird. He felt a strange, unfamiliar pang—not of anger, but of a deep and hollow ache. It was the feeling of a man who has everything and has just discovered that it is all worth nothing.

He waited until nightfall. Then, taking his lantern, he descended the stairs, unbarred the door, and stepped out into the biting cold. He walked down the winding path to the town, his boots crunching on the snow. The people he passed stared at him, for he had not been seen in the town for years. He was a phantom, a legend, the Miser of the Tower.

He found the small cottage at the edge of the wood. A soft light glowed in the window—the light he had given. He hesitated, then knocked. The child’s mother opened the door. When she saw him, her face filled with a mixture of fear and gratitude.

“Sir,” she began, “we cannot thank you enough for your kindness…”

Aldric cut her short, his discomfort a palpable thing. “The child,” he said. “Where is she?”

“She is sleeping,” the woman said. “The fever took her a week ago. She is better now, thanks to the light and the warmth.”

Aldric felt the world tilt beneath him. He had been counting his coins while a child was fighting for her life by the light he had so grudgingly given. He looked past the woman into the small room. The child was asleep in a cot, her face pale but peaceful in the lamplight. On a stool beside her was a small, chipped cup, and in it, a single snowdrop, its white head bowed as if in prayer. It was the first flower of the spring, born out of its time by the warmth of the cottage.

He reached into his purse and pulled out a heavy gold coin, a thing he had never parted with in his life. He pressed it into the woman’s hand. “For her,” he said, his voice rough with an emotion he could not name. Then he turned and walked away, back up the hill to his tower.

He never counted his gold again. The velvet cloth was folded away, and the coins were left to gather a soft, grey dust. The tower was no longer a treasury, but a watchtower. And each night, Aldric would sit by his window, a single candle burning beside him, not for light, but for company. He would look down at the town, and he would watch over the small cottage at the edge of the wood. He had discovered that a single flame, given away in the dark, could warm a man’s soul far more than all the gold in the world could ever warm his hands. The only true wealth, he realised, was a light that one was willing to share, and the only lasting treasure, a kindness one could not stop thinking about.
