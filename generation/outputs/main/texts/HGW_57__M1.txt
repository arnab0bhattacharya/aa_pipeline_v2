There is, I think, no man in Oxford less suited to a landlady’s solicitude than Mr. Alistair Finch. He occupied the two front rooms on the first floor of my house in Jowett’s Walk, and from the day he took them, some three years ago, he conducted himself not so much as a lodger as a sort of permanent, if baffling, experiment. He was a lecturer in physics at the College, a man of lean frame and a permanently distracted air, with spectacles that were always needing a push back up his nose. His mind, it was clear to me from the start, was not on this world at all. He would leave for his lectures with his tie askew and his hair standing on end, and return hours later, looking as if he had been arguing with a thundercloud and had, by some small miracle, won.

His rooms were a testament to this disordered genius. Books and papers were stacked in teetering columns on every available surface. Brass instruments of a terrifying complexity—oscillators, galvanometers, and things for which I have no name—gleamed from the mantelpiece and the windowsills. The air always smelled faintly of ozone and hot metal. And yet, for all the chaos, there was a strange tidiness in certain corners. A particular shelf, for instance, was kept scrupulously clear of the general debris. It was here he kept his most prized possessions: a series of glass jars, each containing what he called a "stabilised atmospheric sample" or a "quantum of condensed light." To my eye, they looked like so many empty bottles, but I learned to dust around them with the reverence one might reserve for the Crown Jewels.

The jar in question was the strangest of the lot. It was a stout, bell-shaped specimen jar of thick green glass, stoppered with a heavy cork and sealed with wax. Inside, there was nothing to be seen. Not a gas, not a liquid, not a solid. Just… a place. A little pocket of darkness that seemed to drink the light from the room. When you looked at it for too long, you felt a peculiar dizziness, a sense that the world was not quite as solid as it ought to be. Finch called it his "Inverted Field." I called it the Empty Bottle and made a point of giving it a wide berth.

The trouble, when it came, was of the most ordinary kind. It was a Tuesday in late November, a day of driving rain and a wind that rattled the windowpanes like a vexed spirit. Mr. Finch had gone off in a great state of excitement, muttering about a "paradigm shift in thermodynamics" and forgetting his umbrella. I, meanwhile, decided it was the perfect day for a thorough cleaning of his rooms. He had given me strict orders never to touch his work, but a landlady has her standards, and a fortnight’s worth of dust is a challenge to any woman’s pride.

I had worked my way around the room and was just setting to rights the mantelpiece when my apron strings caught on the leg of a stool. I gave a little tug to free myself, stumbled, and my elbow struck the corner of the shelf. There was a sickening little clatter, a rolling sound, and then a crash as the green jar fell to the floorboards and shattered.

For a moment, I simply stared at the broken glass. Then I saw it. The thing that had been in the jar was no longer contained. It lay upon the floor like a puddle of ink, but it was not wet. It was a patch of absolute, starless night, perhaps two feet across, and it did not reflect the grey light from the window. It simply… wasn't. My mind, a practical thing built for darning socks and mending fences, refused to process it. I felt a sudden, violent wave of nausea and a strange coldness that had nothing to do with the draught from the window.

Instinctively, I took a step back. And I saw it. The fallen piece of the heavy brass clock from the mantelpiece, which had been knocked down with the jar, was lying near the edge of the darkness. As I watched, it began to slide. Not downwards, for the floor was level, but *upwards* and *towards* the patch of nothing. It rose from the floorboards, tumbled end over end in a slow, silent ballet, and was swallowed by the blackness without a sound. The dust motes in the air, which should have been settling, began to drift towards it, accelerating until they vanished into its depths. A loose thread from the rug began to snake across the floor, being pulled by an invisible, inexorable hand.

This was no longer a matter for a landlady. This was a matter for a man of science. I fled the room, my heart hammering against my ribs, and ran down the stairs. The front door burst open and Mr. Finch stood there, soaked to the skin, his spectacles fogged over, clutching a sheaf of dripping papers.

"Mrs. Gable!" he cried, with a triumphant air. "A most astonishing day! I have proved that entropy is not a fundamental law, but a local convention! A mere habit of our particular cosmos!"

"Mr. Finch!" I gasped, pointing a trembling finger up the stairs. "Your jar! The Empty Bottle! It's broken!"

The colour drained from his face. The triumphant light in his eyes was extinguished as if by a snuffer. "Good God," he whispered. "The containment field…"

He took the stairs three at a time, and I followed, my fear warring with a desperate curiosity. He stopped dead in the doorway of his sitting-room. The patch of darkness on the floor had grown. It was now the size of a small rug, and the effect was more pronounced. A stack of his papers near it was un-stacking itself, the sheets flying one by one into the void. A heavy volume of Newton’s *Principia* was slowly rising from the table.

"Reverse gravity," Finch muttered, his voice tight with academic fascination rather than fear. "Spatial contraction. It’s drawing matter in and… un-making it. Reversing its very properties. The laws of nature are running backwards in there." He looked at the floating book. "Fascinating. The binding is coming undone, thread by thread, in the exact order it was sewn."

"It's a hole in the world, sir!" I cried. "We must do something!"

"Doing something is precisely the problem," he said, pushing his spectacles up. He was no longer a lodger; he was a lecturer, and I was his unwilling student. "Any attempt to plug it would simply be drawn in and reversed. A net would un-weave itself. A solid block of iron would… well, who knows? Perhaps revert to ore, then to dust, then to constituent atoms."

The blackness was creeping towards our feet now. The carpet was beginning to fray, its fibres reaching towards the abyss. The air grew colder, and a low hum vibrated through the floorboards. I felt a dreadful pull, a sensation as if my own bones were being urged to fly apart.

"The conservation of energy!" Finch suddenly exclaimed, his eyes wide. "It must be conserved, even here! It’s not a hole, Mrs. Gable, it’s an engine! A perfect engine of reversal! It takes order and gives back… what? Chaos? Simplicity? It’s running down our reality to power its own!"

He looked around wildly, his scientific mind racing for a solution. His eyes fell upon the heavy iron poker by the fireplace. Then he looked at the floating *Principia*. A slow, terrible smile spread across his face.

"An equal and opposite reaction," he murmured. "Of course. It’s so simple."

Before I could stop him, he seized the poker and, with a grunt, hurled it not *at* the darkness, but into the air just above it. The poker, caught in the strange field, did not fall. It hovered for a second, then began to move, not towards the blackness, but away from it, towards the ceiling. As it flew, it began to change. The black iron grew mottled, then orange with rust, the rust flaking away to reveal raw, gleaming metal beneath. It was un-forging itself in mid-air.

But the effect on the darkness was immediate. It recoiled. The creeping edge stopped its advance and shrank back an inch. The hum lessened.

"It works!" Finch shouted. "Matter and anti-matter, order and its inversion! We must introduce more complexity, more *order*, into its field! It cannot digest it!"

He began to grab things from his shelves—intricate clockwork devices, carefully wound springs, a complex crystal lattice model. He threw them, one by one, into the air above the shrinking void. Each object hovered, reversed its own creation in a bizarre spectacle of un-assembly, and in doing so, forced the patch of darkness to contract further. It was shrinking now, rapidly, folding in on itself. The *Principia* dropped to the table with a thud. The papers fluttered to the floor. The coldness receded.

With a final, faint *pop*, like a soap bubble bursting in reverse, the last speck of darkness vanished. The floorboards where it had been were scarred and warped, but whole. The room was silent, save for our ragged breathing and the ticking of the one clock that had remained on the wall.

Mr. Finch stood amidst the wreckage of his room, a strange, triumphant glint in his eye. He looked at me, then at the mess, and then at the spot on the floor.

"Well, Mrs. Gable," he said, brushing a piece of rust from his sleeve. "I do believe we have just witnessed the first practical demonstration of temporal mechanics." He paused, and a slow, dawning horror replaced the triumph on his face. "Oh, dear. I do hope the University's insurance covers acts of theoretical physics." He looked at me, his expression utterly lost. "I suppose you'll be wanting a reference."
