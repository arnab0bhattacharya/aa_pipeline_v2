The air in the Engadine did not smell of pine, as one might expect, but of damp wool, boiled eggs, and a faint, persistent trace of camphor that seemed to hang in the stillness like a ghost refusing to dissipate. Arthur lay on the chaise longue, the white linen stiff against his chest, watching the dust motes dance in the shaft of pale afternoon light that pierced the heavy curtains. They moved with a slow, deliberate grace, indifferent to the human drama unfolding in the room, indifferent to the coughing in the next corridor, to the tapping of the nurse’s shoes, to the very existence of Arthur. He felt less like a man and more like a piece of furniture, a chair with a heartbeat, waiting for the next command, the next dose of bromide, the next shift in the barometric pressure. The mountain outside was a jagged white tooth biting into a sky so blue it hurt, a blue so absolute it seemed to exclude all other colors, all other possibilities.

He was told he was better. The doctor, a man with a face like a closed book and hands that were never quite clean, had said, *You must have fresh air, Arthur. You must have rest. The mountains will knit your bones back together.* And so Arthur had come, driven up in a carriage that smelled of leather and sweat, to this hotel where the air was thin and the silence was thick. He had come to be cured, but he had not expected to find the cure so difficult to swallow, so at odds with the hollow, echoing quality of his own thoughts.

It was on the third day, perhaps the fourth, that he noticed her. She was sitting by the window in the tea room, not in the corner reserved for the invalids and their anxious attendants, but in a chair that faced the door, her back to the glass. She was not sick. There was no coughing fit, no trembling hand, no pale, drawn face. She was dressed in a simple dress of dark green wool, her hair pinned up severely, and she was reading a book, or perhaps she was only holding it, for her eyes were fixed on the window, on the snow, on the nothingness beyond. She looked as if she had been cut from the same cloth as the landscape, solid, enduring, and cold.

Arthur watched her. He had no right to watch her, he knew that. He was a patient, a specimen, a thing to be observed and measured. Yet his eyes were drawn to her with a magnetism he could not explain. It was not that she was beautiful, not in the way that the young wives of the bankers and the industrialists were beautiful, bright and polished and aware of their own reflection in the world. She was quiet. She was still. In a room full of movement, of nurses and doctors and families, she was a point of absolute rest.

He felt a strange surge of something, not quite hope, not quite desire, but a recognition. It was as if he had seen her before, in a dream, in a memory of a dream, in the space between one heartbeat and the next. He wanted to know why she was there. She was not ill. Why was she there?

The afternoon light shifted, turning the dust motes from gold to grey. The sound of the tea service clattering in the corner grew louder, a sharp, metallic noise that seemed to cut into the silence. Arthur tried to turn his head, to look away, but his neck was stiff, his body heavy with the inertia of illness. He was trapped in the moment, caught in the web of his own perception.

She looked up.

It was a small movement, barely a flicker of the eye, but it was enough. Their eyes met. For a second, the world held its breath. The clattering of the tea cups faded, the coughing in the next room ceased, the wind outside stopped its howling. There was only the two of them, suspended in the thin, cold air, separated by the distance between the chaise longue and the chair, but connected by a thread so fine it was invisible.

She did not smile. She did not nod. She simply looked at him, with an expression that was difficult to read, but which seemed to contain a great deal of sorrow and a great deal of defiance. Then, slowly, she turned her head back to the window, and the moment was broken. The world rushed back in, with its noise and its pain and its demands.

Arthur felt a wave of shame wash over him. He had been rude, intrusive, a voyeur in his own suffering. He closed his eyes, trying to banish the image of her face, but it remained, imprinted on the inside of his eyelids, burning and bright. He thought of his own family, of his father’s stern face, of his mother’s frantic love, of the expectations that had crushed him before the illness had even taken hold. He thought of the hotel, of the mountains, of the doctor’s promises. And he thought of her, who had come here not to be cured, but to escape.

What was it that she was escaping from? What was the thing that was so heavy, so suffocating, that she had had to flee to the top of the world to breathe? Arthur did not know. He did not know who she was, or what her name was, or why she was there. He only knew that she was there, and that she was real, and that in her presence, the emptiness inside him seemed to shrink, just for a moment, just for the duration of a glance.

The evening came, bringing with it a cold that bit through the wool of his coat. The lights in the hotel were dimmed, and the shadows grew long and dark. Arthur lay in his bed, listening to the wind whistle through the eaves, a sound like a ghost moaning. He tried to sleep, but his mind was too active, too full of questions. He thought of the white mountain, the blue sky, the dust motes, the dark green dress. He thought of the silence that had hung between them, thick and sweet and terrible.

He did not know if he would see her again. He did not know if he would leave this hotel in a few weeks, cured or not, and return to the life that had broken him. He did not know if she would leave, or if she would stay, or if she would disappear into the mountain, into the snow, into the nothingness that she had seemed to inhabit. He only knew that he had seen her, and that in seeing her, he had seen something of himself, something that he had lost, something that he was afraid to look for.

The wind howled on, a low, mournful sound, like a prayer that had gone unanswered. Arthur closed his eyes, and waited for the dark.
