It was upon a bleak Tuesday in November, when the yellow fog hung thick over the streets of London, that Mr. Arthur Vane was ushered into our sitting-room. He was a tall, powerfully built man, weather-beaten of face and hands, bearing the unmistakable look of one accustomed to the open air. His boots were thick with the yellow clay of the north, and his manner was that of a man pressed to the very edge of his patience.

"You are Mr. Harding, the investigator?" he asked, glancing keenly from my companion to myself.

"I am," my friend replied, motioning him to a chair. "This is my friend, Dr. Croft, whose assistance is invaluable to me. Pray sit down, Mr. Vane, and tell us what it is that brings an engineer of the Great Northern Railway all the way to London."

Vane sat down heavily and passed a grimy hand over his brow. "It is the Thorndale Viaduct, Mr. Harding. I am the chief engineer upon the line. We are driving a new branch across the Yorkshire moors, and the chief obstacle is the valley at Thorndale. It is a deep, gloomy hollow, requiring a viaduct of some three hundred yards."

"A considerable undertaking," said Harding, his keen grey eyes fixed upon the engineer. "But I gather from your manner that the difficulty lies not in the masonry, but in something altogether less tangible."

"You are right, sir," said Vane, leaning forward. "For three weeks past my surveying stakes have been moved."

Harding steepled his fingers. "Moved? By whom?"

"That, Mr. Harding, is what I am paying you to discover. I set my line by the theodolite, drive in the oak stakes, and stretch the lines. In the morning, the stakes are pulled up and driven in again, a good twelve feet to the east of their original position."

"And this alteration," said Harding, "does it affect any particular property?"

"It affects Hallowgate Farm, which belongs to a man named Josiah Grimes. The original line ran clean through his homestead. The altered line misses it entirely, crossing the valley at a broader, more expensive span."

"Then the motive is clear enough," I observed. "Grimes is preserving his farm."

"So the company believes," said Vane, bitterly. "I went to Grimes. He is a rough, sullen fellow, with a great red beard and an eye like a hawk. He denied all knowledge of the matter, swore he had not been near the works, and threatened me with the law for trespass. But the company directors care nothing for my suspicions. They care only for the delay. If the stakes are moved again, they will dismiss me and send a new man."

Harding rose and crossed to the map that hung upon the wall. "You say the stakes are moved twelve feet to the east. Is the ground there sound?"

"It is," said Vane, "though it necessitates a longer and more costly span. It is sound enough, but it is not my line."

"Very well," said Harding. "We shall take the night express to York, Mr. Vane, and a dog-cart from there to Thorndale. Dr. Croft, you will, I hope, accompany us."

I assented, and by the following afternoon we were rattling over the bare, heather-clad moors in a high dog-cart, the raw Yorkshire wind biting at our cheeks. The Thorndale valley lay before us, a deep cleft in the hills, its sides clothed in scree and coarse grass. At the bottom, a brown beck foamed over its stony bed. Upon the eastern slope stood a square, stone-built farmhouse, from which a thin wreath of peat-smoke arose.

We alighted at the works, where a gang of navvies were at work upon the foundations. Harding walked rapidly along the line of stakes, his sharp eyes taking in every detail of the ground. He examined the stakes themselves, pulling at them and noting the nature of the soil. Then he walked to the edge of the works and stood looking down into the valley, his hands in his pockets.

"Tell me, Mr. Vane," said he, "what lies beneath the original line? You spoke of driving the line through the homestead. But what of the ground itself?"

"It is a hard gravel," said Vane. "Excellent foundation."

"And the new line, twelve feet to the east?"

"It is the same gravel, as I have said."

"Are you sure of that?" asked Harding, with a curious intonation.

Vane looked startled. "I have not tested it. The ground appeared the same."

"Precisely," said Harding. "It appeared the same. Come, let us walk down to the farmhouse. I should like to see this Josiah Grimes."

We descended the slope to Hallowgate Farm. It was a substantial building of grey stone, with a slate roof and small, latticed windows. As we approached, a tall man came out of the door. He was dressed in a rough homespun coat, and his hands were buried in his pockets. His face was dark and forbidding, and his red beard gave him a wild appearance. It was Josiah Grimes.

"What do ye want?" he asked, looking from one to the other of us with no friendly eye.

"I am looking over the railway works," said Harding, easily. "A fine valley this, Mr. Grimes. You have a good property here."

"Aye, I have that," said the farmer, "and I mean to keep it, railway or no railway. I have told Mr. Vane as much."

"You deny, then, that you have touched the surveying stakes?"

"I do. I have not been near his old sticks."

Harding looked at him thoughtfully. "You are a man of your word, I doubt not. But tell me, how comes it that your cowshed yonder is built upon a foundation of new brick, when the rest of the farm is of the old grey stone?"

Grimes started, and his dark eyes flashed. "What business is that of yours?" he cried.

"None, perhaps," said Harding. "Only it is a curious thing to build a new foundation and then to put an old shed upon it. Good day, Mr. Grimes."

We turned and walked away, leaving the farmer staring after us with a look of sullen apprehension.

"What do you make of it, Harding?" I asked, as we climbed the slope.

"I think, Croft, that Josiah Grimes is a very clever man, and that he is hiding something. But I must see the ground by night."

That night, under a cold, starlit sky, we lay in hiding among the heather at the head of the valley. Harding had brought a dark lantern, and Vane carried a heavy stick. The wind sighed in the heather, and the beck murmured far below. We lay there for two hours, chilled to the bone, before Harding suddenly gripped my arm.

"Listen!" he whispered.

I heard the sound of footsteps, soft and cautious, coming up the slope from the farmhouse. Presently a dark figure appeared, carrying a lantern. It was Grimes. He went straight to the line of stakes, and setting down his lantern, began to pull them up.

Harding was upon him in an instant. The farmer turned with a snarl of rage, but Harding's grip was like iron.

"Steady, my man," said he. "You will do yourself no good by violence. I want to know why you move these stakes."

Grimes made no answer, but his face was convulsed with fear.

"Come," said Harding, "you are a clever fellow, Grimes. You have discovered that the ground under the original line is not solid gravel, but a thin crust overlying a bog. You know that a viaduct built there would subside and fall. You have tried to save your own land by moving the line, and you have kept silent because you feared that if the truth were known, the company would resume your farm at a low price."

Grimes stared at him. "How did you know?" he gasped.

"The new brick foundation of your cowshed told me that you had been digging. The soil here is yellow clay, but the clay on your boots, Mr. Vane, when you came to me, was black. There is black peat under this yellow gravel. It is a bog, Mr. Vane, and your original line would have been a disaster."

The engineer stood speechless. "Then the man has saved the line!"

"He has," said Harding, "but he has gone the wrong way about it. Come, Mr. Vane, let us settle this matter with the company in the morning. I think we may say that Mr. Grimes's fears were well founded, and that the new line is the better line after all."

And so it proved. The company, recognizing the danger that had been so strangely revealed, adopted the altered line, and Thorndale Viaduct stands today a monument to the shrewdness of a Yorkshire farmer and the insight of my friend.
