MR. BARNABY SKEGGS, Parish Constable of the united parishes of St. Sepulchre-in-the-Marsh and St. Clement-Without-the-Walls, was a man of such surpassing dignity of bearing that the very beadles, those humble satellites of authority, were wont to droop their heads as he passed, and the urchins of Clerkenwell-green to cease their marbles and stand in silent awe, as though the great man’s shadow, falling upon them, had frozen them to the spot. He was a bulky personage, with a face the colour of a well-kept copper kettle, a nose that seemed to have been designed for the express purpose of sniffing out offences against the peace, and a pair of eyes that rolled with a slow and terrible significance, as though they were perpetually reviewing the whole criminal code from the preamble to the final clause. His gait was a sort of majestic roll, his voice a deep and hollow rumble, and his hat—a hat of the most uncompromising blackness and the most aggressive brim—was worn at an angle that seemed to say to all the world, “I am the law; stand aside!”

It was this very hat, and the roll of the gait, and the rumble of the voice, that had first attracted the notice of Mrs. Priscilla Pinhorn, widow, of the sign of the Golden Gridiron, in Pig-and-Whistle-court, where she kept a cookshop of no small repute among the clerks and small tradesmen of the neighbourhood. Mrs. Pinhorn was a plump, rosy, bustling little woman, with a cap of such elaborate whiteness that it seemed to have been bleached in the very sunshine of her own cheerful countenance, and a pair of arms that were never idle, whether in the kneading of dough, the stirring of a pot, or the boxing of the ears of her boy, a small and shrivelled youth of twelve, who seemed to have been born with a natural antipathy to soap and water, and who went by the name of Bags.

Now, it had long been the secret ambition of Mrs. Pinhorn to be a person of consequence. She was not without a certain respectability of her own, it is true; her pies were celebrated, her sausages were unexceptionable, and her Sunday dinners were the envy of half the parish. But she felt that she wanted a something—a something that should lift her above the mere purveyor of victuals, and place her in that enviable position where one may look down upon one’s neighbours from the height of an official connection. And what could be more respectable than to be the wife of a parish constable? The very thought of it made her caps seem whiter and her pies more substantial.

Mr. Skeggs, on his part, had his own views. He was a man of large appetite and small means; his salary was respectable but insufficient, and his lodgings, in a dark and mouldy house in Turnagain-lane, were the abomination of his soul. He had cast his eyes upon the Golden Gridiron, and upon Mrs. Pinhorn, and upon the little stocking under the loose board in her bedroom, which he had once glimpsed by accident, and which he had calculated, with the rapid arithmetic of a man who has long studied the subject, to contain no less than seventy pounds. To marry the widow, to become master of the cookshop, to have his meals served hot and his ale drawn fresh, and to have the spending of that seventy pounds—these were the visions that floated before him as he rolled home at night, and caused him to smack his lips with a solemn satisfaction.

He accordingly began to pay his addresses with all the pomp and circumstance of his office. He would drop in of an evening, and seating himself in the corner of the little parlour, with his hat upon his knee, would discourse upon the enormities of the times, the increase of poaching, the prevalence of drunkenness among the lower orders, and the necessity of a firm hand in the administration of justice. Mrs. Pinhorn would listen with a respectful admiration, and would press upon him the best of her sausages and the freshest of her ale, and would remark, with a little simper, that it was a great comfort to a lone woman to have a gentleman of his experience to advise her in matters of business.

“Ma’am,” Mr. Skeggs would reply, “the law is a great blessing, and them as has the administering of it has a great responsibility. I feel it, Mrs. Pinhorn, I feel it here”—and he would lay his hand upon his waistcoat with an air that was meant to be affecting.

“I’m sure you do, sir,” Mrs. Pinhorn would say; “and it’s a credit to you, Mr. Skeggs, that you do.”

And so the courtship went on, with a great deal of eating and drinking on one side, and a great deal of nodding and smiling on the other, until at last Mr. Skeggs, having drunk a pint of ale more than usual one evening, and having been particularly eloquent upon the subject of the watch-house, suddenly declared that he was a lonely man, and that if Mrs. Pinhorn would consent to make him happy, he would be proud to call her his own.

Mrs. Pinhorn, who had been expecting this for some weeks, and who had her answer ready, replied with a proper modesty that she was but a humble woman, and that she feared she was not worthy of such an honour; but that if Mr. Skeggs really thought so, she would not say him nay. And then there was a great shaking of hands, and a great smacking of lips on the part of Mr. Skeggs, and a great deal of blushing on the part of Mrs. Pinhorn, and the wedding was fixed for the following Monday.

Now, it is a remarkable fact, and one that shows how little we know of the human heart, that during the whole of this courtship, neither of these two persons had the slightest suspicion of the other’s real motive. Mr. Skeggs believed Mrs. Pinhorn to be a simple, affectionate woman, who admired him for his dignity. Mrs. Pinhorn believed Mr. Skeggs to be a generous, disinterested man, who admired her for her person. Each was thinking of the seventy pounds, or of the respectability of the office, as the case might be; and each was as blind as a beetle to the other’s designs.

It was on the Saturday evening before the wedding that the discovery was made. Mr. Skeggs had come in to take his usual supper, and to give his usual lecture upon the state of the parish. He was in high spirits, for he had that morning committed a poacher, and had received the congratulations of the beadles. He had eaten three sausages, and was about to attack a fourth, when Mrs. Pinhorn, who had been bustling about with a face of unusual gravity, suddenly drew her chair close to his, and said, in a confidential whisper:

“Mr. Skeggs, I have been thinking a great deal about our marriage.”

“Have you, ma’am?” said Mr. Skeggs, with his mouth full. “And what have you been thinking?”

“I have been thinking,” said Mrs. Pinhorn, “that as you are a man of property, and as I am a woman of property, it would be as well if we came to a right understanding about it before the ceremony.”

Mr. Skeggs laid down his knife and fork. “Property, ma’am?” said he. “I don’t know as I have much property, except my character, which is, I hope, unblemished.”

“Oh, fie, Mr. Skeggs!” said Mrs. Pinhorn, with a little laugh. “You know what I mean. The little matter of the savings. I have seventy pounds in the stocking, and I don’t doubt you have as much or more in the bank.”

Mr. Skeggs turned the colour of a boiled lobster. “Seventy pounds!” he gasped. “In the stocking! Why, ma’am, I thought—that is to say, I didn’t know—I never supposed—”

“And as for you,” continued Mrs. Pinhorn, “I have always understood that a parish constable is a man of substance. You have your salary, and your fees, and your perquisites, and I make no doubt you have a pretty little sum laid by.”

Mr. Skeggs was silent for a moment. He looked at the sausage on his plate, and then at Mrs. Pinhorn, and then at the sausage again. At last he said, with a very rueful countenance:

“Mrs. Pinhorn, I’m a man of few words, and I’ll tell you the truth. I haven’t got a penny in the world but my salary, and that’s mostly spent before I get it. I thought you had the seventy pounds, and I thought it would be a comfortable thing to have the spending of it.”

Mrs. Pinhorn started up from her chair. “And I thought you had a fortune!” she cried. “I thought you were a man of property! I thought I should have been a lady! And you’re nothing but a poor, proud, empty-headed constable, as hasn’t got a sixpence to bless himself with!”

“And you’re nothing but a cookshop keeper!” retorted Mr. Skeggs, rising in his wrath. “And a pretty figure you’d have cut as the wife of a parish constable, with your sausages and your pies, and your nasty little boy as never washes his face!”

“Get out of my house!” screamed Mrs. Pinhorn, seizing the poker. “Get out of my house, you deceiver! You impostor! You base, false, designing man!”

Mr. Skeggs did not wait to be told twice. He clapped his hat upon his head, and made for the door; but at the threshold he turned, and shaking his fist at the trembling widow, he exclaimed, with all the dignity he could muster:

“Mrs. Pinhorn, I despise you! I have been deceived, ma’am, shamefully deceived; and I have the honour to wish you good evening!”

And with that he rolled out of the house, and the door was slammed behind him with a violence that made the very sausages dance in the window. And so ended the courtship of Mr. Barnaby Skeggs and Mrs. Priscilla Pinhorn; and the neighbours, who had seen the constable’s hat go rolling down the court, and had heard the poker rattle against the door, remarked to one another that it was a pity, but that after all it was better to find out these things before the wedding than after; and Mrs. Pinhorn, having cried a little, and having boxed Bags’s ears to relieve her feelings, went back to her sausages, and was as brisk and as rosy as ever by the time the evening customers came in.
