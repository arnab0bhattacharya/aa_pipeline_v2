It is in the nature of human sorrow to seek the privacy of dusk, just as the small field-creatures, whose lives are passed under the constant menace of the plough and the sparrow, contrive their nests in the deepest hedgerows. Sophia Marlow had turned from the parlour window in Southrey, where the pale October sun was falling upon the market-cross, and now stood by the deal table, turning over a letter which had come by the morning coach from Hull. She turned it over with a slow, absent movement, as one who handles the outward envelope of some invisible electrical machine, fearful of the shock which the hidden wires are preparing for the touch. Her mother, Mrs. Marlow, sat by the fire in the rocking-chair, knitting with that steady mechanical industry which seems to belong especially to widows who have known much tribulation, as if the constant action of the needles were a sort of penance by which the past might be kept from rising up too vividly in the mind.

The secret which Sophia had that morning discovered was one of those facts which, long hidden beneath the smooth surface of domestic routine, are at length forced into the air by some trifling fracture in the crust of circumstance. Mrs. Marlow had been understood, for fifteen years, to be a widow; and the townsfolk of Southrey, who are much occupied with the quality of wool and the price of oats, had never thought to question a story which was at once convenient and sufficiently melancholy to satisfy the local appetite for narrative. But the letter now lying on the deal table was not from a dead man. It was from Jonas Marlow, alive and dwelling in some obscure street of Hull, asking, with a certain battered and diminished eloquence, to see his daughter before he died.

Sophia’s mind was not one of those which can rapidly adjust themselves to a new perspective. She was a young woman of four-and-twenty, possessed of a strong but slow understanding, and her affections had grown in the rich, unquestioning soil of filial trust. To find that her mother had deceived her, even for the most compassionate of reasons, was to feel the solid earth-rock of her moral universe shift beneath her feet. She looked at her mother with a new, strange, hesitating gaze, as if she were seeing her across some dividing fog; and Mrs. Marlow, conscious of that gaze, knit faster, while her thin lips took a firmer pressure.

"Mother," Sophia said at last, "I must go to him."

Mrs. Marlow laid her knitting on her lap and looked up with a face in which wounded pride and maternal terror were mingled. "You shall not," she said. "I forbid it. You shall not go to a man that left me with nothing but the clothes on my back and you in my arms, to face the sneers of my own kin and the cold charity of strangers. A pretty father he was! And now, because he’s like to die, he thinks to call you daughter again. Let him die where he left us—for all he cares."

"But he is my father," said Sophia, in a low voice, feeling the while how insufficient was that assertion against the heavy weight of her mother’s wrongs, yet unable to dismiss the claim of blood, which operates upon the mind with a force not unlike that of gravitation upon the body, invisible in itself but manifest in all the motions of life.

"Father!" Mrs. Marlow repeated, with a scornful intonation which belonged rather to the market-women of the town than to her usual carefully guarded speech. "A father is as a father does. I’ve been father and mother both to you, Sophia, these fifteen year gone by, and I’ll not have my work undone by a drunken, shiftless wastrel that writes fine words when he knows he’s near his end. You know nought of him, and you shall not know."

Here was the old, sharp dialect of the Lincolnshire fen-edge, which Mrs. Marlow had subdued in her ordinary conversation but which came back to her in moments of strong feeling, as the deeper strata of rock come to the surface where the upper soil has been worn away by sudden floods. Sophia recognised the tone; it was the tone in which her mother had spoken when the bailiff came to distrain for the rent, or when the fever was in the town and she had sat up three nights running to nurse the children in the alley. It was the tone of a woman who had fought the world single-handed and had grown hard in the fighting.

Yet Sophia, while she felt the force of her mother’s anguish, was conscious of a widening breach between her own conscience and that maternal authority which had hitherto been the guiding principle of her life. She was thinking, with that slow analytic tendency which was the chief intellectual inheritance she owed to her mother, of the possible state of mind of the man in Hull. He had done wrong, doubtless; but the wrong was fifteen years old, and he was now in the shadow of death, and the desire to see the child whom he had deserted might be, after all, the one surviving motion of a conscience not wholly dead. It is difficult for a generous nature to refuse the last petition of even a culpable fellow-creature; for the imagination, which is the parent of pity, represents to us the lonely deathbed with a vividness that overpowers the historical remonstrances of justice.

"I cannot let him die without seeing me," Sophia said, with a quietness which she did not feel, for her heart was beating with that painful tumult which belongs to the first assertion of individual will against the authority we have been accustomed to revere. "I am sorry, mother, for all you have borne; but I must judge for myself in this. He may be all you say, but I must see him once."

Mrs. Marlow rose from her chair and stood upright, her small figure drawn to its full height, her grey eyes fixed upon her daughter with a look of concentrated reproach. She said nothing more, but turned and left the room, closing the door behind her with a careful precision which was more eloquent than any passionate violence. For in the lives of obscure and unlettered people, whose feelings have never found expression in the large language of literature or the dramatic situations of history, emotion is often most intense where it is most silent, and the closing of a door may carry the whole weight of a tragedy which a more articulate society would have spread over many pages of dialogue.

Sophia sat down by the table and took up the letter again, reading it through for the third time. It was written in a cramped, uneven hand, with many blots, and the spelling was not such as would have satisfied the schoolmaster; but there was in it a certain crude pathos, a rough endeavour to express sentiments which the writer had probably never been accustomed to reduce to words, that touched her the more because it was so unskilful. He spoke of his failing health, of his desire to make what amends he could, of his hope that she would not be hard upon him. He did not, she observed, mention her mother; and this omission seemed to her the saddest token of the gulf which lay between those two lives, once so closely united and now so widely sundered that even the approach of death could not bridge it.

The afternoon waned, and the shadows in the little parlour lengthened, and still Sophia sat, turning over in her mind the difficult problem of duty and affection. She was aware that in going to Hull she would be setting up her judgment against her mother’s, and that the act would carry consequences which could not be foreseen. The web of family life is so intricately woven that the pulling of a single thread may loosen the whole fabric; and in the narrow society of a provincial town, where every action is observed and commented upon, the rupture between mother and daughter would become the property of the public, to be turned over and moralised upon in many a kitchen and many a shop. Yet the inward voice, which is often the most authoritative because it speaks without the passion of personal wrong, urged her to go. It told her that the claims of the living are not always paramount over the claims of the dying, and that the highest form of filial respect may sometimes consist in refusing to follow the dictates even of a mother’s sorrow.
