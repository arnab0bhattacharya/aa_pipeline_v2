The morning room at Delamere Priory was, by long habit, Mrs. Ellinor Ashcombe’s own; and it argued a certain generosity of temper in her son, or at least a certain indolence, that she had never been asked to consider it otherwise. It was a handsome apartment, with a bow window commanding the lawn and the two cedars, and a fire that was permitted to burn from October to May without remark; and here the widow sat, as she had sat for eleven years, with her work-basket, her little dog, and the comfortable sense of being necessary to the order of the house.

That she was no longer necessary was a truth she had not yet been able to admit into her heart, though she had begun to suspect it in her understanding. The new Mrs. Ashcombe—for the son had married in the spring, and it was now November—had brought with her from Bath a quantity of books, a manner of great composure, and no discernible desire to be managed. She was civil, she was even kind; but she was kind in the way of a person who has decided in advance how much kindness the occasion requires, and who does not intend to be drawn beyond it. Mrs. Ashcombe the elder, accustomed to the effusions of gratitude and the small daily dependencies of a household that had learned to lean upon her, found this economy of feeling very hard to bear, and could only conclude that she was to be turned out.

The fear had come upon her gradually, as fears of that nature do, and had been fed by trifles. A chair moved from its place by the fire. A suggestion, offered with the sweetest possible air, that the draught from the bow window might be injurious in December. The books—those books!—arriving by the carrier’s cart and being unpacked, not in the library, but in the morning room itself, as though the morning room were to become a place of study rather than of conversation. Each circumstance was nothing; together they composed a sentence of banishment, which the widow read with the solemnity of a judge and the trepidation of a culprit.

She therefore redoubled her attentions. If Mrs. Ashcombe the younger wished to read, Mrs. Ashcombe the elder would read to her; if she wished for quiet, she should have the quiet of a devoted companion, who would sit by her in silence and press her hand at intervals. She sent for the physician when the young woman coughed twice; she ordered the cook to prepare a broth that had done good service in the family for three generations; she inquired, with tender persistency, after the state of her daughter-in-law’s spirits, her sleep, her appetite, and her correspondence.

“My dear,” said she, upon a Tuesday morning, entering the morning room with the little dog under her arm and a shawl over her own, “you are pale. You are decidedly pale. I have observed it these three days. You must let me send for Mr. Harbottle.”

Mrs. Ashcombe the younger looked up from her book—it was a volume of Cowper, and she had been reading the same page for a quarter of an hour—and said, with perfect civility, “You are very good, ma’am, but I am not in the least ill.”

“Not ill! My dear, I know what it is to be ill and not to know it. Your father—I mean Mr. Ashcombe’s father—was just so. He would never allow himself to be ill until he was at death’s door, and then it was too late for Mr. Harbottle, quite too late.” She sighed, and settled herself in the chair opposite. “You will let me ring for tea.”

“I have had my tea, ma’am.”

“A little more will do you no harm. It is a cold morning. The wind is easterly. I felt it in the passage. You should not sit so near the window, my dear; indeed you should not.”

“I am very fond of the window.”

“So was I at your age—so was I. But we learn wisdom, my dear, as we grow older, and we learn that a window is not always a friend.” The widow smiled, with the melancholy archness of one who has suffered. “I hope I have not disturbed your reading.”

“Not at all,” said Mrs. Ashcombe the younger, and turned a page.

The elder lady was not to be so dismissed. She drew her chair a little nearer, and lowered her voice into the tone of tender confidence which she had found efficacious with her son in his boyhood. “I have been thinking, my dear, of the winter. The winter at Delamere is very trying—very trying indeed. The roads are shocking, and the house is old, and the chimneys—but I need not tell you of the chimneys. I have been thinking that you and Frederick might perhaps wish to be more in the world; and that I—that I might be no hindrance to you.”

She paused. Mrs. Ashcombe the younger looked at her with an expression of mild inquiry, and said nothing.

“I have a little income of my own,” continued the widow, with a quiver in her voice which she could not entirely command, “a very little, but sufficient. There is a cottage at Wroxeter—a pretty cottage, I am told—which might be had, I daresay, for a trifle. I should not be far from you. I should not be altogether lost to you. But I should be—I should be out of your way.”

Here she faltered, and the little dog, perceiving distress in the voice, lifted its head and whined.

Mrs. Ashcombe the younger closed her book upon her finger, and for the first time looked at her mother-in-law with an attention that was not merely civil. She was a woman of few words, but those few were usually to the purpose. “Ma’am,” said she, “do you wish to leave Delamere?”

“Wish! My dear, what has my wish to do with it? I am sensible that a young woman must have her own house, her own way. I am sensible that an old woman is a burden. I have seen it—I have seen it in a hundred little things. The chair by the fire, the window, the books—”

“The books,” said the younger lady, “are mine, and I am sorry they have offended you. They were sent down in a hurry, and the library is damp.”

“Offended me! My dear, I am not offended. I am only—I am only aware.”

There was a silence. The younger Mrs. Ashcombe regarded her book, and then her mother-in-law, and then the fire; and it was plain that she was making an effort of a kind to which she was not much accustomed. “I have a great many books,” she said at last, “and very little conversation. I daresay I have been a dull companion. If I have seemed to wish you at a distance, it was not because I wished you out of the house. It was because I wished—because I am not used to being attended to, ma’am, and it fatigues me.”

“Fatigues you!” cried the widow, and her work-basket slipped from her knee. “Oh! I have fatigued you.”

“I should not have said fatigues,” returned the other, with a faint colour. “I should have said—I am not used to it. I have lived a great deal alone. When you send for Mr. Harbottle, and order the broth, and ask after my sleep, I am very grateful; but I am also—I am also at a loss. I do not know what is expected of me. I do not know how to be grateful enough.”

Mrs. Ashcombe the elder sat very still, and the little dog, finding itself unheeded, jumped down and trotted to the window. It was a new idea to her, that her attentions might be a tax rather than a comfort; and like all new ideas, it was for a moment unwelcome. But she was a woman of good heart, and her good heart had been for eleven years in the service of her son, and now, she began to perceive, must be in the service of his wife. She picked up her work-basket with dignity.

“My dear,” said she, “I believe we have both been a little foolish. I have been afraid that you wanted me gone; and you have been afraid that I wanted you grateful. It is a great pity that we could not have said so at Michaelmas.”

The younger lady smiled—a real smile, not the civil one—and said, “I am not afraid now.”

“Then I shall not send for Mr. Harbottle,” said the widow, with an attempt at severity, “unless you are truly ill; and I shall not order any more broth. And you shall sit by the window as long as you please, and catch what cold you can, and I shall not say a word. But I shall still come in of a morning,” she added, with a return of her old manner, “for I cannot be expected to give up the fire, you know, and the little dog, and the two cedars. And if you are reading, I shall bring my work and sit very quiet, and you may read to me if you choose—only if you choose, my dear—for I am not so fond of Cowper as I might be.”

“I shall read to you,” said Mrs. Ashcombe the younger, “and you may fall asleep if you like. I shall not be offended.”

“I never sleep in a morning,” said the widow, with great dignity; and then, after a pause, “I sometimes doze a little over a sermon. But Cowper—Cowper is another thing.”

And so the matter was settled, not with any formal treaty, but with the small, comfortable understandings that do more for the peace of a household than any number of professions; and Mrs. Ashcombe the elder went to her own room to write a letter to her sister, in which she said that she had been quite mistaken in her daughter-in-law, who was a very sensible woman, and that she had no intention of removing to Wroxeter, the cottage being damp, and the neighbourhood, she was told, very indifferent.
