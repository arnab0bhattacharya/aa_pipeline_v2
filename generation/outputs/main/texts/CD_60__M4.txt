The town of Cinderhaugh, in the West Riding of Yorkshire, was a town that had been built, as it were, in a single convulsion of nature, and had never quite settled afterwards. It stood in a hollow, and the smoke of its chimneys had, by long perseverance, persuaded the very daylight to take on a yellowish-brown complexion, as though the sun itself were a grudging creditor who paid his dues in worn and tarnished coin. The river ran black through the middle of it, not from any natural depravity, but because the mills had long since taught it to run so; and the houses climbed the hillside in tiers, like a ragged gallery of spectators who had come to see the river do its worst and had stayed to cough. There was a church, a market-cross, a Mechanics’ Institute, four public-houses, and a great many little shops that sold everything and seemed to sell nothing; and over all these presided, in the manner of a minor deity who had taken a wrong turning, the mill of Messrs. Talloward and Sons, Flax-Spinners and Manufacturers, whose tall chimney was the first thing a traveller saw and the last thing he forgot.

Mr. Ambrose Talloward was a man of sixty, with a face like a well-scoured trencher—flat, broad, and shining—and a habit of rubbing his hands together as though he were perpetually washing them of something that would not come off. He was a strictly religious man, and had a pew at the front of the church, and a voice in the vestry that was heard above the rest, and a way of saying “the Lord has prospered us” at the annual dinner of the Spinners’ Benevolent Society that made the younger men look at their plates. He had two sons, both of whom he had placed in the business and neither of whom he trusted with the keys; and a foreman named Caleb Rook, who had been in the mill these thirty years, and who knew every thread and every spindle and every man and woman in the place, and who was, in the phrase of the town, “the mainspring of the concern, and Talloward the case.”

Now it happened, on a certain night in the October of eighteen hundred and fifty-two, that the mill of Messrs. Talloward and Sons took fire. The discovery was made by a boy named Tim Gubbins, who had been sent to sweep the carding-room, and who, having swept it, sat down upon a bale of flax to eat an apple and to meditate upon the injustice of the world, when he observed a smell. The smell was not the ordinary smell of the mill, which was a compound of oil and dust and damp wool and the perspiration of three hundred human beings; it was a sharper, drier, more arrogant smell, and it came from beneath the door of the drying-room. Tim Gubbins, who was ten years old and had never in his life been believed, went and told the watchman, who told him to be off; and then the smell became a crackle, and the crackle became a roar, and the roar became a thing that could be heard at the market-cross, and by that time it was too late to tell anybody anything.

The engines were stopped, the hands were turned out, the fire-engines were dragged up the hill by horses that had never been so frightened in their lives, and the whole town stood in the street and watched the mill of Talloward and Sons burn. It burned with a horrible cheerfulness, as though it had been waiting for the opportunity; the windows went off one after another like a feu-de-joie; the great chimney stood up black and bare against the red sky, and the smoke rolled down the hollow and filled the church and the public-houses and the Mechanics’ Institute with the smell of ruin. Three hundred hands stood in the wet street and watched the thing that fed them turn into a heap of charred beams and twisted iron; and the women cried, and the men said nothing, and the children asked questions that nobody answered.

But there was one man who did not stand idle. Caleb Rook, the foreman, went into the counting-house through the smoke, with his coat over his head, and came out again with the ledgers and the order-book and the letter-book under his arm, and his eyebrows singed off, and his lungs so full of smoke that he sat upon the kerb and coughed for a quarter of an hour, while the young Mr. Talloward (the elder of the two sons) stood by and said, “Well done, Rook, well done, that’s the spirit,” in a voice that suggested he was congratulating a dog that had brought back a stick from the water.

The mill was insured. Mr. Ambrose Talloward, standing upon the hill with his hands behind him and his chin sunk upon his breast, observed to the town at large that the Lord had been pleased to visit them, and that they must bow to the rod; but that, thanks to a proper foresight, which he had always exercised in things temporal as well as spiritual, the loss would not fall upon the firm. He said this with a certain unction, and the town, which had seen him exercise his foresight in the matter of wages and the price of meal and the rent of the cottages, received it in silence.

It was Caleb Rook who discovered the rest. He was sitting in his own little house in Back Lane, three days after the fire, with his hands upon his knees, looking at nothing in particular, when his wife put down her sewing and said, “Caleb, there’s a thing I cannot make out.”

“What thing?” said Caleb.

“The insurance,” said Mrs. Rook. “It was only took out a week before the fire. Betty at the post-office told me. She saw the letter go, and she saw the man from the office come up to value, and she says it was all done in a hurry, as if the house was going to be sold.”

Caleb Rook sat still for a long time. He was a slow man, and he had a respect for his master that had grown with him for thirty years, like a tree round a wall; and it is not easy to pull down a tree that has been thirty years a-growing. But he got up at last, and put on his hat, and went out.

He went to the office of the insurance company’s agent, a little man named Pinfold, who had a desk and a stool and a brass plate, and who received him with the air of a man who is not at home to inconvenient questions. Caleb Rook asked when the policy on the mill had been effected. Mr. Pinfold said it was not his habit to discuss the affairs of his clients. Caleb Rook asked whether it was a week before the fire. Mr. Pinfold said that the policy had been effected in the regular way, and that if Mr. Rook had any complaint to make, there was a proper quarter in which to make it. Caleb Rook asked whether the mill had been valued at its full worth, or at a worth that would leave a margin for rebuilding elsewhere. Mr. Pinfold rose from his stool, and said that he did not know what Mr. Rook meant, and that he was a busy man, and that he wished him good morning.

But Caleb Rook was not to be put off so. He went to the elder Mr. Talloward, and stood before him in the dismantled counting-house, with his hat in his hand, and said, “Sir, I have been in your service thirty years, and I have never asked you a question yet that was not about the work. But there is a thing I must ask you now. Was the mill insured only a week before the fire?”

Mr. Ambrose Talloward looked at him with a face like a well-scoured trencher, and said, “Rook, you forget yourself.”

“I hope not, sir,” said Caleb. “I hope I remember myself, and my duty to the hands, and to the town, and to God. Was it insured only a week before the fire?”

“And if it was,” said Mr. Talloward, “what then?”

“Then, sir,” said Caleb Rook, “I must ask you why.”

Mr. Talloward rubbed his hands together, as though he were washing them, and said, “Rook, you are a good servant, and a useful one, and I should be sorry to part with you. But you are not a man of business. The ways of business are not the ways of the workshop. A man insures his mill when it suits him to insure it, and not before. I am not answerable to you for the date of my policy.”

“No, sir,” said Caleb Rook. “But you are answerable to the hands, and to the town, and to God.”

“I am answerable to my Maker,” said Mr. Talloward, with great solemnity, “and to my own conscience, and to no one else. And I will tell you what I have told no one else, Rook, because you have been a faithful servant, and because I would not have you go about the town with a wrong impression. I intend to rebuild. But I intend to rebuild elsewhere. The site is not healthy. The smoke lies in the hollow. The river is foul. I have had my eye for some time upon a piece of land at Bradley Bottoms, where there is a fall of water and a clean air, and where the hands will be better housed and better taught. I intend to rebuild there, and I intend that you shall go with me.”

Caleb Rook stood very still. “And the three hundred hands here, sir?”

“They will follow the mill,” said Mr. Talloward, “if they are wise. If they are not wise, they will stop here and starve, and that is their affair.”

“And the town, sir?”

“The town,” said Mr. Talloward, “will do what it has always done—it will get on.”

Caleb Rook went out of the counting-house and down the hill, and stood upon the bridge, and looked at the black river and the charred chimney and the three hundred hands who were standing about the street with nothing to do. He was a slow man, and he had a respect for his master; but the tree had been pulled down at last, and he saw the wall it had been growing against, and the wall was not a wall but a painted board. He took off his hat and wiped his forehead, and said, aloud, “Well, I have saved the ledgers, and the ledgers will save the truth.”

And the truth was this: that the policy had been effected on the eleventh of October, and the fire had broken out on the eighteenth; that the mill had been valued at a sum which would not only rebuild it at Bradley Bottoms, but would leave a handsome surplus in the pocket of Mr. Ambrose Talloward; and that the three hundred hands, who had made the fortune of Talloward and Sons with their labour and their lungs and their lives, were to be left in the hollow, with the black river and the charred chimney and the smoke that never lifted. And when Caleb Rook brought the ledgers into the light, and the town read what was written there, there was a great indignation in Cinderhaugh, and a public meeting, and a subscription, and a speech from the Mechanics’ Institute; and Mr. Ambrose Talloward, who had been a strictly religious man, found that the Lord had prospered him no longer, and went to live at Bradley Bottoms, where there was a fall of water and a clean air, and where, it is to be hoped, he washed his hands of the whole affair.
