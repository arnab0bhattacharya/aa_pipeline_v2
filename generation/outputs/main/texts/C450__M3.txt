# The Bayswater Parcel

The parcel arrived on a Tuesday in March, wrapped in brown paper that smelled of tar and distance. It had been routed through three forwarding agents, its outer surface so smudged with handling that the address was nearly illegible. Only the surname remained clear: Challen.

Eustace Challen set the parcel on the sideboard in the dining room of his Bayswater house and studied it for a long while before opening it. His wife, Hester, watched from the doorway. She had learned not to ask questions of her husband since his return from the Victorian goldfields two years prior. He had gone out a quiet man with modest prospects and come back wealthy, but the wealth had settled on him like armour, and the quietness had become something closer to silence.

Inside the parcel was a nugget of gold the size of a walnut, rough and yellow, wrapped in a scrap of canvas. And on the canvas, in faded ink, four letters: W.D.E.H.

Eustace held the scrap for a long time. His face did something Hester had not seen it do since his return — it moved. A twitch beneath the left eye, a tightening of the jaw, and then a stillness that frightened her more than any outburst could have.

He folded the canvas and put it in his breast pocket. He set the nugget on the sideboard beside the parcel paper. Then he went to his study and did not come out for the rest of the evening.

Their daughter, Clara, learned of the parcel the following morning when she found the nugget sitting unattended on the sideboard. She was twenty-three and had inherited her father's caution without his secrecy, which made her, in her own estimation, the more practical of the two. She picked up the nugget, felt its surprising weight, and carried it to her mother in the morning room.

Hester told her what she knew, which was little. The parcel, the canvas, the initials. Her father's refusal to speak of any of it.

Clara went to the study and knocked. Her father's voice granted her entry. He was seated at his desk, but no papers lay before him. His hands were flat on the blotter, fingers spread, as though bracing himself against something.

"Father," she said. "What does it mean?"

"It means nothing you need concern yourself with."

"Gold and a threat do not arrive together without meaning something."

He looked up at her then, and she saw that his eyes were bloodshot, that he had not slept. "It is not a threat."

"Then what is it?"

"A debt," he said. "A very old one. And not yours to pay."

He asked her to leave, and she did, because she had been raised to obey, but she stood in the hallway afterward and listened to the sound of his desk drawer opening and closing, and she heard the distinct click of a revolver's cylinder being checked.

Eustace began carrying the revolver that same day. It was a Webley, short-barrelled, and he wore it in a leather holster beneath his coat. Clara saw the outline of it when he came down to breakfast on Wednesday, and again on Thursday when he went out to his club. She mentioned it to her mother, who pressed her lips together and said nothing useful.

By the end of the week, the household had adjusted to this new strangeness. Eustace went about his business — visiting his solicitors, dining at his club, walking in Hyde Park each afternoon — but the revolver went with him everywhere, and the scrap of canvas stayed in his breast pocket. Clara caught him once, late at night, standing at the window of the front parlour, looking out at the street. The gas lamps cast their light across the wet pavement, and in their glow she could see the face of a man who was waiting for something he did not wish to come.

It was on the following Thursday that he failed to return from his walk.

He had left at half past three, as was his custom, heading west toward Kensington Gardens. When six o'clock came and went without his appearance, Hester sent a footman to look for him. The footman returned an hour later, having found nothing. Clara went herself, walking the routes she knew her father favoured — along the Serpentine, through the Flower Walk, past the Round Pond — but the evening was cold and the park was emptying, and she saw no sign of him.

She returned home after nine and found her mother in a state of restrained collapse, seated upright in a chair in the drawing room, her hands gripping the armrests as though the house might shake her loose.

Clara did not wait for morning. She sent a note to a private inquiry agent whose name she had obtained from the husband of a friend, a man named Aisworth who operated from offices on Chancery Lane. She described her father, his habits, his recent behaviour, and the parcel. She did not mention the initials. Some instincts held even in crisis.

Aisworth came at eight the next morning. He was a lean man in his forties, dressed in dark wool, with the careful manner of someone accustomed to entering households where trouble had recently arrived. He listened to Clara's account without interrupting, and when she finished, he asked two questions. The first was whether her father had any connections to the Australian goldfields beyond his own time there. She said she did not know. The second was whether she had the parcel paper. She produced it from the sideboard drawer where she had placed it.

Aisworth studied the paper for several minutes. He turned it over, held it to the light, and examined the postmark and the forwarding labels. Then he asked a third question: whether her father had ever mentioned a place called Dargo, or a man named Whitley.

Clara said he had not.

Aisworth folded the paper carefully and returned it to her. He said he would begin at the forwarding agent listed on the outer label, and that he would report back before evening. He asked whether the revolver had been taken. Clara confirmed that it had. He nodded and left.

He returned at seven with news that was neither good nor bad but merely strange. The forwarding agent, a firm in Wapping that handled colonial freight, had received the parcel six weeks prior from a station near Dargo in Gippsland, Victoria. The sender's name on the manifest was Whitley, D. The agent recalled the parcel because it was unusual to receive gold through the post, and because the instructions had been specific: hold for collection by hand, and if not collected within one month, forward to the London address.

"Whitley, D," Clara said. "The initials were W.D.E.H."

"Yes," said Aisworth. "Four initials. A sender with two. The parcel may have passed through other hands."

"What is Dargo?"

"A settlement. Gold was found there in the sixties, in the river. It was rough country."

"My father was in Ballarat."

"Men moved between fields."

Clara sat with this for a moment. "Do you believe he has gone there? Back to Australia?"

"No," said Aisworth. "I believe someone has come here."

He explained. The parcel had been held at the forwarding agent for a full month before being sent on, which suggested the sender had expected someone to collect it in person. When no one did, it was posted to the only address provided. This meant the sender had expected the recipient — or someone associated with the four initials — to be in London already, or arriving shortly.

"You think the person who sent the gold is here," Clara said.

"I think your father believed so. The revolver suggests as much."

Aisworth left again and did not return that evening. At half past ten, Clara received a telegram: FOUND YOUR FATHER STOP HE IS ALIVE STOP COME TO 14 RANELAGH ROAD STOP DO NOT BRING YOUR MOTHER.

She went alone in a hansom, the nugget of gold wrapped in a handkerchief in her pocket. Number 14 Ranelagh Road was a boarding house in a terrace of similar buildings, each with white stoops and black railings. Aisworth met her at the door.

"He is upstairs," he said. "He is not injured, but he is not well."

Clara found her father seated in a chair beside a gas grate in a narrow room. The revolver lay on the bed beside him. Across from him, in the room's only other chair, sat a woman of perhaps fifty, thin, weathered, dressed in dark travelling clothes. Her hands were folded in her lap, and she watched Eustace with an expression that Clara could not immediately read — not hostility, not grief, but something between accusation and exhaustion.

Eustace looked up at his daughter. His face was grey.

"Clara," he said. "This is Mrs. Whitley. Her husband is dead. He was my partner at Dargo, before Ballarat. The initials on the canvas were his. William David Eustace Halloran Whitley. He used all of them, because there were too many men named William in the fields and he did not trust the keeping of records."

Clara looked at the woman, who had not moved. "What is the debt?" she asked.

Her father did not answer. But Mrs. Whitley did, in a voice flattened by distance and use.

"The debt," she said, "is a life. My husband found the gold. Your father registered the claim. When the claim was disputed, your father testified that the gold was his alone, and David was sent away. David died at sea, returning to try again. The nugget in your pocket is the last of what he found. He sent it to remind your father of what he owes."

The room was quiet. The gas hissed in the grate.

Clara looked at her father, who sat in his chair with the weight of the goldfields still settled on him, and she understood finally that the silence he had carried home from Australia was not the absence of speech but the presence of something he could not bring himself to say. She took the nugget from her pocket and set it on the table between the two chairs.

"Tell me what you intend to do," she said to Mrs. Whitley, and her father did not interrupt, because the time for his silence had passed, and they all three knew it.
