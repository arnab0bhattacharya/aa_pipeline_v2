Mr. Josiah Pumphrey, of the firm of Tottlebury, Tottlebury, and Grubb, Solicitors, of Lincoln’s Inn Fields, was a man of fifty-eight years, of a spare and upright figure, who had worn the same suit of black for the last twelve years (not, indeed, the identical cloth, but a succession of faithful copies of the original pattern), and who had, for the space of thirty years, copied deeds, wills, and affidavits in a small, neat, clerkly hand, so very small and so very neat that it might have been executed by a machine, had any machine been capable of the profound and awful patience which was the leading characteristic of the man. He had never in that time been absent from his desk on a single occasion, whether of sickness or of pleasure; and he had never once been late, though the fog had been so thick that the very link-boys had lost their way, and the rain had fallen in such torrents that the Thames had seemed to be coming up the Strand to pay a morning call upon the lawyers.

His only relaxation—if relaxation it could be called, which consisted in a tender and absorbing anxiety—was a canary-bird, which hung in a little cage by the window of his room in Pentonville, and which he had taught to whistle a tune that his sister, Miss Pumphrey, declared to be ‘The Last Rose of Summer,’ though the bird itself might have entertained a different opinion. To this bird Mr. Pumphrey would speak in terms of the most endearing endearment, and would sometimes, when the office was closed and the gas was lit, and the great clock of the Inn was tolling the hour, sit with his pen in his hand, and his head on one side, listening to its little twittering song with a face of such mild and melancholy satisfaction as might have belonged to a man who heard the music of a better world.

It was upon a certain afternoon in the month of November, when the fog had been so dense that the lamps in the Fields were lighted at noon, and the very cats had gone about with an air of apology, that Mr. Pumphrey was summoned into the private room of Mr. Grubb, the surviving partner of the firm. Mr. Grubb was a portly gentleman, with a face like a full moon in a state of indigestion, and a manner which he intended to be kind, but which had the effect of making everybody who came near him wish to run away. He had a way of putting his thumbs into the arm-holes of his waistcoat, and rocking himself to and fro in his chair, which Mr. Pumphrey had long since learned to regard as the precursor of some announcement of importance.

‘Pumphrey,’ said Mr. Grubb, ‘you have been with us a long time.’

‘Thirty years, sir,’ replied Mr. Pumphrey, with a little bow, ‘come the third of April next.’

‘Exactly,’ said Mr. Grubb, rocking himself more violently than before. ‘And you have been a faithful servant, Pumphrey—a very faithful servant. I have no fault to find with you—none whatever. But the fact is, Pumphrey, the times are changing—the times are changing. We live in an age of progress—of steam, Pumphrey, and of telegraphs, and of—of—’ Here Mr. Grubb, who was not a man of very ready invention, waved his hand vaguely, as if the age of progress were a thing that might be caught by a dexterous movement. ‘And we have thought it advisable—my partner and myself—to—to infuse a little younger blood into the establishment. We have engaged a young gentleman, Pumphrey, who writes a very fine flowing hand—a very fine flowing hand indeed—and who has, moreover, a cousin in the office of the Stamp Office, which may be of use to us—of great use to us. And so, Pumphrey, we have decided—with the greatest regret, I assure you—to—to—in short, to dispense with your services from the end of the quarter.’

Mr. Pumphrey stood perfectly still. The little room, with its red curtains and its blazing fire, and Mr. Grubb rocking in his chair like a ship in a storm, seemed to be turning round and round, and then to be going away from him, very slowly, like a thing seen in a dream. He opened his lips once or twice, but no sound came from them. He thought of the thirty years—of the thousands of deeds he had copied—of the cold mornings when he had risen in the dark—of the summer days when he had not dared to look out of the window—of the canary-bird, and of his sister, and of the little house in Pentonville, and of the rent which was due on the quarter-day.

‘I am very sorry, Pumphrey,’ said Mr. Grubb, who began to be a little frightened at his silence, ‘very sorry indeed. But you will have your pension—a small pension—and the young gentleman will be here on Monday. You will have the goodness to show him the—the—the method of the office, and to—to—in short, to make him acquainted with the duties.’

‘Yes, sir,’ said Mr. Pumphrey, in a voice that sounded like the voice of somebody else, a long way off. ‘I will show him the duties, sir. Is there anything else, sir?’

‘Nothing else, Pumphrey—nothing else. Good afternoon, Pumphrey—good afternoon.’

Mr. Pumphrey went back to his own room, and sat down at his desk, and took up his pen. But the pen shook in his hand, and the words danced before his eyes, and he laid it down again, and looked at the canary-bird, which was hopping about its cage, and twittering with all its might, as if it knew that something was the matter, and wished to comfort him. And then he put on his hat and his great-coat, and went out into Lincoln’s Inn Fields, where the fog was so thick that he could scarcely see the lamp-posts, and where the people passed him like shadows, and the sound of their footsteps was muffled and strange.

He walked all the way to Pentonville, though it was a long walk, and though the omnibuses went by him with their lamps shining like eyes in the fog, and the drivers shouting to him to get out of the way. He walked slowly, with his hands behind him, and his head bent down, and he did not notice that the rain was beginning to fall, or that the wind was blowing coldly upon his face. He was thinking of the little house in Pentonville, and of his sister, who was a widow, and who kept the house, and who was the only creature in the world (besides the canary) that he had to love. He was thinking of the little parlour with its horsehair sofa, and its portrait of the late Mr. Pumphrey (who had been a grocer, and had died of a complication of disorders, brought on by a too liberal indulgence in his own raisins), and of the little garden with its one tree, and of the tea-things set out upon the table, and of the canary-bird’s cage hanging in the window, and of the peaceful, quiet, monotonous life that had gone on for thirty years, and that was now to be broken up for ever.

When he reached the door of his house, he stopped and put his hand upon the knocker, and then took it away again, and stood looking at the little brass plate, with ‘Pumphrey’ upon it, as if he had never seen it before. He could not go in and tell her. He could not say, ‘Susan, I am turned off—turned off at fifty-eight, after thirty years—and we must go and live in a garret, and sell the furniture, and the canary-bird will have to be given away, for we cannot afford to keep him.’ He could not say that. And so he stood upon the doorstep, in the rain, with the fog all round him, and his heart beating so loud that he wondered the people in the street did not hear it.

At last he opened the door with his latch-key, and went in. His sister was in the parlour, with the tea-things set out upon the table, and the fire burning brightly, and the canary-bird singing in the window. She looked up as he came in, and said, ‘Why, Josiah, you are late to-night! And you are wet through, I declare! Whatever have you been thinking of, to walk home in such weather as this?’

Mr. Pumphrey sat down in his chair by the fire, and looked at her, and tried to speak, but could not. He thought of the thirty years, and of the little pension, and of the young gentleman with the flowing hand, and of the cousin in the Stamp Office, and the words seemed to stick in his throat, and to choke him. He put his hand up to his neck, and pulled at his cravat, and then he said, in a strange, husky voice, ‘Susan, my dear, I—I—the fact is, I—I am a little tired to-night. I think I will go to bed.’

And he went up to his little room, and sat down on the edge of the bed, and looked at the canary-bird, which he had brought up with him, cage and all, and which was now singing away as merrily as if there were no such things as Tottleburys or Grubbs in the world. And the tears came into his eyes, and rolled down his cheeks, and he said, ‘Poor little fellow! poor little fellow! I wonder what will become of you?’ And then he thought of his sister, and of the tea-things downstairs, and of the thirty years, and he covered his face with his hands, and cried as if his heart would break.
