The Khurlik Depression was, as Hetherington put it to himself for the hundredth time, an insult to geology and a personal affront to any man of sensibility. It was a vast, shallow bowl of blistered ochre and grey, rimmed by the jagged, skeletal remains of the Tien Shan foothills, and it possessed a climate of such systematic cruelty that it seemed less a natural feature than a deliberate, malicious invention. For three months, Hetherington and his small party had been chipping away at its western escarpment, a process he described in his weekly letters to the Royal Society as "patient stratigraphical analysis," but which in reality amounted to little more than a stubborn, sun-baked argument with the planet.

His sponsor, the fabulously wealthy and almost equally secretive Sir Humphrey Croft, had funded the expedition on the peculiar condition of absolute discretion. "No telegrams, Hetherington. No announcements. Not a whisper until I have seen the material with my own eyes," Croft had commanded, his voice crackling down the telephone line from London with the assurance of a man who owned half the city's power stations. It was a strange request for a man of science to accept, but Croft's money was good, and the promise of a fully equipped expedition to this forgotten corner of Asia was a lure Hetherington, in his slightly threadbare academic position, could not resist.

The find, when it came, was not a moment of thunderous revelation, but one of profound and creeping bewilderment. It began with a tooth. It was a molar, but its shape was all wrong for any mammal Hetherington knew of in the fossil record of the Oligocene strata they were excavating. It suggested an omnivore, certainly, but one with a dental arrangement that was frankly, undeniably, *peculiar*. Then came a femur, unearthed by his young assistant, a keen but perpetually dusty man named Abercrombie. "Looks rather like a man's, sir," Abercrombie had said, holding it up. "But the angle of the head... it's not right. And the size. Too big for a hominid of this age, surely?"

Hetherington agreed. It was not right. None of it was right. Over the next week, in a fever of quiet, methodical work, they assembled the skeleton in the makeshift tent that served as their laboratory. It was a bipedal creature, standing perhaps five feet high, with a ribcage broader and flatter than a man's, and a pelvis that spoke of a different, more efficient gait. The skull was the final, shocking element. It was not a human skull. The braincase was large, but the brow was heavy, the face prognathic, and the dentition was the same strange, bear-like omnivore's set they had first found. It had walked upright, this thing. It had stood on two legs and surveyed its world with a brain of considerable size, and it had done so, according to every principle of stratigraphy Hetherington trusted, twenty million years before the first ape-like ancestor of man had ever thought to leave the trees.

"We've broken it, sir," Abercrombie whispered one evening, as they stared at the articulated bones laid out on a canvas sheet. "We've broken the whole timeline."

But the true vertigo, the moment the world seemed to tilt on its axis, came with the tools. They were found in a tight cluster near the creature's right hand, preserved in a pocket of fine, wind-blown sand. They were not crude flakes of flint or chipped river stones. They were fashioned from a dense, dark horn-like material, perhaps from some other extinct beast. There was a scraper, its edge honed to a wicked sharpness. There was a pointed awl, for piercing hide. And there was a larger, heavier implement, a kind of hand-axe, its form so perfectly balanced and functional that it looked less like a primitive tool and more like an object of deliberate, sophisticated design. To hold it was to feel an echo of the intelligence that had shaped it—an intelligence utterly alien to the human story.

Hetherington knew, with a certainty that chilled him more than the desert's nocturnal cold, that this discovery was bigger than Darwin, bigger than Lyell. It was not an addition to the known history of life; it was a contradiction of it. It suggested a parallel, a forgotten, a *failed* experiment in intelligent life that had run its course and vanished, leaving only these silent bones and their impossible tools.

He immediately composed a long, cautious, but nonetheless thrilling telegram to Sir Humphrey Croft. The reply, when it finally arrived via the circuitous and slow-moving telegraph office three days' ride away, was short and utterly baffling.

REPEAT STOP. NOTHING TO BE COMMUNICATED STOP. AWAIT MY ARRIVAL STOP. CROFT.

Hetherington read the message three times. *Repeat?* Was the man mad? Did he think it was a joke? The arrival of Sir Humphrey himself, two weeks later, did little to clarify matters. He was a man of immense physical presence, with a face like a clenched fist and eyes that missed nothing. He did not waste time on pleasantries. He walked directly to the tent, his polished boots kicking up little puffs of dust, and stood over the skeleton in a silence that was more unnerving than any shout.

"My God," he said, finally. It was not an exclamation of scientific wonder, but of grim, dawning confirmation. He picked up the hand-axe, weighing it in his palm as if he knew its heft already.

"It is, as you see, an astonishing assemblage," Hetherington began, his voice regaining its academic confidence. "The morphology suggests a bipedal hominid, but one entirely outside our current understanding. The tools indicate a level of cognitive ability—"

"I know what they indicate, Hetherington," Croft cut him off, his voice flat and hard. "I know precisely what they indicate. That is the problem."

"The problem, Sir Humphrey? This is the solution! To a dozen questions we didn't even know to ask! This will be the greatest find of the century!"

"No," Croft said, turning to face him. The sunlight filtering through the canvas threw his face into a mask of harsh shadows. "It will be the end of the century. And the next one. Do you have any conception, any at all, of the social and theological chaos this would unleash? To tell mankind that we are not the first? That we are not unique? That a thinking, tool-making race rose and fell on this Earth before us, and left no trace but this?" He gestured dismissively at the skeleton. "The churches would call it an abomination. The atheists would call it proof of a meaningless cosmos. The politicians would find a dozen new reasons to go to war over it. Humanity is not ready for this, Hetherington. It is a truth that would shatter the world."

Hetherington stared at him, aghast. "But... but it's the truth! It is a fact of nature! We have a duty—"

"We have a duty to posterity," Croft snapped, his voice rising with a sudden, terrifying passion. "And that duty is to protect it from a truth it cannot possibly digest. This is not a discovery, Professor. It is a bomb. And I will not allow you to detonate it."

"But the science..."

"Science is a game for men with the leisure to play it. The rest of the world runs on faith, and order, and simple, necessary fictions. This," he said, tapping the skull with one thick finger, "would destroy them all."

He reached into his coat and produced a leather wallet. "I will double the grant. Triple it. You will return to England with me. You will publish a paper on the stratigraphical anomalies of the Khurlik Depression. You will report that you found nothing of significance. The bones and these... implements... will be crated and shipped to my private collection. They will be safe. They will be studied, in time. But not by the world."

Hetherington felt a wave of nausea. It was a betrayal of everything he stood for. It was intellectual cowardice on a planetary scale. "And if I refuse?"

Sir Humphrey Croft smiled, a thin, humourless expression that did not reach his eyes. "Then I will report to the Royal Society and the press that the desert heat has quite turned your head. I will say you have become obsessed with a fantasy, that you are fabricating evidence from the bones of bears and extinct apes. I will ruin you, Hetherington. Utterly and completely. You will be a laughingstock. And then, I will take the specimens anyway."

Hetherington looked from the impassive, terrible face of his sponsor to the strange, noble skull of the creature on the canvas. He saw the high, inhuman brow and the empty eye sockets that seemed to stare back at him across an abyss of twenty million years. It was a silent, fossilised plea for remembrance. And he knew, with a sickening certainty, that he was going to fail it. The desert wind moaned outside the tent, a long, lonely sound, like the sigh of a world forgetting its own dead.
