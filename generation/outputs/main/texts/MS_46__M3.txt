The ruin of my felicity was consummated on the eve of that day which was to have beheld me the wife of Lord Edric; and I was left to drag out the heavy hours in a solitude rendered sacred by despair. We had established our retreat at Thornfield, a solitary house in the wildest part of the Peak District, where the rugged mountains and the sweeping storms seemed formed to be the congenial companions of a heart as desolate as mine. I had loved him with the entire devotion of a soul whose capacities for feeling were deep and overflowing; and when he departed, casting me from him with a cold, unfeeling abruptness, the shock was as the snapping of the main-spring of a watch—the whole machine of my existence was shattered, and the wheels stood still. I shut myself from the world; the face of man was hateful to me; and in the dim, wainscoted chamber of our mountain-home, I passed the mournful days and nights, feeding my grief, and refusing to be comforted.

It was in vain that my young brother, Ferdinand, a boy of fourteen, whose lively spirits and glowing cheek formed a bitter contrast to my wan and faded looks, endeavored to rouse me from this lethargy of woe. He would stand by my couch, and with an eloquence far beyond his years, paint the beauties of the morning sun upon the crags, and the soft mist sleeping on the valleys; but I turned from him with a heavy sigh, and answered only by tears. I had no compassion on myself, and none to bestow on the living world. The dead, the past, were my portion; and I clung to my misery as the only legacy left me by him who had caused it.

But Ferdinand was not to be repelled. His love for me was unbounded; and with a gentle violence, a mingled sway of authority and tenderness, he compelled me to leave my chamber. Each morning, ere the sun had climbed the eastern hill, he would enter my room, draw aside the curtains, and bid me arise and breathe the fresh air of the mountains. "Come, Clara," he would say, "the sky is cloudless; the linnets are singing on the gorse; and the dew lies thick on the heather. You must not mope here, and wear away your life in idle lamentation. Come, and I will show you a cataract that I have discovered, leaping from a precipice three hundred feet in height, and flinging its spray upon the rocks below, till they shine like diamonds in the sun."

His voice, so sweet and yet so commanding, awoke a faint thrill of pleasure in my torpid heart; and I suffered him to lead me forth. We walked together over the moors, and climbed the steep ascents of the hills. The scenery around us was of the wildest and most romantic character. Vast masses of limestone rock, piled in fantastic shapes, frowned upon us from the summits; while below, the river Dove pursued its tortuous course through a narrow valley, its waters swollen by the recent rains, and rushing with a sullen roar beneath the overarching crags. The wind swept across the moorland with a mournful sound, and the clouds, gathering in dark masses on the horizon, seemed to portend a storm.

As we ascended, Ferdinand kept close to my side, now pointing out the rare flowers that grew among the stones, now relating some legend of the country, and now bursting into a strain of youthful merriment, which, in spite of my sadness, forced a smile upon my lips. He was indeed a dear, noble boy; and as I looked upon his bright, intelligent face, and marked the ardor of his glances, and the graceful energy of his movements, I felt that I was not utterly forsaken—that I had still a treasure to preserve, a being to love and to protect.

We reached the summit of the hill, and sat down upon a mossy stone. The view was extensive and sublime. On one side, the richly-wooded valley of the Dove, with its winding stream and scattered cottages; on the other, the bleak and barren moors, stretching away to the horizon, unbroken by a single tree or habitation. The sun was setting, and his level beams cast a golden glow upon the landscape, tipping the summits of the hills with fire, and illuminating the mists that hung in the hollows with a purple light. It was a scene calculated to impress the mind with solemn and elevated thoughts; and as I gazed, the burden of my sorrow was lightened, and I felt a soothing influence steal over my spirits, as if the great mother of us all were offering me her consolation.

"Ferdinand," I said, "this is indeed beautiful. I had forgotten that such beauty existed; and yet it is fit that the earth should be fair, even though I am miserable. But tell me, my dear boy, what is the object of your bringing me here? You have some motive, I know, beyond the mere love of nature."

He looked at me with a grave and earnest expression, and said, "Clara, I have a motive. I wish to show you that there is still a world worth living for, and that you have not yet exhausted the gifts of fortune. You have suffered, I know; but you are young, and have many years before you. Do not waste them in idle repining. Look upon that sun, as he sinks behind the hills; he will rise again to-morrow, as bright and glorious as to-day; and so may your spirits rise, if you will but rouse yourself, and shake off this deadly lethargy. You have friends who love you; you have a brother who would lay down his life for you. Come back to us, Clara; be our companion, our guide, our sister; and let the past be buried in oblivion."

He spoke with an energy and pathos that touched me to the heart. The tears gushed from my eyes; and I threw my arms around his neck, and wept upon his bosom. The frozen current of my grief was thawed; the long-suppressed tide of emotion flowed forth; and I felt, in the genial warmth of that embrace, that I was not wholly miserable. The love of this gentle, noble boy was a talisman to call me back to life; and as I looked upon his face, irradiated by the setting sun, I felt that there was still a charm in existence, and that I might yet be happy.

We descended the hill in silence; but it was the silence of content, not of despair. The storm which had been gathering, now broke over our heads; the thunder rolled in heavy peals among the mountains, and the lightning flashed from cloud to cloud. But I felt no terror; the tempest without was in unison with the tumult within; and as the rain descended in torrents, and the wind swept by with a hollow moan, I lifted my head, and breasted the storm with a newly-aroused courage. The next morning, Ferdinand again led me to the hills; and from that day, the dead weight of my sorrow was gradually removed; the current of my life resumed its course; and though the scar remained, the wound was healed. I had learned the lesson which nature teaches to her children: that sorrow is the lot of man, but that it is by the endurance of sorrow that the soul is strengthened and purified.
