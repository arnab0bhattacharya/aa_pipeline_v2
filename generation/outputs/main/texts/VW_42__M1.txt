It began, as so much begins, in a haze of steam and the clatter of cups. Four girls in white dresses, their hair pinned with varying degrees of success, sat in the garden of Miss Granger’s school for young ladies, and the future was a blank, un-ruled page upon which they could write anything at all. Eleanor, who had the prettiest hands, poured the tea. It was her party. And it was she who first named the thing they all felt but could not say. “I shall never forget this,” she said, and her voice was as light as the steam rising from the teapot. “This feeling. That we are all of one piece, somehow.”

Katherine, whose mind was always a step ahead, a flicker of calculation behind her clear grey eyes, nodded. “Of course,” she said, as if Eleanor had merely stated the obvious. “We shall be friends for life. We shall have a pact.” She looked at Margaret, who was staring at a ladybird making its laborious way across the white tablecloth. Margaret’s silence was not emptiness, but a deep, absorbing quiet, a kind of still water into which everything fell. She felt the truth of Eleanor’s words not as a statement but as a warmth in her chest. And then there was Iris, who laughed. “A pact! How thrilling! What shall we swear on? The Bible? A hairpin?” Her laughter was a bright, spilling thing, and it made the sun-dappled leaves of the lime tree seem to tremble. They were four distinct notes in a chord, and for that one afternoon, under the drowsy hum of bees, the chord was perfect. The future was a country they would travel to together, its borders drawn in tea-leaves and sunlight.

The wedding was a different kind of light. It was the flat, even light of a spring morning in London, falling through the tall windows of a church and illuminating the dust motes in a solemn, swirling dance. Eleanor was the bride, of course. It had always been Eleanor. She was a blur of white lace and orange blossom, a creature made of joy and terror, her face a pale oval beneath the veil. Katherine, as bridesmaid, was a column of severe, practical blue, her attention already on the reception, on the arrangement of the flowers, on the logistics of the day. She was already, Eleanor felt with a sudden, sharp pang, somewhere else. Margaret stood in the pew, her hands folded in her lap. She watched Eleanor’s face and felt the moment as if it were happening to her own body—the solemnity of the vows, the finality of the ring sliding onto a finger. She was not losing a friend, not exactly, but a shared world was closing its doors. Iris, beside her, was a splash of inappropriate colour in a hat like a crushed flower. She fidgeted, she smiled, she caught the eye of a handsome usher, and Eleanor, looking out from the altar, saw not a sisterhood but three separate women, each sealed in the glass of her own life. The chord had broken. It had not shattered; it had simply resolved into its constituent parts, and they would never be played together in quite the same way again.

Years passed, and they gathered again, this time in the grey, damp air of the country. Rain fell on the black umbrellas, a steady, percussive drumming on the silk that seemed to measure out the silence. It was Margaret’s funeral. She had been the quiet one, the still water, and it seemed impossible that she should be the first to leave them. Katherine arrived by train, her coat still smelling of coal smoke, her face set in lines of efficient sorrow. She had organised the flowers, spoken to the rector, ensured everything was done properly. Iris came late, her hat askew, her eyes red-rimmed, bringing with her a gust of wind and a scent of damp fur. She wept openly, unashamedly, a great, shuddering grief that was almost a performance. And Eleanor stood between them, feeling nothing but a hollow ache, a vast and echoing emptiness where Margaret’s quiet presence used to be. They looked at the polished wood of the coffin and saw not an end, but a terrifying new beginning. The pact they had made in the garden was not about living forever; it was about the deaths they would witness. They were no longer girls sharing a future, but women sharing a past, a past that was now irrevocably, painfully diminished. They were the remaining notes, and the silence between them was larger than any sound.

The last time, it was an accident. A meeting of chance in the great, iron-vaulted cavern of Paddington Station. Eleanor, older now, her hands spotted with age, was waiting for a train to the coast. The air was thick with the smells of steam, oil, and boiled beef. And there, by a bookstall, was Iris. The years had been kinder to her energy than to her features; she was still a splash of colour, but the colours were faded, the edges blurred. “Eleanor!” she cried, and the sound was lost in the shriek of a whistle. They clung to each other, two old women in a river of rushing people. There was so much to say and nothing at all. They spoke of Katherine, who had written a stern, sensible letter last Christmas; of Margaret, whose name was now a soft ache; of children and grandchildren and ailments. But the real conversation was in their eyes. They saw the girl in the white dress, the bridesmaid in blue, the grieving friend in black, all layered one upon the other like old photographs. The station roared around them, a testament to journeys, to arrivals and departures, and they were two fixed points in the swirling chaos. The tea party had been a beginning, the wedding a parting, the funeral an ending. But this—this was the whole of it. This sudden, fleeting recognition of a shared life, seen in the reflection of a station window, before the whistle blew again and Iris was swept away, waving, her hand a pale flag in the smoke-filled air. Eleanor was left alone on the platform, holding the warmth of that brief contact, the ghost of a chord struck once more, and then dissolved into the noise of the world.
