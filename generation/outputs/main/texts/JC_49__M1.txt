There was a moment, I think, when the whole world of that room—the smell of wet wool, of bad tobacco, of the damp plaster of the walls—seemed to contract to a single point of light in the speaker’s eyes. His name was Yefimov, and he spoke not with the voice of a man but with the voice of a conviction, a deep, resonant instrument that seemed to draw its power from the very chill of the Zurich winter pressing against the windowpanes. He spoke of Russia, a land that was not a country but a wound, a vast, bleeding expanse where the people slept a heavy, drugged sleep under the weight of centuries. His words were not arguments; they were revelations, falling upon us in the crowded, smoke-filled room like a sudden, cleansing rain upon a parched and dusty field.

We were a motley collection, the flotsam and jetsam of a great political storm, gathered in a Swiss city that offered us the sanctuary of indifference. There were students like myself, Nikolai, and others; there were old men who had been exiled before I was born; there were women with hard, bitter faces and eyes that had seen the inside of the Peter and Paul Fortress. And in the midst of this human wreckage, Yefimov stood as a prophet, a figure of absolute certainty. I, who had come to this city to study the cold, verifiable truths of the human body, to learn the mechanics of life and death from textbooks and cadavers, found myself utterly defenceless against the terrible poetry of his vision. He offered not science, but a faith. He spoke of a future that would be purged of injustice, a dawn that would break over the plains of our homeland with the force of a physical cataclysm. And I, Varvara Petrovna, felt my heart, a young and foolish organ, beat in time with the rhythm of his terrible, beautiful hope.

I was aware of Nikolai beside me. He was always beside me, it seemed, a quiet and constant presence in the lecture halls of the medical faculty. He was a Pole, and carried his nationality like a subtle, persistent limp. His face, pale and finely drawn, was a study in restraint, his hands, which could tie a surgeon’s knot with unnerving precision, were usually still, folded in his lap. He did not listen to Yefimov; he observed him. Where I saw a leader, Nikolai saw a performance. Where I heard truth, he heard a dangerous and seductive lie. He did not argue with me in the passionate, foolish way of young people. His opposition was a matter of silence, a slight tightening of his lips, a gaze that would drift from the speaker to the grimy window, as if seeking a more honest truth in the reflection of the gaslight on the wet cobblestones outside.

After one such meeting, when the air was thick with the fervour of the newly converted, he walked me back to my lodgings along the Limmat. The river was a black, sluggish serpent coiling through the sleeping city. The silence between us was more eloquent than any speech.

“His words are a drug,” Nikolai said at last, his voice low and without inflection. “They offer the comfort of a grand design to those who feel themselves to be small and meaningless. It is a very potent remedy.”

I bristled, the warmth of the meeting still a fire in my blood. “And what do you offer, Nikolai Antonovitch? The cold comfort of anatomy? The certainty of a suture?”

“I offer nothing,” he said, stopping and turning to face me. The gaslight from a nearby bridge threw his face into sharp relief, carving shadows beneath his cheekbones. “I only warn. Men like Yefimov build their cathedrals of the future on foundations of human bone. They speak of the people, of the masses, as if they were a single, pure entity. But a mass is only a collection of individuals, each with their own soul, their own weakness. He would sacrifice them all for his idea. He would call it a necessary holocaust.”

His words, so quiet and reasonable, felt like an insult. They were the words of a man who had been broken by history, who had lost the capacity for faith. And a small, ugly thought, which I was ashamed of even as it formed, crept into my mind. It was jealousy. It was the envy of the quiet, the cautious, the intellectually proud, for the man who could command the hearts of others. Nikolai, with his careful hands and his guarded heart, could not compete with the fire of a man like Yefimov. He saw in that fire not a promise, but a rival.

“You are afraid of him,” I said, my voice colder than I intended. “You are afraid of his power.”

A strange look passed over his face—a flicker of something that might have been pity. “Yes,” he admitted, with a disarming honesty that only served to anger me more. “I am afraid of men who are certain of everything. They are the most dangerous of all.”

He did not speak of it again. Our friendship, which had been a comfortable and unspoken thing, built on shared lectures and the quiet companionship of the library, now had a shadow at its heart. I would see him watch me across the room when Yefimov was holding forth, his gaze not accusatory, but deeply, profoundly sad. It was a look that followed me, a silent question that I refused to answer. I began to see him as a man standing on the bank of a great river, warning me against the current that I was so desperate to enter. And like all who are warned, I felt only the stronger pull of the water.

The final meeting I attended was in a cellar beneath a bakery, the air thick with the smell of yeast and revolution. Yefimov was at his most magnificent. He spoke of the coming struggle, of the necessity of action, of the iron will required to remake the world. He was no longer a poet but a general marshalling his troops. His eyes swept over us, and I felt myself to be not Varvara Petrovna, a student of medicine from a provincial town, but a part of something immense and historic. When he finished, there was a silence that was not empty but full, heavy with unspoken oaths.

I felt a light touch on my arm. It was Nikolai. He did not look at me, but at the speaker, who was now surrounded by his acolytes, a new messiah receiving his disciples.

“Look at him,” he whispered, his voice barely audible. “He does not see us. He sees only the reflection of his own grandeur in our eyes. We are not people to him. We are fuel.”

He turned and looked at me then, and in his eyes I saw no jealousy, no petty rivalry. I saw only a vast and terrible understanding, the knowledge of a man who had seen this before, in other faces, in other lands, and who knew, with the certainty of the surgeon who has opened a body and found the disease beyond all cure, how it must end. In that moment, the heat drained out of me, leaving a cold, hollow space. The great and glorious future Yefimov had painted suddenly seemed thin, a brittle fresco painted over a wall of stone. And I understood that Nikolai’s warning was not for Yefimov, but for me. It was the last, quiet act of a man who knew he was already too late. The silence of the cellar pressed in, no longer full of promise, but of a profound and unutterable loss. The river, I knew, had already taken me.
