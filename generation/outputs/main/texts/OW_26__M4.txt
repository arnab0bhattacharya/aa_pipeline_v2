The morning of the nuptials of Miss Angelica Vandeleur dawned with a splendour that was almost insolent, and found Mr. Alaric Quinton—a gentleman whose entire philosophy of life might be said to have been buttoned into his coat—standing before his cheval-glass in a state of profound and unaccustomed agitation. His costume was a miracle of restrained elegance, a frock-coat of so deep a blue that it trembled upon the verge of black, a waistcoat the colour of pale champagne, and a cravat whose knot was a triumph of geometric art. Yet his eye, which was accustomed to dwell with satisfaction upon such perfections, passed over them all with a look of tragedy, for it rested, with a kind of horror, upon the empty buttonhole of his lapel.

The flower was gone. The single, perfect carnation, of a yellow so pale that it seemed to have been woven from moonlight and lemons, which had been his companion for the last seven years, his signature, his silent epigram upon the world, was no more. It had, he was forced to admit, been a little weary of late, its petals edged with the faintest premonition of decay; and he had placed it with his own hands in a glass of water upon his dressing-table the night before, intending to replace it in the morning with a fresh one from the same establishment in the Burlington Arcade. But the morning, with a malice peculiar to Sunday, had brought with it the discovery that the establishment, like all others of its kind, was shuttered and barred, its windows a blank and mocking stare. A flower, on the Sabbath, was a thing more precious than a ruby, more unattainable than a virtuous woman.

“It is the irony of the gods,” he murmured to his reflection, which looked back at him, pale and sympathetic. “To be perfect in every detail but the one that matters most. I am like a sonnet with the final couplet missing.”

He summoned his valet, a sombre man named Pettigrew, who had the face of a disappointed clergyman.

“Pettigrew,” said Mr. Quinton, with the calm of despair, “I am without a flower.”

“Indeed, sir,” replied Pettigrew, whose manner was always that of one receiving news of a minor bereavement. “I had observed it. Most unfortunate, sir.”

“Unfortunate is a word for a wet afternoon. This is a catastrophe. A wedding, Pettigrew, is a ceremony of flowers. To arrive without one is to be a guest at a feast who has brought no appetite. It is to be a voice in a choir that is merely speaking. What are my resources?”

“The conservatory, sir, is quite denuded of bloom. The season has been unkind. And the hour is early.”

“Then I must go forth,” declared Mr. Quinton, with the air of a general who has decided upon a forlorn hope. “I must scour the city. A carnation, Pettigrew, a single yellow carnation, is all I ask of London.”

His journey began in the decorous silence of Mayfair. The streets were a desert of polished stone, swept clean and untenanted save for the occasional hansom, which rattled past like a black beetle with a gentleman inside it. He walked with a brisk, determined step, his eyes scanning the facades of houses for any sign of floral life. He saw window-boxes, it is true, but they contained only geraniums, which he considered vulgar, and ivy, which he considered a vegetable. He saw a solitary gardener, but the man was engaged in the melancholy task of raking leaves, and when Mr. Quinton inquired after a carnation, he merely touched his cap and said, “Sunday, sir,” as though that single word contained the whole of the Decalogue and the entirety of the British Constitution.

He turned his steps towards Covent Garden, that fragrant temple of the dawn, hoping to find some lingering spirit of the market. But the great piazza was a scene of desolate cleanliness. The stalls were folded, the cobblestones were washed and glistening, and the only perfume that lingered was the ghost of a thousand crushed roses, a scent that seemed to him now the very breath of his own failure. A solitary barrow, pushed by a man with a face like a withered apple, offered a few bunches of wilting violets and a cabbage. Mr. Quinton looked at the cabbage and felt that his soul was in sympathy with it.

“My good man,” he said, “I am in search of a carnation.”

The man stared. “A what, sir?”

“A carnation. A flower. A thing of beauty. It is not a cabbage.”

“Never ’eard of it,” the man said, and trundled his barrow away, leaving Mr. Quinton to the contemplation of a city that had suddenly revealed itself as a vast and heartless emporium of cabbages.

He pressed on, into the Strand, where the churches were emptying their congregations onto the pavements. He passed ladies in silks and gentlemen in top hats, all of them, he was convinced, wearing flowers. He saw a young girl with a primrose, a matron with a spray of orchids, a bishop with a rose that was a positive scandal of horticultural pride. Each one was a dagger in his heart. He felt like a man who has lost his shadow, or his sense of humour, and is forced to watch the world go by, complete and self-possessed.

It was in a narrow lane off the Seven Dials, a place of curious shops and forgotten alleys, that his desperate eye caught a splash of colour. It was a small, grimy window, and behind it, in a chipped jar, stood a single flower. It was a carnation, of a yellow so pale that it seemed to have been woven from moonlight and lemons. Mr. Quinton’s heart gave a leap. He tried the door; it was locked. He knocked. A shuffling sound, and the door was opened by an ancient woman with a face like a crumpled paper bag.

“I must have that carnation,” he said, his voice trembling with a sincerity that surprised him.

The old woman looked at the flower, then at him. “It’s not for sale,” she said.

“Everything is for sale,” he replied, “except, perhaps, a good reputation. I will give you a sovereign for it.”

“It’s not for sale,” she repeated. “It was given to me by my son, who is dead.”

Mr. Quinton was silent for a moment. The world, which had seemed so trivial, had suddenly become very serious. He looked at the old woman, at the flower, and then at his own empty buttonhole. He thought of the wedding, of the assembled company, of the importance of a single, perfect detail. And then he thought of the dead son.

“Madam,” he said, with a bow that was entirely without irony, “I apologise for having disturbed you. Your flower is in its proper place.”

He turned and walked away, leaving the old woman staring after him in astonishment. The sun was now high in the heavens, and the bells of a hundred churches were ringing a joyful, discordant peal. Mr. Quinton walked on, his lapel still empty, but his step was lighter. He had discovered a truth more valuable than any flower: that a buttonhole, however perfect, is but a decoration, while a gesture, however small, is a thing of substance.

He arrived at the church just as the bride was entering. He slipped into a pew at the back, his empty buttonhole a small, private badge of honour. And when Miss Vandeleur, radiant and triumphant, passed him on her father’s arm, she paused for a moment, looked at his lapel, and then at his face, and smiled. It was a smile that said she understood, and that she forgave him. And Mr. Quinton, who had spent the morning in a fever of vanity, felt for the first time that day that he was, in truth, perfectly dressed.
