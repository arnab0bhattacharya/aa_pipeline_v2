The fog that morning hung over London like a damp shroud, turning the gas lamps of Cheyne Walk into ghostly orbs of yellow light. I had just reached the door of Mr. Archibald Vane’s townhouse, a respectable residence in a part of the city where wealth often masqueraded as respectability, when the butler, a man of formidable aspect, admitted me with a bow that suggested he would have preferred to keep the door barred.

Mr. Vane was seated by the fire, a glass of brandy in his hand, his face pale and drawn. He was a man of fifty, with the crisp, precise manner of one who had commanded men and businesses alike, but his eyes held a tremor that spoke of a very different experience.

"You are the man who was recommended to me," he said, his voice low and urgent. "They say you are thorough, Mr. Holmes. I am not interested in your methods. I am interested in a result. I have a debt of life that I cannot pay because I do not know to whom I owe it."

I drew my chair closer to the grate. "You seem agitated, Mr. Vane. Pray, tell me the facts."

"Last Tuesday," Vane began, leaning forward, "I was crossing the bridge near the railway arches. I am not a light man, and my heart is not what it used to be. I had barely stepped onto the stone parapet when the deck gave way beneath me. I fell into the black, rushing water below. I am a poor swimmer, and the cold seized me instantly. I was struggling, sputtering, convinced that my end was at hand, when a hand shot out from the gloom and seized my collar.

"I was hauled up onto the bridge by a man of medium stature. He was dressed in a grey coat and a soft cap. He did not speak. He simply looked at me, his face obscured by the shadow of his cap, and then he turned and walked away into the mist. I had no time to thank him. I had no time even to catch his name. I went home, shook like a leaf, and have not slept a wink since."

"An act of great courage," I remarked. "But why the urgency? Why the fear?"

Vane’s hand tightened on his glass. "Because I have tried to find him. I have offered a reward of five hundred pounds for any information leading to his identity. I have hired private detectives. And yet, every clue leads to a contradiction. Two days ago, a woman on the Embankment saw a man matching his description buying a newspaper. At the same hour, a clerk in the City reported seeing the same man entering a bank in Threadneedle Street. They are two miles apart. It is impossible."

"Impossible, yes," I said, "unless the man is a phantom, or unless your witnesses are mistaken. Have you checked the witnesses?"

"Thoroughly. They are credible. And there is another matter. Last night, I received a letter. It contained no signature, only a single sentence: 'You have not thanked me, Sir. The debt remains unpaid.' There was a smell of river water about the paper."

I felt a chill that had little to do with the London fog. A threat wrapped in gratitude is a peculiar thing. I asked Vane for a description of the man’s face.

"His features were sharp," Vane said. "There was a scar, a thin white line, running from his left ear to the corner of his mouth. He had dark eyes, very deep-set. And he had a habit of tapping his finger against his temple when he thought."

"Is it possible," I asked, "that this man has a reason to be near you? Did you recognize him?"

Vane shook his head violently. "No. I swear to you, I did not. But I have a feeling, a premonition, that this man is not a stranger. He is a shadow from my past, and he has come to collect."

I left Vane in a state of heightened anxiety and returned to my rooms in Baker Street with a sense of unease. The case of the man in the grey coat was not a simple matter of identification. It was a puzzle of physics and psychology. How could a man be in two places at once?

The next day, I visited the police station nearest the bridge. The records showed no missing persons, no reports of a man falling into the river, and no complaints of theft. The city’s memory was short, and the river’s secrets were long.

I went to the street where the woman had seen the man. She was a laundress, a woman of sharp eyes and sharper tongue.

"He was there," she insisted, pointing to the corner. "Buying the *Times*. He paid with a silver coin. I saw it glint. He had that scar, yes. And he tapped his head like that."

I walked to Threadneedle Street. The clerk was a nervous young man, his hands trembling.

"He was in the bank," he whispered. "He stood at the counter. He did not speak. He just looked at me. I felt a coldness in my stomach. He had the same coat. The same cap."

I stood in the street, looking up at the grey sky. The two accounts were distinct, yet the details were identical. The same man, the same time, the same mannerisms. It was a paradox that defied logic.

I returned to Vane’s house that evening. The philanthropist was pacing the room, his face ashen.

"I have made a decision," he said. "I will go to the police. I will tell them everything. I will name the man."

"You cannot name a man you do not know," I said.

"I don't need to know his name," Vane said, his voice dropping to a whisper. "I know who he is. I have been a fool, Mr. Holmes. I have been blind to the truth because I did not wish to see it. The man who saved me... he is my brother."

I stared at him. "Your brother?"

"Thomas," Vane said. "We were estranged. He ran off with a woman I despised. He was a drunkard, a gambler, a disgrace. He died, or so I was told, three years ago. But I have not seen his body. I have not seen his grave. And now, this letter. This impossible man."

"Are you certain?" I asked. "The scar? The habit?"

Vane nodded. "Thomas had a scar. And he had a habit of tapping his head when he was thinking. It was a nervous tic."

The pieces began to fit, but not in the way I had expected. If Thomas Vane was alive, and if he had been seen in two places at once, then the explanation was not supernatural, but human.

"Mr. Vane," I said, "if your brother is alive, why would he save your life? And why would he vanish?"

"I do not know," Vane said. "But I am afraid, Mr. Holmes. I am afraid that he is not saving me. I am afraid that he is waiting for me to be alone. The letter said the debt remains unpaid. What is the price of a life?"

I thought of the man in the grey coat. I thought of the two witnesses. I realized then that the man had not been in two places at once. He had been in one place, and the witnesses had been deceived. The man in the grey coat was a decoy.

"Who else knew about the accident?" I asked.

"Only my butler," Vane said. "He was the one who took me home."

I looked at the butler, who was standing in the corner, his face impassive. "And the letter, Mr. Vane? Who delivered it?"

"I do not know. It was in my mailbox."

"Then we must look at the mailbox," I said. "And we must look at the butler."

I called for the police. As they arrived, I examined the letter again. The smell of river water was strong. But there was another scent, faint but distinct. It was the scent of old tobacco, of pipe clay. It was the scent of a man who had been standing by a fire.

I looked at Vane. "You said you did not recognize the man. But you said he had dark eyes. Are you certain they were dark?"

Vane hesitated. "Yes. Very dark."

"Did he have a beard?"

"No. He was clean-shaven."

"Your brother," I said slowly, "had a beard, did he not?"

Vane’s face went white. "Yes. A thick, dark beard. He always had it."

"Then the man who saved you was not your brother," I said. "And the man who sent the letter was not your brother. Someone is using your brother’s identity. Someone who knows your family, knows your fears, and knows how to manipulate your guilt."

Vane sank into his chair. "Who?"

"Someone who has a reason to want you to believe your brother is alive," I said. "Someone who wants you to be afraid. Because a man who is afraid makes mistakes. And a man who makes mistakes can be ruined."

I looked at the butler. "And you, Mr. Blackwood? Where were you on Tuesday night?"

The butler’s face changed. The impassive mask cracked, revealing a flash of fear. "I was in the kitchen, Sir."

"Then I suggest you let the police verify that," I said.

As they led the butler away, Vane turned to me, his eyes wide with a new kind of terror. "Who is doing this, Mr. Holmes? Who is behind this?"

I looked out the window, where the fog was beginning to lift, revealing the cold, hard lines of London.

"Someone who has been watching you for a long time," I said. "And someone who is very good at disappearing."
