The message arrived not on paper, but in the mouths of two sweating porters who had run the last twenty miles of the riverbank, their feet bloody, their eyes wide with the fever of their errand. They stood in the shaded veranda of the station, gasping out the words of the Company’s coastal manager, words that had been passed from steamship to telegraph office to the lips of a dozen runners until they were worn smooth and simple as river stones.

Abandon Station Kivu. Return to the coast. Immediate.

Alistair Finch listened, his own breath held tight in his chest. He gave the men water and a few brass coins, then sent them to the cook-fire. The silence they left behind was heavier than the humid air that pressed down on the tin roof of his bungalow. He looked out from the veranda, past the neat rows of the Company’s storehouse and the dusty yard where the porters’ huts stood, to the great, slow curve of the Lulonga River. It was a brown, muscular serpent, coiling through an endless green world. For three years, this world had been his.

He had come to Station Kivu with ink-stained fingers and a head full of ledgers and regulations. He was a Company man, a cog in the great machine of commerce and expansion. But the machine was a distant thing here. The rhythm of his life was set by the sun, the rains, and the tide of the river. He knew the smell of the red laterite dust after a storm, the particular cry of the fish eagles at dawn, the weight of a well-balanced rifle in his hands. He had learned the local tongue, a language of clicks and soft vowels, and could barter for yams and palm oil with the best of them. He had built this place from a handful of mud-and-wattle huts into a functioning outpost, a fragile speck of order in the vast, breathing wilderness.

He walked the length of the veranda, his worn leather boots thudding on the planks. The order was clear. The steamship *Marie-Galante* was due at the bend in two days. It would take him downriver, back to the brick and cholera of Boma, back to starched collars and the endless, petty politics of the coastal administration. The thought left a taste of ash in his mouth.

A shadow fell across the doorway. Alistair didn’t need to turn to know who it was. The man moved with a silence that belied his size.

“Msimu,” Alistair said, turning.

Chief Msimu filled the frame of the doorway. He was a man built of old hardwood and river clay, his face a roadmap of seasons, his eyes holding the deep, placid wisdom of the forest. For three years, Msimu’s protection had been the station’s true currency. His warriors kept the raiding tribes to the north at bay; his people traded fairly, and his word was law in this stretch of the Lulonga. He had saved Alistair’s life during the fever last year, sending his own wives to nurse him with bitter root poultices when the Company’s quinine ran out.

“They say the water-spirit has sent a message to the coast,” Msimu said, his voice a low rumble. His gaze was fixed on Alistair’s face, missing nothing. “The porters run like frightened guinea fowl. The spirits of the station are troubled.”

Alistair sighed, rubbing the back of his neck where the sweat gathered. “It is not a water-spirit, Msimu. It is the Company. They are… closing the station. I am ordered to leave.”

He said the words plainly, as he would report a change in the price of ivory. But they felt like a betrayal as they left his lips.

Msimu did not move. His expression was unreadable, carved from the same ancient wood as the forest itself. He stepped into the bungalow’s shade, his bare feet making no sound. He looked at the maps on the wall, the ledgers on the desk, the rifle propped in the corner. He was looking at the bones of Alistair’s life.

“Leave,” he repeated, the word foreign and heavy on his tongue. “And the station? The storehouse with its cloth and its iron? The path to the river? What becomes of these things?”

“They will be left. Or perhaps dismantled. It is not my concern. My orders are to return to the coast.”

“The orders of the men with soft hands who have never seen the Lulonga when it is angry?” Msimu’s voice was still calm, but a new current ran beneath it. “We have a pact, you and I. My people protect your people. You trade with us fairly. You bring the medicine that stops the shaking sickness. This station is a fixed point. It is a rock in the river. When the water rises, we know where to cling.”

He took a step closer, his large, calloused hand resting on the edge of the desk. “When the Nkosi raiders came last season, it was your fire-sticks and my spears that turned them back. You are part of the strength of this place now, White Man. The people say it. The spirits of the river know it. If you leave, the rock will be gone. The water will wash us all away.”

Alistair felt a tightening in his throat. He saw the faces of the children who traded berries for beads, the old woman who mended the station’s nets, the young men who stood guard with him on the palisade. He saw Msimu’s trust, a thing more valuable than all the ivory and rubber the Company could ever extract. He had a duty to the Company. It was written in his contract, sealed with their corporate stamp. But he had another duty, unwritten and profound, to this place and its people.

“The Company is my chief, Msimu. Just as you are the chief of your people. I must follow his orders.”

“Your chief is a shadow,” Msimu said, his eyes narrowing. “A name on a paper. I am here. My people are here. We are flesh and blood. We are the ones who would mourn you if the river took you. Would this shadow of a chief do the same? Or would he simply write another name on his paper?”

The question struck Alistair with the force of a physical blow. He thought of the coastal manager, Mr. Davies, a man whose face he could barely recall. He imagined Davies at his desk in Boma, a fan turning lazily overhead, signing the order without a second thought. Station Kivu was a line item, an expense to be cut. Alistair Finch was another.

He looked from Msimu’s intense, pleading gaze to the river, shimmering in the afternoon heat. It flowed on, indifferent to the orders of men, whether they were chiefs in the forest or clerks on the coast. It would be there tomorrow, and the day after. But would he?

His loyalty to the Company was an abstract thing, a matter of contract and career. His loyalty to Msimu, to the station, to the life he had built here, was immediate, tangible, and felt in his bones. He had come here to be an agent of the Company, but he had become an agent of this place.

Msimu watched him, waiting. The silence stretched, filled only by the drone of insects and the distant cry of the fish eagle. Alistair knew that whatever he chose, a part of him would be broken. To obey the order was to betray the man who had saved his life and the community that had claimed him. To stay was to defy the power that gave him his purpose and his name.

He walked slowly to his desk. He picked up the quill pen, its nib still wet with ink from the morning’s accounts. He looked at the ledger, at the neat columns of debits and credits that represented his entire existence to the Company. Then he looked at Msimu. The chief’s face was patient, ancient, waiting for the tide to turn.

Alistair dipped the pen in the inkwell. He turned to a fresh page in the ledger. His hand was steady. He was a Company man. But he was also a man of Station Kivu. And he had to decide which of those men would walk out the door. The pen hovered over the page, a tiny, dark promise of a future yet to be written.
