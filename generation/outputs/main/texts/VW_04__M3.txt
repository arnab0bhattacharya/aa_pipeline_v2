She mounted with that little skip which girls from the country have, as if the step were higher than she had judged, and the conductor, pulling the bell, noticed nothing in particular except that she held her purse awkwardly, fumbling, and that her gloves were the sort that are bought in a market town, the leather already creased across the knuckles, and that she looked up at him when she gave her pennies with eyes that expected, absurdly, some form of recognition, as if she were still in a shop where one says good morning. He did not say good morning. He tore the ticket and turned to the next body pressing up the stairs, and she went and sat downstairs by the window where the rain streaked the glass and the houses of Bayswater went by in a yellow wash that blurred and ran, so that she might have been crying, though she was not crying, only pressing her forehead to the cold pane and thinking how odd it was that the trees in the park had no leaves, not a single leaf, and yet one knew they were alive, that the leaves were in there somewhere, folded up, waiting, and she thought of the elm at the gate at home, how it would be standing now in the November dark, and her mother’s lamp in the window, and the dog at the hearth, and all the little solid things that made a life—milk on the step, the postman’s bicycle bell on the lane—while here she was in this great jostling vehicle, carried forward, and nobody knew her name, and the conductor called out the stops in a voice that seemed to grind the very air into small hard pellets of sound, Kensington, Gloucester Road, and the wheels ground too, and she thought, pressing closer to the glass, that if one could only hold on to something, a railing, a hand—but there was nothing to hold; only the window, slipping with rain, and the street pouring past.

Mrs. Almond, who kept the hostel in Pembridge Square, saw her come in at the door and thought, as she always thought when she saw one of them, how young they were, how defenceless, standing in the hall with the key on its string, and the postcards pinned to the board, and the smell of cabbage and boot-polish that no amount of violet powder could quite subdue, because the house was old, and old houses have their own smell, their own memories, rather like people, only people are more resilient, or so Mrs. Almond found, having buried a husband and weathered a son who had gone to Canada and wrote once a year on a picture postcard of a mountain; and she thought, watching this girl—Catherine, was it? Cathcart—hang her wet coat on the peg with that careful exactness that speaks of someone who has been taught to fold, how her mother must have packed her trunk, folding each thing, pressing it flat, and how the trunk had come on the train and she had met it, and opened it, and the things had smelled of lavender and of home, and she had laid them in the drawer and thought, this is London now, this drawer is mine, and the pillow, and the strip of carpet, and the water jug with the chip on the lip—and Mrs. Almond felt a movement of something not quite pity, because pity was too easy, too thin, but something heavier, a kind of recognition, as if she saw her own young self standing there, only thinner, only more lost, because in her day at least there had been God, and the service on Sunday, and one knew where one was, whereas now they did not know, they went to offices and typed letters and came home and sat on their beds and stared at the wall, and the wall gave them nothing, and Mrs. Almond, turning back to her ledger, thought that she would ask the girl to tea, and then forgot.

In the office, which was in a street off the Strand and smelled faintly of gas and damp paper and the coats of men who came in wet from the street, Philip Marlow, who was a junior clerk and wrote figures in columns, watched her type. She typed with two fingers, striking the keys with a force that seemed almost personal, as if each letter were an obstacle to be overcome, and the little machine jumped and chattered under her hands, and he thought, watching the back of her neck where the hair was pinned up in a coil that was already loosening, that she was not very good at it, that she would get faster, or she would not, and either way it did not matter, because the world went on, and the letters went out, and the rain came down, and the buses ground past, and she would sit here typing, or she would sit somewhere else typing, and the letters would be about orders and invoices and nothing that mattered, and he thought how strange it was that one could sit three feet from a person and know nothing, absolutely nothing, of what went on inside that skull, behind that forehead, that she might be thinking of a man, or of God, or of nothing at all, only the next letter, and the next, and the bell that rang at one o’clock, and he wanted to say something, to lean across and say, the rain has stopped, or, do you like London, or simply, hello, but the words that one speaks are so meagre, so brittle, compared with the vast interior life that goes on and on beneath them, like a river underground, and so he said nothing, and she gathered her papers, and the day ended, and another began, and the rain came again.

And in Yorkshire, in the kitchen that smelled of the wash-house and of bread, her mother read the letter by the lamp, holding it close, because her eyes were not what they had been, and the words swam a little, and she read them twice, and then a third time, and the letter said that she was settling in, that the work was not hard, that she had seen the Tower and the river, and that the hostel was comfortable, and the words were the words that a daughter writes to a mother when she does not wish her to worry, and the mother, folding the sheet along its creases, knew this, knew that behind the words was a life she could not see, a life that was going forward without her, in a great city she had never visited, among people she would never meet, and she felt the distance like a hand pressed against her chest, not painful exactly, but heavy, the heaviness of things that cannot be altered, and she put the letter in the drawer where she kept the others, and the lamp burned, and the dog sighed at the hearth, and the elm at the gate stood in the dark with its leaves folded up inside it, waiting.
