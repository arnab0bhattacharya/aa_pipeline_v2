It is a peculiar thing, perhaps, to reflect upon the sheer volume of human folly that must pass through the ground-floor windows of a respectable boarding house in Clapham. I am a widow, and my establishment, though I say it myself, is a thoroughly respectable one. I cater exclusively to young men in the commercial line—clerks, junior agents, and those connected with the more mechanical sorts of engineering. I have found that a landlady, if she possesses any degree of sense, becomes a sort of human repository for the discarded secrets of the male sex. They bring their troubles to the breakfast table, scattering them among the toast and marmalade, and I dare say I have listened to more romantic distress and financial panic than your average parish priest.

The trouble, as it often does in households of this sort, began with a young woman. She was a typist at the District Surveyor’s office, a Miss Mabel Vane, and she possessed that particular sort of sharp, neat prettiness that plays havoc with the arithmetic of a junior clerk’s salary. Two of my lodgers fell prey to her simultaneously, and with the most tremendous earnestness.

The first was Mr. Penrith, a tall, pale young man with spectacles, who worked for a firm of stockbrokers in the City. He was of an argumentative, idealistic turn, given to reading paper-bound novels of advanced social thought, and he considered his attachment to Miss Vane a thing of tragic, cosmic significance. The second was Mr. Bott, a stout, red-faced youth from the hardware trade, who possessed all the sentimental subtlety of a traction engine. 

They courted her in their respective fashions. Mr. Penrith wrote her letters full of evolutionary philosophy and the future of the race, which he would read aloud to me in the parlour, pacing the rug with his hands in his pockets, while Mr. Bott bought her enormous boxes of chocolate creams and paper flowers, and spoke of her to the other lodgers in the passage as "a little bit of all right."

"I tell you what it is, Mrs. Gamp," said Mr. Bott, stopping me on the stairs one evening, "that girl is the only decent thing in the whole dashed Surveyor's office. But I'm blowed if Penrith isn't always hanging about, talking a lot of rotten tosh about Plato and the rights of women. She don't want to hear that. She wants a man who knows the value of money."

"My dear Mr. Bott," I said, "Miss Vane is quite capable of making up her own mind."

"She is," he agreed, stoutly. "And her mind is me."

Mr. Penrith, on the other hand, was sunk in a gloomy, speculative despair. "She is the victim of bourgeois materialism, Mrs. Gamp," he said to me, his spectacles gleaming in the gaslight. "Bott appeals to the lower instincts. He offers her sweets. He offers her a velvet bonnet. I offer her a comradeship of intellects. But how can I compete with a man who earns three pounds a week?"

I might have laughed at them both, had not another, far more serious matter intruded upon my attention. 

My third floor back lodger was a Mr. Thorne, a quiet, furtive little man who kept to himself and worked in the counting-house of a wholesale chemist in Lambeth. He was always remarkably precise about his rent, which should have been a warning to me. The quiet ones, who never grumble about the gas bill and always wipe their boots, are the ones who are concealing something.

One Thursday afternoon, I was in the basement, boiling a pudding, when I heard a violent ringing at the front door. I went up, wiping my hands on my apron, and opened it to a large, square-jawed man in a bowler hat, who pushed past me without ceremony.

"Thorne?" he said. "Albert Thorne. Upstairs?"

"He is at his business," I said, with some asperity. "What do you mean by coming in like this?"

"Police," said the man, flashing a badge. "We've got a warrant. Embezzlement. Four hundred pounds from the chemist. He's been cooking the ledgers for a year."

I was so astonished I could only stare. The detective, or whatever he was, went bounding up the stairs, and I followed, my heart in my mouth. We found Mr. Thorne in his room. He had not gone to his business at all. He was sitting on the edge of his bed, cramming a handful of sovereigns into a canvas bag, and when he saw the detective, he let out a sort of gasp and dropped the bag. The sovereigns rolled all over the floor.

It was a most distressing scene. Thorne was arrested, searched, and marched out of the house, while my other lodgers, attracted by the noise, appeared on the landing in various states of undress. Mr. Bott was in his shirt-sleeves, and Mr. Penrith had a towel round his neck. They watched Thorne being taken away with a kind of awe-struck horror.

"Four hundred pounds!" said Mr. Bott, picking up a sovereign that had rolled under the washstand. "I say, Mrs. Gamp, this is a bit thick."

"It is a great deal worse than thick, Mr. Bott," I said. "It is a disgrace to the house. I must ask you all to keep this quiet. I will not have my establishment known as a harbour for thieves."

But keeping it quiet was, of course, impossible. By evening, the whole house was in a ferment. The lodgers gathered in the dining-room, arguing about Thorne's guilt, the nature of his fraud, and the likelihood of his being transported. Mr. Penrith, true to form, attempted to turn it into a discussion of the iniquities of the capitalist system.

"It is the pressure of commercial competition," he said. "Thorne is a victim of the wage-slavery that grinds us all to powder."

"Gammon," said Mr. Bott. "He's a thief. He took the money because he wanted it. I hope they give him seven years."

They were still at it, hammer and tongs, when the parlour maid announced Miss Vane. 

She came in, looking very pretty, but rather flustered. She had heard the news, it seemed, from one of the other clerks at the Surveyor's office, and had come to offer her condolences. The effect upon my two lodgers was instantaneous. Mr. Bott swelled visibly, and Mr. Penrith drew himself up to his full height.

"I am sure, Miss Vane," said Mr. Penrith, "that you will not judge us all by the standard of Mr. Thorne. We are not all—er—opportunists."

"I don't know what you mean by opportunists," said Mr. Bott. "But I'm sure Miss Vane knows a straight chap when she sees one."

Miss Vane looked from one to the other, and then, to my astonishment, she sat down on the horsehair sofa and burst into tears.

"It's no good," she sobbed. "I can't stand it. I've got to tell you. I was going to marry Mr. Thorne."

There was a silence you could have cut with a knife. Mr. Bott's jaw dropped. Mr. Penrith went very pale.

"Marry Thorne?" said Mr. Bott. "That little—"

"He was going to set up a shop," wept Miss Vane. "He said he had saved up the money. I didn't know he'd stolen it. I thought he was so clever."

Well, that, of course, was the end of it. The romance went out of the affair like air out of a punctured balloon. Mr. Penrith went upstairs without a word, and Mr. Bott, after staring at Miss Vane for a long minute, went out and got very drunk at the public house on the corner.

I gave Miss Vane a cup of tea and sent her home in a hansom, and then I sat down in the empty dining-room and looked at the crumbs on the tablecloth. It is a strange thing, human nature. Two young men had been fighting over a woman, and the woman was in love with a thief. I suppose it is the sort of thing that happens every day, but it seemed to me, sitting there in the gaslight, to be a very poor sort of arrangement. 

The next morning, Mr. Penrith came down to breakfast as usual, but he did not speak of Miss Vane. He read his newspaper and ate his kipper. Mr. Bott appeared later, looking rather the worse for wear, and ate three eggs in silence. The house was quiet again. Thorne was gone, Miss Vane was out of the running, and life had resumed its normal, humdrum course. I cleared the table and went to order the dinner, reflecting, as I went, that the chief advantage of keeping a boarding house is that one is always too busy to be romantic oneself.
