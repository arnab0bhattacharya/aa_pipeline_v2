The silence of the library at Ashworth Hall was a living thing, thick with the dust of centuries and the patient weight of paper. For three weeks, Eleanor Vance had been its sole inhabitant, a small figure in a vast, wood-panelled room where ladders climbed to galleries of forgotten spines. Her task was to catalogue the collection for the new owner, Mr. Julian Croft, a man who had inherited the hall from a distant great-uncle and seemed to regard the place with the cheerful indifference of a man who had bought a large, inconvenient piece of furniture. He had given her a curt nod and a key, and then retreated to the modern world, leaving her alone with the ghosts.

Her work was methodical. She would take a book from the shelf, note its title, author, and date of publication in her ledger, then gently open it to check for inscriptions or bookplates. It was in the margins that the history of Ashworth truly began to bleed through. The books were not merely objects; they were confidants.

It started with the poetry. In a slim volume of Keats, bound in faded blue morocco, she found them: verses pencilled in a looping, girlish hand. They were not copies, but original compositions, shy and earnest, written beside the printed poems as if in conversation with them. *‘Ode to a Wren in the Winter Garden’* was scribbled in the space beside ‘Ode to a Nightingale’. The name ‘Clara’ appeared at the foot of a sonnet about the autumn light on the lake. Eleanor felt a prickle of intimacy. This was not the stiff, formal script of a governess, but the spontaneous outpouring of a young heart. She imagined a girl, perhaps sixteen, curled in a window seat with this very book, the afternoon sun warming the worn leather of the armchair, her pencil scratching out her own small truths in the quiet of the house.

Then came the soldier. In a stout, practical history of the Napoleonic Wars, the margins were a different country entirely. The script was a sharp, decisive cursive, the ink a faded sepia. Here were not poems, but notes. ‘Grandfather says Waterloo was won by the infantry’s feet, not the cavalry’s charge,’ one read. Another, beside a map of the Peninsular Campaign, was a simple, stark list: ‘Badajoz. The Forlorn Hope. God help them all.’ The signature was ‘Thomas’. Eleanor could feel the restless energy of the man who had written it, a young man with a keen, analytical mind, perhaps destined for Sandhurst, his thoughts turning to strategy and honour while the ink was still wet. He was writing not just for himself, but for a future he intended to shape.

The third voice was the most poignant. It belonged to a mother. In a battered copy of Culpeper’s *Complete Herbal*, tucked between entries for feverfew and yarrow, was a list written in a steady, careful hand. It was a record of childhood, told in fevers and remedies. ‘Clara, aged 5. Measles. The fever lasted three days. Dr. Albright prescribed a poultice. She cried for her doll.’ A few pages on: ‘Thomas, aged 8. A fall from the oak tree. A broken arm. He did not cry.’ And later, ‘Clara, aged 12. A cough that would not leave. Dr. Albright called it a weakness of the lungs. We have moved her to the south-facing room.’ The entries were dated, precise, and filled with a quiet, enduring terror. This was not a mother’s diary; it was a ledger of survival, a testament to the fragility of the lives she held so dear. The name ‘Eleanor Ashworth’ was written at the beginning of the list.

Eleanor began to piece them together. Eleanor, the mother. Clara, the poet. Thomas, the soldier. They were no longer just names in the margins; they were a family, their joys and sorrows layered upon the pages of their library. She would walk through the house and see it not as a collection of rooms, but as a stage. The window seat in the library was where Clara had written her poems. The long refectory table was where Thomas had pored over his maps. The south-facing room, now shuttered and cold, had been Clara’s sickroom.

One afternoon, Mr. Croft appeared in the library, startling her from her reverie. He was holding a cigarette, its smoke a blue-grey intrusion in the still air. “Making progress, Miss Vance?” he asked, his tone breezy.

“Very good progress, sir,” she replied, closing the Keats volume. “The collection is quite remarkable.”

“Is it? Good, good.” He ran a hand over a shelf of leather-bound classics, his touch casual. “Just old books to me. All a bit dreary, really. My plan is to turn this room into a billiard room. Much more practical. The books can go into storage, I suppose. Or be sold.”

Eleanor felt a cold shock, as if he had slapped her. Sell them? Put them in a dark, forgotten store? It would be like scattering the family’s bones. “They have… a history, sir,” she said, her voice tighter than she intended.

He looked at her, a flicker of mild curiosity in his eyes. “Everything has a history. Doesn’t mean it’s interesting. This place is a mausoleum. I want to live in it, not embalm myself in it.” He gestured around the room. “My great-great-uncle, or whatever he was, was a recluse. Died with no one but a valet and a housekeeper for company. The whole line was rather odd, I’m told. The boy, Thomas, got himself killed in the Boer War. The girl, Clara, died of consumption. Sad, but there you are.”

He said it all so casually, with the detached finality of a man reading a weather report. He saw the facts, the dates, the bare bones of a family tree. He did not see the girl’s shy verses or the mother’s fearful lists. He did not feel the weight of Thomas’s ambition or the chill of Clara’s long illness. He knew the story, but he did not know them. In that moment, Eleanor understood that she knew the inhabitants of Ashworth Hall far better than this man who held the deed to their home. She knew the texture of their lives, their private fears and quiet hopes, whispered into the margins of books. To him, they were a footnote; to her, they were the whole text.

“Of course, sir,” she said, her voice calm and even. “Whatever you wish.”

After he left, the silence in the library felt different. It was no longer just a peaceful quiet; it was a fragile, threatened thing. Eleanor looked at the shelves, at the thousands of spines holding their secrets. She felt a fierce, protective surge for the Ashworths. She was a stranger, a paid employee, yet she felt like their last friend, the only one left to read their stories.

She picked up the ledger and continued her work, her pencil moving with renewed purpose. She wrote not just the titles and dates, but added small, careful notes of her own. ‘Inscribed by Clara Ashworth, c. 1885.’ ‘Margin notes by Thomas Ashworth, c. 1892.’ ‘Medical notes by Eleanor Ashworth, c. 1880-1895.’ She was not just a cataloguer now. She was a curator of ghosts. She could not save the books from the billiard table or the storage crate, but she could save their stories. She would bind the Ashworths into the record itself, ensuring that whoever opened these volumes in the future would not just see a book, but would feel, for a moment, the presence of the hands that had held it. She was preserving their voices against the cheerful, hollow silence of the new age that was coming. And in the vast, dusty quiet of the library, that felt like a victory.
