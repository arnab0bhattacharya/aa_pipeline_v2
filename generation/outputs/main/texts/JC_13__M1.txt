It was the silence that he remembered most, afterwards; not the silence of the open steppe, which has a kind of pastoral dignity to it, but the close, waiting silence of the station at Zyrardów, where the very air seemed to hold its breath between the announcements. A silence filled with the smell of hot oil, wet coal, and the faint, acrid dust of Poland. For ten years, Feodor Ilyich had served the Tsar’s railways in that province, priding himself on a certain imperviousness, a professional isolation from the intricate, bitter passions of the place. He was a man of timetables and clearances, of pressures and gradients; his world was a straight line drawn across a map, and he had always held that to deviate from it, for any reason whatsoever, was a form of treason against the very logic of the machine he served.

The order, when it came, did not arrive by the usual telegraphic chattering from Warsaw, but in a sealed envelope brought by a driver, a man he knew, who would not meet his eye. The paper was heavy, the handwriting familiar and yet, in its sharp, impatient strokes, a stranger’s. It was Nikolai’s hand. His brother, Nikolai, who had last written from Geneva with a kind of desperate, intellectual fire, speaking of new dawns and the cleansing necessity of action. The note was brief. The 10:40 from Kovno, carrying a certain “consignment of state importance” to the fortress, must be held at Zyrardów. For how long? Until a signal was given. A green flare, perhaps, from the woods beyond the northern signal box. It was not a request. It was an appeal to a loyalty Feodor Ilyich had not known he possessed, a loyalty not to the state, not to the service, but to the shared memory of a mother’s face, a loyalty of the blood.

He stood at the window of his small, bare office and watched the 10:40 draw in, a great, sweating beast of iron and steam, its long line of carriages shuddering to a halt. It was a perfect machine, an embodiment of order, and he was its temporary master. The thought was obscene. He could see the guard’s van at the rear, its small windows barred. Within that steel shell was the “consignment”—a man, a name, a political abstraction that had suddenly, horribly, acquired the weight of his brother’s plea. He felt a profound, sickening disgust, not for the prisoner, nor for Nikolai, but for himself, for the position in which he found himself, a cog that had been suddenly, deliberately, made to grind. He was no longer a functionary; he was a conspirator. The air in the office, once merely stuffy, now felt thick with a moral turpidity that coated his tongue.

He gave the order to the stationmaster, a sallow, meticulous Pole named Kowalski, whose face betrayed nothing but a professional’s concern for the disruption. “A technical fault,” Feodor Ilyich heard himself say, the words sounding hollow and fraudulent in the quiet room. “A problem with the line ahead. We must hold.” Kowalski bowed his head slightly, his eyes fixed on a point somewhere over Feodor Ilyich’s shoulder. He had heard such explanations before, no doubt, and knew better than to probe the silences that surrounded them. The minutes began to stretch, each one a small eternity. He could feel the impatience of the passengers leaking from the carriages, a faint murmur that was swallowed by the vast, indifferent quiet of the station. The sun, a pale disc in the hazy sky, beat down upon the tin roofs, and the heat rose from the tracks in shimmering waves, distorting the world. He was waiting for a green light in the woods, a signal that would mean freedom for one man and damnation for another. His brother’s world, with its terrible certainties and its demand for sacrifice, had reached across a continent to seize him by the throat.

And then, it happened. Not with a shout or a clamour, but with a sudden, sharp crack of a rifle from the direction of the woods, followed by another, and another. The sounds were thin and inconsequential at first, like dry twigs snapping, but they were followed by a deeper, more resonant boom—the explosion of a charge, perhaps, on a distant culvert. Panic, when it came, was not a roar but a swift, chilling current that ran through the station. Shouts went up. Men in plain clothes, who moments before had been lounging near the water tower, were now running, their faces grim and purposeful. A green flare arced into the sky, a ghostly, unnatural star that promised violence. From the guard’s van, there was a commotion. A door was wrenched open. A figure, pale and blinking in the sudden light, was half-dragged, half-carried out onto the platform and across the tracks towards the waiting shadows of the woods. It all happened with a dreadful, swift efficiency. Feodor Ilyich stood frozen at his window, a spectator to the consequences of his own compliance. He saw one of the rescuers fall, clutching his side, and another turn to fire a revolver at the uniformed men who were only now beginning to understand what was occurring. The prisoner, the “consignment,” was gone, swallowed by the trees. The train stood silent and violated on the track. The silence returned, but it was a different silence now—not one of anticipation, but of a profound and terrible emptiness. He had not lifted a finger; he had only given an order and waited. Yet he felt as though he had fired the shots himself. His brother’s victory was his own annihilation. He had held the line, and in doing so, had stepped off it for ever, into a moral wilderness from which he knew, with a cold, clear certainty, there would be no return. The machine had been stopped, and he was its broken cog.
