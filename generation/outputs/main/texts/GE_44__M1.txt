It is a truth generally acknowledged by those who have had the good fortune to be praised in print, that the eulogist is often a greater obstacle to the praised than the most virulent of detractors; for while the detractor leaves one free to hate him, the eulogist demands a gratitude which must be constantly simulated, until the very soul of the benefactor is felt to be a burden upon the life of the beneficiary. This was the peculiar situation in which Miss Adela Thorne found herself placed on the evening of the great Literary Banquet at the house of Mr. Cuthbert Vane, the eminent critic of *The Saturday Review*, whose article on "The New Realism in Fiction" had done more to establish the reputation of the anonymous author of *The Mill on the Wye* than the book itself had done in its first quiet circulation.

Miss Thorne, standing before the pier-glass in her small lodging in Bloomsbury, adjusting the black lace of her mantilla, felt a strange dissociation of her own identity, such as is often the experience of those who live a double life. To the world, she was merely the sister of "Julian Thorne," the shy and reclusive author who had sent her as his delegate; to herself, she was the creator of the very scenes and sentiments which Mr. Vane was to dissect over his turtle soup and venison. The deception had been necessitated not by any love of intrigue, but by that iron law of the publishing trade which declares that a novel of serious philosophical import cannot be sold if it be known to flow from the pen of a woman; the public, it seems, can forgive a book for being dull, but not for being feminine.

As she entered the long, gas-lit dining room of Mr. Vane’s town house in Cavendish Square, she was struck by the atmosphere of masculine self-complacency which pervaded the assembly, an atmosphere as thick and stifling as the cigar smoke that would later cloud the drawing-room. Mr. Vane, a gentleman of expansive girth and a polished baldness that reflected the chandelier, greeted her with a warmth that was intended to be fraternal but which struck Adela as the condescension of a giant to a pygmy.

"Miss Thorne!" he boomed, taking her hand in both of his. "This is a delight, an unexpected delight. We had looked for your brother, of course, but to see the domestic muse, the sister who, I doubt not, first inspired his tender sentiments—why, it is better, far better. You are the visible proof of the gentleness that lies beneath his rugged style."

Adela smiled, a smile she had practised in the glass, a smile of modest vacuity. "My brother is much indisposed, Mr. Vane. The London air does not agree with him, and he begs you will forgive his absence."

"Say no more, say no more," said Mr. Vane, leading her to her place between himself and a grave gentleman from the Board of Trade. "We know the temperament of genius. It is a delicate plant. But we shall feast on his words, if not on his presence."

The dinner was a succession of courses and of opinions, the latter being served with a richness that left the former somewhat pallid. The conversation roamed from the price of corn to the philosophy of Mr. Mill, and then, inevitably, to the subject of the new school of fiction. It was here that Adela felt the peculiar torture of the eavesdropper listening to a discussion of her own soul.

"And the remarkable thing about this *Mill on the Wye*," said Mr. Vane, wiping his mouth with a napkin of damask, "is the virility of the sentiment. There is no namby-pamby, no sickly sentimentality. It is the work of a man who has looked at life with the eye of a surgeon and the heart of a stoic. I said to the publisher, 'Here is a man who understands the machinery of society, who sees the forces at work beneath the drawing-room floor.'"

"I confess," said the gentleman from the Board of Trade, "I was surprised to learn the author was a Thorne. I had thought the style somewhat... continental."

"Julian is a great observer," Adela interjected, her voice sounding thin and reedy in her own ears. "He spends much time in the manufacturing districts."

"Indeed," said Mr. Vane, benevolently. "And he has done well to avoid the pitfall into which so many of our modern writers stumble. I refer, of course, to the female novelists."

Adela felt a sudden chill, a premonition of disaster, as one feels when the floor gives way beneath a rotten board. She kept her eyes fixed upon the silver epergne in the centre of the table, watching the artificial grapes glisten in the gaslight.

"It is a curious anomaly," Mr. Vane continued, raising his voice slightly as if to address the room, though he looked at Adela with a kindly, pitying smile. "We have of late been inundated with works by ladies. The Brontës, the Evans woman who writes as Eliot, and a dozen others of lesser note. They possess talent, I grant you—talent of a certain order. They have an exquisite sensibility to the minor shades of emotion. They can describe a broken heart or a faded rose with a fidelity that is touching to the female reader. But they lack the weight, the ballast, the *gravitas* of the male intellect."

"Do you think so, sir?" Adela asked, her hands clasping the napkin in her lap until the knuckles showed white. "Is it not possible that a woman might understand the... the broader aspects of life?"

Mr. Vane laughed, a hearty, booming sound that seemed to shake the very decanters. "My dear Miss Thorne, you are generous to your sex. But no. A woman’s view is essentially microscopic. She sees the texture of the carpet, but not the architecture of the house. She is governed by the personal, the immediate, the domestic. She writes of what she feels; a man writes of what he knows. That is why *The Mill on the Wye* is such a triumph. It is a masculine book. It is robust. It smells of the soil and the counting-house. It is impossible to believe it was written by a creature who wears petticoats."

"And yet," said the gentleman from the Board of Trade, speaking for the first time with animation, "I have heard that the author of *Middlemarch* is a woman. A Miss Evans."

"An exception that proves the rule," Mr. Vane retorted, waving a fork dismissively. "And even there, there is a certain heaviness, a lack of that airy grace which is woman’s proper ornament. No, no. When a woman attempts the masculine style, she is like a child in her father’s coat; she may fill it out in time, but the cut is wrong. She is playing at being a man. The true feminine genius lies in the diary, the letter, the sketch of character—not in the construction of a social epic."

Adela felt a strange sensation, a mixture of amusement and despair, such as might be felt by a wolf who hears the shepherd praising the lamb for its gentleness. She looked at Mr. Vane—at his earnest, ruddy face, at the absolute conviction in his eyes—and she saw that he was not a hypocrite. He was simply a man of his time, a man who had constructed a theory of the universe in which she could not exist, and who, when confronted with the evidence of her existence, simply explained it away as a misapprehension of nature.

"You are very kind to say so, Mr. Vane," she said softly. "My brother will be gratified to know his work is considered so... masculine."

"It is the highest compliment I can pay it," said Mr. Vane, turning to his soup. "To write like a man is to write like a human being. To write like a woman is to write like a... well, let us say, a specialized variety of the species."

The conversation moved on to the subject of the Emperor of the French and the prospects of the coming exhibition, but Adela did not hear it. She sat in a state of suspended animation, feeling the irony of her position settle upon her like a physical weight. She was praised for being what she was not, and condemned for being what she was, all in the same breath, by the same man. It occurred to her, with the force of a revelation, that the world of letters was not a republic of merit, but a kingdom of masks, and that the mask of the man was the only one that commanded respect.

When the ladies rose to leave the table, Mr. Vane stood up with a courtly flourish.

"Miss Thorne," he said, "you must convey to your brother my deepest admiration. Tell him that he has done what no woman could do: he has written a book that a man can read without blushing."

"I shall tell him," said Adela, moving towards the door, her head held high. "I shall tell him exactly that."

And as she passed into the hall, she felt a sudden, fierce pride in her work—a pride that was entirely her own, and which required no validation from the great Mr. Vane. She had written the book; she had created the world; and if the world chose to see a man’s hand in it, then so much the worse for the world’s eyesight. The truth, like the grain of wheat beneath the chaff, remained; and though it might be hidden for a season, it was the only thing that would endure when the critics and their theories had turned to dust. She took her shawl from the servant, and stepped out into the cool London night, leaving the warmth of the masculine consensus behind her, and returning to the cold, clear reality of her own mind.
