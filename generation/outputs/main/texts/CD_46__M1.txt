In the village of Coalhew, where the very air was a sort of soup compounded of coal-dust, damp, and despair, there stood a row of cottages known as the Butcher’s Row, for reasons no living soul could now remember, though it was generally agreed they were grimly appropriate. Here lived Mrs. Maggs, a woman whose face was a roadmap of sorrows, and her son, Tom, a boy of twelve whose chief employment was to sort the lumps of coal and shale that came rattling up from the black throat of the pit. His hands were the colour of the earth he worked upon, and his ambition, if he could have been said to have one, was to be tall enough one day to be a putter, and to earn a shilling a day instead of sixpence.

One Tuesday, which was like every other Tuesday until the moment it was not, a scream tore through the mechanical roar of the pithead. It was a sound so thin and sharp it seemed to have been cut from the very fabric of the noise around it, and yet it silenced everything. A chain, worn to a hair’s breadth by a thousand journeys into the earth, had snapped. A cascade of coal, a small, angry avalanche, had tumbled from the sorting-screen, and Tom Maggs, in trying to jump clear, had not been quick enough. He was pulled from the wreckage of shale and splintered wood with a leg that bent at an angle God and nature never intended, his face as white as the chalk on the surveyor’s maps.

The news of the accident, and of the boy’s condition, had scarcely time to settle upon the damp hearths of Coalhew before it was eclipsed by a greater event: the arrival of Mr. Silas Vane’s carriage. Mr. Vane was the owner of the pit, a man who resided in a comfortable house in Durham and whose face, seen only in engravings on the side of a coal-scuttle, was a masterpiece of bland self-satisfaction. He did not come himself, of course. In his stead came his agent, Mr. Grimshaw, a gentleman whose spine appeared to be constructed of the same iron as the pit-props, and whose heart was a small, hard, unlit furnace. He brought with him a doctor, a man of few words and fewer sympathies, and a paper.

The paper was laid upon the Maggs’s deal table, next to a half-empty cup of chicory. “Five pounds, Mrs. Maggs,” said Mr. Grimshaw, his voice as dry as a bone in a desert. “A generous sum. Mr. Vane is a generous man. It is, as you see, a full and final settlement. A consideration for the… inconvenience.”

Mrs. Maggs looked at the paper, then at her son, who lay moaning on a cot in the corner, his leg splinted with barrel-staves. Five pounds! It was more money than she had ever held in her life. It was rent and bread and coal for a year. It was a miracle wrapped in a legal seal. Her hand, trembling, reached for the pen that lay beside the document.

At that precise moment, the cottage door opened, and a young man entered, bringing with him a gust of cold air and an altogether different atmosphere. This was Mr. Elias Thorne, the new preacher at the little Methodist chapel, a man barely older than a boy himself, with eyes that burned with a fire not of this world and a conviction that could move mountains, or at least the hearts of men. He had heard of the accident and had come to offer what comfort he could.

“Mrs. Maggs,” he said, his voice clear and firm, ignoring Mr. Grimshaw entirely. “Do not sign that paper.”

Mr. Grimshaw turned, his eyebrows arching with theatrical disdain. “And who, sir, are you, to interfere in a private transaction between an employer and the mother of his injured workman?”

“I am Elias Thorne, a servant of God,” replied the young preacher, “and I am here to serve His children. And this paper,” he said, pointing a long, righteous finger at it, “is a snare. It is the price of a boy’s leg, and his future, and it is a price far, far too low. It is thirty pieces of silver, Mrs. Maggs, offered not in a garden, but in a hovel, for a betrayal not of a master, but of a son.”

His words, full of biblical thunder, filled the small room. Mrs. Maggs looked from the preacher’s zealous face to the agent’s sneering one, and her hand withdrew from the pen. She was a simple woman, but she understood the language of scripture, and the preacher’s words had struck a chord in her soul that the offer of five pounds could not reach.

Mr. Grimshaw’s face darkened to the colour of the coal he administered. “You are a fool, preacher, and you will make a beggar of this woman. The offer is from Mr. Vane. It will not be repeated. Let me be plain. The cottages in this row are the property of Mr. Vane. The employment of every man in this village is the gift of Mr. Vane. There is a clause, madam, in your tenancy agreement. It speaks of conduct unbecoming and the bringing of disrepute upon the company’s property. A refusal to settle a matter of this kind, a stirring up of strife and litigation… well, it would be considered a very grave matter indeed. A family that causes trouble is a family that can find itself without a roof, and without a friend in the entire county.”

He let the threat hang in the air, thick and poisonous as the fumes from a faulty lamp. He looked at the preacher. “And you, sir, will find your chapel’s lease is also subject to review. Mr. Vane does not care for agitators in his village.”

But young Elias Thorne did not flinch. He stepped forward and placed a gentle hand on Mrs. Maggs’s shoulder. “Let him threaten, good woman. Let him threaten. His master owns the coal, and the stone, and the very air we breathe, it seems. But he does not own your conscience. He does not own your boy’s suffering. A house of brick is a poor shelter if the soul within it is sold. Trust in the Lord, who provides for the sparrows. Do not trust in the charity of a man who would measure a boy’s agony on a butcher’s scales.”

Mrs. Maggs looked at the preacher’s earnest, shining face, then at the cold, calculating eyes of Mr. Grimshaw. She looked at her son, whose pitiful moans were the only true argument in the room. With a sudden, decisive movement, she swept the paper and the pen from the table. They fluttered to the floor like a wounded bird.

“I’ll not sign,” she said, her voice quiet but unshakeable. “My boy’s worth more than that. God will provide.”

Mr. Grimshaw stared at her for a long moment, his face a mask of cold fury. “So be it,” he hissed. “You have made your choice. When the bailiffs come, you may pray to your God for a new home.” He turned on his heel and stalked out, leaving behind him the chill of his threat and the lingering scent of expensive, worldly power. The young preacher knelt by the woman, and began to pray, his voice a small, defiant flame of hope in the vast, encroaching darkness of Coalhew.
