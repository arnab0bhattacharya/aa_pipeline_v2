I had been sent to the island of Kapu-Tiva in the July of ’94, nominally as a botanical observer for the Crown, but in reality to keep a watchful eye upon the restless activities of the British Guano and Phosphate Company. It was a miserable, fever-haunted place, a mere ring of coral lifted from the heaving Pacific, yet it held a secret that has since shattered my nerves and driven me to put this account down in the solitude of a London lodging-house.

The station consisted of three corrugated iron shanties and a bungalow, clinging to the inner edge of a shallow lagoon. The Company’s agent, a lean, sardonic man named Brinton, spent his days consuming inferior whisky and writing furious, unanswering dispatches to his directors in London. The native population, perhaps a hundred souls, lived in a cluster of huts on the northern shore. They were a remarkably taciturn people, dark-skinned and robust, who went about their fishing with a quiet efficiency that bordered on the mechanical. 

My presence on the island was a sore point with Brinton, who suspected me of being a spy for the Colonial Office, and we maintained a frigid, argumentative truce over our evening meals. It was during one of these meals that I first noticed the old man.

He was sitting cross-legged on the sand outside Brinton’s office, mending a fishing net with an odd, jerky vigour. His skin was deeply browned and wrinkled, yet his hair was a lustrous black, and beneath the furrowed brow his eyes were startlingly bright and quick. I passed him, and he looked up at me with a frank, unblinking stare, his mouth twitching into a sudden, unsettling grin. I nodded to him, but he made no reply. 

“Who is that old boy?” I asked Brinton, who was lounging in his canvas chair, fanning himself with a copy of the Sydney Morning Herald.

“Boy?” said Brinton, without looking up. “That’s old Tavake. He’s a fool, like the rest of them.”

“He’s remarkably active for an old man,” I remarked. “He looks nearer seventy than sixty, but his eyes are like a boy’s.”

Brinton lowered his paper and stared at me with an expression of profound, ironic boredom. “He’s twenty-four,” he said.

I laughed, thinking it a clumsy jest at my expense, but Brinton’s face remained perfectly grave. “Twenty-four next month,” he repeated. “If you don’t believe me, ask him. He’s proud of it. It’s a disease they have here, or a trick of the climate. I’ve given up trying to explain it to the directors. They think I’m drinking the inventory.”

I did not press the matter then, but the thing took root in my mind. Over the following weeks I observed Tavake with a creeping, scientific curiosity. He aged, visibly and rapidly, before my eyes. In August his hair turned iron-grey, and his limbs grew stiff. By September he was a palsied, toothless wreck, crawling about the sand. And then, quite suddenly, in the middle of October, the reversal began. 

The wrinkles smoothed out. The grey hair fell away in patches, to be replaced by a fine, dark down. His teeth sprouted in his gums—I saw them cut through the flesh, a sight that made me shudder—and his limbs regained their youthful elasticity. Within a fortnight, Tavake was a boy again, running naked on the beach with the other children.

It was a biological impossibility, yet it was happening before my eyes. I discovered, by dint of much patient questioning and the bribery of the chief with trade tobacco, that this cycle was not a natural phenomenon. It was induced. 

The chief, a cunning old savage named Maru, led me one evening to a rocky cove at the far end of the island. There, in a shallow limestone basin, grew a thick, gelatinous fungus, pale blue and faintly luminescent in the twilight. It was fed by a volcanic spring that welled up from deep beneath the coral. Maru explained, in his broken pidgin, that the tribe cultivated this fungus, drying it into a bitter green powder. Once a year, the adults consumed the powder, and the great unwinding began. They shed their years like a snake sheds its skin, reverting to infancy, only to grow up again, and age, and revert, in a perpetual, unending cycle. They were immortals, trapped in a relentless biological treadmill.

I was staggered. I thought of the profound implications for human physiology, the staggering triumph over senility and death. And then, with a sudden, cold clarity, I thought of the British Guano and Phosphate Company.

Brinton had been writing to London again. His dispatches, which I had intercepted out of sheer bureaucratic habit, spoke of a “native medicinal compound” that might have commercial value as a tonic. He had sent a sample of the dried fungus to a chemist in Sydney. The directors, hungry for new monopolies, had cabled back with an urgent, avaricious interest. They wanted the formula. They wanted the exclusive right to harvest the fungus. And they had authorized Brinton to use any means necessary to secure it.

Brinton, for his part, was simply eager to be transferred from the island. “I don’t care if they bottle it as patent medicine or use it to dye their wives’ hair,” he said to me, pouring himself a generous measure of whisky. “If I can get this cursed fungus shipped off to London, they’ll give me a post in Fiji. I’m sick of the sight of these grinning idiots and their eternal youth. It’s unnatural.”

“It’s a miracle,” I said, hotly. “It’s the greatest discovery in the history of medicine.”

“It’s a commodity,” Brinton retorted, with the brutal directness of the Company man. “And it’s on Company land, or close enough to it. We claim the lagoon, and everything in it. If these savages won’t hand over the recipe, we’ll simply take the basin. What can they do? They haven’t got a rifle between them.”

I tried to argue the ethical case, but I might as well have argued with the coral. Brinton was immovable. He had dispatched a canoe to the next island to summon the Company’s trading schooner. In three days, he said, the captain would arrive with a dozen armed Kanakas, and they would clear the natives out of the cove and secure the fungus for the directors.

I was caught in a wretched dilemma. I was not a sentimentalist about native races; I believed, in the abstract, in the march of civilization and the dissemination of knowledge. But the thought of these peaceful, extraordinary people being driven from their ancestral spring, of their sacred, life-giving fungus being ground up into pills for the enrichment of a parcel of London shareholders, revolted my sense of justice. And yet, I was an official of the Crown. To actively oppose the Company was to ruin my career, to be dismissed in disgrace. 

I spent a sleepless night, pacing the floor of my bungalow. The thing was not merely a question of native rights versus commercial enterprise; it was a question of the fundamental nature of the discovery itself. Was this secret meant for the world? Could it even be replicated in a London laboratory? Or was it, like some terrible elixir, dependent upon the precise volcanic chemistry of this one lonely island? If the Company took it, they would hoard it, exploit it, perhaps destroy it in their greed. 

By dawn, I had made my decision. I was a poor man, and a timid one, but there are limits to the obedience a reasonable man will offer to a board of directors.

I sought out Maru. I told him, in plain terms, that the white men were coming to take his spring. He listened to me with those dark, fathomless eyes, his face betraying no emotion. When I had finished, he nodded slowly.

“We know,” he said, in his thick, guttural English. “We see the ship come. We go.”

“Go where?” I asked, astonished. “There is no other land for a hundred miles.”

“The sea,” he said, simply. “The sea takes all things. We go. We do not come back. The fungus dies.”

I stared at him, aghast. “You would destroy it? You would drown yourselves rather than give it up?”

Maru looked out over the lagoon, where the morning sun was painting the water in streaks of fire. “It is ours,” he said. “It is not for the white man. The white man makes everything into a box, into a bottle, into a shilling. This is not for shillings. This is for the breath.”

I did not attempt to dissuade him. I knew, with a cold certainty, that he meant what he said. The secret of Kapu-Tiva would die with its people.

I returned to the station and wrote my report. I stated that the native compound was a fraud, a mere local superstition with no scientific basis, and that the supposed rejuvenation was a trick of the light and the fevered imagination of a homesick agent. I recommended that the Company abandon its interest in the island. 

Brinton, when he read it, flew into a rage. He called me a liar and a meddler. But when the schooner arrived two days later, there were no natives to be found. The huts on the northern shore were empty. The fishing canoes were gone. The limestone basin in the cove had been smashed to pieces, and the volcanic spring ran clear and barren. 

The Company men searched the island, but they found nothing. The people of Kapu-Tiva had vanished into the vast, indifferent Pacific, taking their miracle with them.

I was recalled to London shortly after, my career blighted by Brinton’s vindictive reports. I am left now with nothing but my memories, and the haunting vision of old Tavake, grinning up at me from the sand, his eyes bright with the terrible, fleeting promise of an endless youth.
