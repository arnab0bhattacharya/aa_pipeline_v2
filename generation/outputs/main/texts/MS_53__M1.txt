I have often reflected, in the solitary hours which the pestilence has forced upon us, that there is no agony like that of the man who, having devoted his youth to the study of Nature’s laws, beholds those laws perverted by the wild ravings of fanaticism. I, who had walked through the wards of the dying, who had breathed the tainted air of the charnel-house, and who had seen the human form divine dissolve into a mass of corruption, felt a peculiar bitterness when I heard that a new prophet had arisen in the Midlands, a man named Elias Thorne, who proclaimed the disease to be a divine scourge, and his own followers to be the only chosen vessels of safety. It was with a heart heavy with indignation, and a mind resolved to tear the mask from this impostor, that I left the sterile safety of the capital and journeyed toward the town of Ashenford.

The journey itself was a fitting prelude to the drama that awaited me. The fields, which in happier times had waved with golden corn, were now blasted and blackened, as if the very earth had caught the contagion of the human pest. A thick, sulphurous fog hung over the landscape, obscuring the sun and casting a spectral light upon the ruined villages we passed. The silence was profound, broken only by the rumble of the carriage wheels and the occasional cry of a bird of prey. In this desolation, my thoughts turned incessantly to the man I sought. I pictured him as a charlatan, a creature of gross appetites and cunning intellect, fattening upon the fears of the ignorant. I resolved that I would confront him with the cold, hard logic of science; I would show him the microscope, the germ, the vector of contagion; I would expose his "miracles" as the coincidences of a chaotic world.

When I arrived at Ashenford, I found a community transformed. The streets were not filled with the frantic bustle of panic, but with a strange, hushed solemnity. The houses were closed, yet there was no sign of the rotting corpses that littered the alleys of London. The people moved with a slow, deliberate grace, their faces pale but calm. I was directed to the house of Elias Thorne, which stood upon a hill overlooking the town, a structure of grey stone that seemed to grow from the very rock of the earth. As I approached, the wind howled about the gables, and the trees writhed in the storm like tortured spirits. I felt a tremor in my own soul, not of fear, but of a terrible, sublime anticipation.

I was admitted into a large hall, where a fire blazed in a cavernous hearth, casting long, dancing shadows upon the walls. There, seated in a high chair, was the prophet. He was not the bloated hypocrite I had imagined. He was a man of middle age, but his frame was wasted, his limbs thin and attenuated, as if the spirit within him had consumed the body. His eyes were large and dark, burning with an intensity that was almost painful to behold. He did not rise as I entered, but fixed upon me a gaze that seemed to pierce through my flesh and read the very secrets of my heart.

"You come from the city of the dead," he said, his voice low and resonant, vibrating with a strange music. "You come to tell me that I am a liar, and that God is a myth."

"I come," I replied, struggling to maintain my composure against the magnetism of his presence, "to tell you that you are a danger to mankind. I come to prove that this pestilence is a matter of biology, not of theology, and that your promises of salvation are a cruel mockery to those who suffer."

I opened my bag and produced my instruments—the slides, the vials, the diagrams of the bacillus. I spoke with the fervor of a zealot myself, pouring out my knowledge of virology, of transmission, of the blind, unfeeling mechanism of nature. I expected him to recoil, to rage, or to dismiss me with a priestly sneer. But he listened with an attention so profound that it silenced me.

When I had finished, he rose and walked to the window. The storm had broken, and the rain lashed against the panes. "You show me the instrument," he said, "but you cannot show me the hand that wields it. You dissect the corpse, but you know nothing of the soul that has fled. You say this is nature; I say nature is the garment of the Almighty, and the pestilence is the rent in the fabric through which His wrath is poured."

He turned to me, and his eyes were filled with a pity that stung me more than anger. "Do you think I desire this power? Do you think I enjoy the adulation of fools? I am the vessel of a truth that breaks me. I have seen the vision, not in a microscope, but in the burning light of revelation. The pestilence is a fire, and only those who have stripped themselves of the dross of earthly pride—of which you, doctor, have a great store—can pass through it unscathed."

"Then why are your followers spared?" I demanded, my voice rising. "Is their blood different from mine? Are they not subject to the same laws of physics?"

"They are spared," he whispered, "because they have surrendered their will. They do not fight the stream of fate; they float upon it. They have made their peace with death, and in doing so, have conquered it. You, who cling to life with such desperate tenacity, are the most vulnerable of all."

I looked at him then, truly looked at him, and for the first time, the conviction of my own intellect faltered. I saw in his face no guile, no avarice, no calculation. I saw only a terrible, consuming sincerity. He was not a deceiver; he was a man possessed by an idea so vast and so dark that it had eclipsed his reason. The melancholy that radiated from him was not feigned; it was the burden of a man who believed he carried the sins of the world upon his shoulders.

I left Ashenford that night, as the storm raged with a fury that seemed to shake the foundations of the earth. I had gone to expose a fraud, but I returned with a wound in my spirit that no science could heal. I had found the prophet sincere, and in his sincerity lay a horror far greater than any deception. For if he was wrong, he was a madman leading the innocent to slaughter; but if, by some terrible possibility, he was right, then the universe was a place of cruelty beyond my comprehension, and I, the rational man, was the true heretic. The rain fell upon the roof of my carriage like the tears of a desolate heaven, and I knew that I was alone, a solitary wanderer in a world that had lost its mind.
