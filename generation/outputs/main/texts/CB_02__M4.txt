The rain had beaten against the long windows of Fieldhead since morning, and now, at four o’clock, it had settled into a steady, sullen downpour that turned the mill-yard to a lake of black mud and blurred the distant chimneys of Bradford into a smear of smoke and mist. Within the schoolroom, however, there was a fire of good coal, and a tolerably bright carpet, and two little girls—Ellen and Maria Sykes—bending over their copybooks with that particular air of injured virtue which children assume when they are conscious of having been unusually idle.

I had been three years in that house, and I knew the sound of every door in it. I knew, too, the sound of the gate at the bottom of the carriage-drive, and the particular clatter of hoofs which announced that Mr. Harry Sykes had returned from the town. That clatter came at half-past four, and I did not lift my head from the exercise I was correcting; I merely drew my pen through a badly formed letter with a steadiness that surprised me, and said to Ellen, “Your ‘m’ is a ‘w,’ and your ‘w’ is nothing at all. Begin the line again.”

She was not a bad child, but she was a Sykes, and the Sykeses were accustomed to have their own way with the world. “Miss Bell,” said she, “I heard Harry’s horse. May we not go down? He promised us a box of comfits from town.”

“You may go when your copy is finished, and not before.”

“But he will go out again directly—he is going to Rawdon to dine. He always goes to Rawdon now.”

The name fell into the room like a stone into still water. I felt the colour rise in my face, and I bent my head lower over the paper, and hated myself for the flush, and hated the child for her innocent cruelty. “Rawdon” was written on my heart in letters of fire; “Rawdon” was the name of the great house among the trees beyond the river, where lived Miss Beatrice Hargreaves, only daughter of old Hargreaves the banker, with her fortune and her fine clothes and her air of serene and careless command. And Harry Sykes, the eldest son of my employer, the young master of the mill, was going to Rawdon to dine.

I had known it these three weeks. The whole house knew it; the servants talked of it in the kitchen, the clerks in the counting-house nodded over it, and Mrs. Sykes had already begun to speak of “dear Beatrice” in a tone of proprietorship that made me sick at heart. Only Harry himself had never spoken of it to me. He had come into the schoolroom a fortnight ago, and stood by the window with his hands in his pockets, looking out at the same rain, and said, “Miss Bell, you are a sensible little body; tell me, do you think a man is a fool to marry for money?” And I had answered, with a steady voice and a dry throat, “I think, sir, a man is a fool to marry without it.” And he had laughed—that short, hard laugh of his—and gone out, and I had sat down on the stool by the fire and shaken as though I had the ague.

I was a nursery governess—a thing of no account in the world’s eyes—the daughter of a poor curate who had died of a fever in a Leeds lodging-house, leaving me nothing but a plain education and a proud stomach. I had no beauty to speak of; my hair was brown and would not curl, my hands were red with the winter, and my best dress was a black stuff that had been turned twice. And Harry Sykes was the young master, with a fortune to make and a name to keep, and Miss Hargreaves had thirty thousand pounds and a face like a rose. It was not for me to lift my eyes to such a height; and I had not lifted them—no, not in my secret soul, I told myself—I had only admired him as one admires a fine picture or a noble building; I had only been glad when he came into the room, and sorry when he went out; I had only thought of his voice and his step and the way he had of throwing back his head when he laughed, until the thought of him had become the air I breathed. That was all. And now he was going to Rawdon to dine.

The copy was finished at last, and the children clattered away. I sat alone in the schoolroom, with the fire burning low and the rain still beating on the panes, and I took up my work—a piece of plain sewing that I did for Mrs. Sykes’s charity-basket—and I sewed at it with a kind of fierce industry, as though the needle could stitch up the wound in my breast. The clock struck five; I heard the carriage come round to the door; I heard Harry’s voice in the hall, and Mrs. Sykes’s voice, and the rustle of Miss Hargreaves’s silk—for she had come to dine with them before they went on to Rawdon—and then the front door banged, and the wheels rolled away into the rain.

I put down my work and went to the window. The carriage lamps were two blurred stars in the darkness, moving down the drive. I watched them until they turned into the road and were gone; and then I pressed my forehead against the cold glass and shut my eyes, and said to myself, in a voice that was scarcely a whisper, “This is madness, Jane Bell; this is wickedness and folly; you must kill it, or it will kill you.”

But the heart will not be killed at a word. I thought of the first day I came to Fieldhead—how he had met me in the hall and taken my box from the porter’s hand and carried it up himself, and said, “You will find my little sisters wild as hawks, Miss Bell, but I dare say you will tame them.” I thought of the evening in the garden when he had found me reading under the lime-tree, and had sat down on the grass at my feet and talked to me of the books I loved, and of the great world beyond Bradford, and of the things he meant to do when he was his own master. I thought of the day when I had a headache and he had brought me a glass of wine with his own hand, and his fingers had touched mine, and he had looked at me with a strange, grave look, and said, “You work too hard, little governess.” And I had treasured that look and that word for months, as a miser treasures a coin; and now—now he was going to Rawdon to dine.

I turned from the window at last, and lit my candle, and went up to my own chamber. It was a small room at the back of the house, looking out on the mill-yard; and when I had set the candle on the table, I stood and looked at myself in the little glass—at my pale face and my plain black dress and my brown hair that would not curl—and I said, “You are a fool, Jane Bell; you are a fool and a dreamer, and you have no more claim on Harry Sykes than the girl who scrubs his floors.” And then I fell on my knees by the bed, and prayed—not for him, I dared not pray for him—but for strength to do my duty and keep my secret and be faithful to the children who were given into my charge.

The rain ceased before midnight, and the moon came out and shone cold and clear on the wet roofs and the black mill-chimney. I heard the carriage return at last, and the voices in the hall, and the door shut, and the house grow still. And I lay in my narrow bed with my eyes open, staring at the patch of moonlight on the wall, and thinking—thinking—thinking—until the dawn came grey through the curtain, and the mill-bell rang for the six o’clock hands, and I rose to begin another day.
