The great house at Wrenscote stood in a park of such studied and melancholy beauty that a stranger, viewing it from the eastern terrace at the hour when the sun declines, might have supposed its inmates to be the happiest of mortals. The cedars drooped their heavy branches over the smooth turf with a benediction; the deer, moving in slow and shadowy groups, gave the landscape that air of antique peace which is the peculiar gift of an English domain; and within, the long galleries, the storied windows, the very silence of the corridors, spoke of a race whose roots had struck deep into the soil of the county for generations before the first George had come to the throne. Yet it is a truth, which the wise have observed in every age, that the external fitness of a life is no sure measure of its inward satisfaction; and the mistress of this fair domain, had you been gifted with the power of reading the human countenance, would have appeared to you less like a woman in possession of her heart's desire than like one who walks in a dream from which she cannot awake.

Lady Ormsby—for it was by this title that Mrs. Anne Ferrers, late of the Theatre Royal, Drury Lane, was now known to the county—had been, in her day, the most celebrated tragic actress of the English stage. There were those still living who declared that her Lady Macbeth had never been equalled, and that her Beatrice, her Portia, her Hermione, had a sweetness and a dignity which no subsequent performer had approached. She had retired, at the age of thirty-eight, in the full blaze of her reputation, to marry Lord Ormsby, a widower of grave and somewhat taciturn disposition, whose estates in Northamptonshire were among the most considerable in the kingdom, and whose sense of what was due to his name and position was as inflexible as the oak that had been his family's emblem for three centuries. The marriage had been, in the estimation of the world, a brilliant one; and Lady Ormsby, who had never known the comforts of a settled home, had entered upon it with a sincere determination to be all that her new station required. She had laid aside her art as a garment that is done with; she had studied the duties of a great lady with the same conscientious ardour she had once bestowed upon the study of a part; and if she had sometimes found the days long and the conversation of her neighbours barren, she had attributed the feeling to a natural regret for the excitements of her former life, and had striven, by a more rigorous attention to her new obligations, to silence the voice that would not be stilled.

But the years had passed, and the voice had not been silenced. It had grown, indeed, more importunate with every season. Lord Ormsby was a good man, and she honoured him; but he was a man to whom the present was everything and the past a closed book, and he could not understand—perhaps it was not to be expected that he should—the passionate longing that consumed his wife for the exercise of that art which had once been the very breath of her nostrils. He had never seen her act. He had, indeed, made it a condition of their marriage that her name should never again be associated with the stage; and she, in the first flush of her gratitude and her resolve, had accepted the condition without a murmur. But the stage had been to her not merely a profession—it had been a world, a religion, a means of escape from the narrowness and the weariness of common life. In the green-room she had been free; on the boards she had been herself. And now, in the midst of her magnificence, she found herself stifled by the very completeness of her prosperity.

It was in the third week of October, when the leaves were falling in the park and the first fires were being lighted in the great drawing-room, that a letter arrived which was destined to disturb the even tenor of her existence. It was brought to her by her own maid, together with the other letters of the morning, and she recognised at once, by the bold, unformed hand upon the envelope, the writing of her old friend and manager, Mr. Joseph Craddock, of the Theatre Royal, Drury Lane. She opened it with a beating heart. Mr. Craddock wrote to inform her that the great actor, Henry Alderney, whose fame was then at its height, was about to produce a new tragedy, and that he had expressed a passionate desire to play the principal part in it with her, and her alone. The season would be short—only twelve nights—but it would be the event of the theatrical year. He implored her, in the name of their old friendship and of the art they both served, to consent. He would make every arrangement that could minister to her comfort and her dignity; her name should be placed at the head of the bill; and he would undertake that nothing should be wanting which could secure the success of the undertaking. "I know," he wrote, "that you have retired, and that you have your reasons. But I also know that you were born for the stage, and that the stage has never ceased to sigh for you. Think of it, my dear friend, before you refuse. It is not for the money, nor for the fame, but for the art that is in you, and that will die with you if you do not give it one more utterance."

Lady Ormsby laid down the letter, and for a long time she sat quite still, gazing at the fire that burned in the grate with a steady and cheerful flame. The room was very quiet; the only sound was the ticking of the clock upon the mantelpiece and the distant murmur of the wind among the trees. She thought of her husband, and of the promise she had made him; she thought of the county, and of the scandal that would be caused by her return to the stage; she thought of the cold and stately house, and of the long evenings when she had sat alone, listening to the wind, and wondering whether life had nothing more to give her. And then she thought of the theatre—of the smell of the gas and the paint, of the crowded house, of the thrill of the first entrance, of the moment when the audience is hushed into silence and the great words of the poet fall upon the listening air. Her heart swelled within her; she rose from her chair and walked to the window, and looked out upon the falling leaves.

It was at this moment that Lord Ormsby entered the room. He was a tall, spare man, with a grave and handsome face, and the air of one who has never known a doubt. He came towards her with his usual measured step, and asked her whether she had any news.

She hesitated for a moment. Then she said, "I have had a letter from an old friend, Mr. Craddock, of Drury Lane. He wants me to act again—only for twelve nights—with Henry Alderney, in a new tragedy."

Lord Ormsby's face changed. It was not the change of anger, but of a deep and settled displeasure, which was the more formidable because it was so rarely expressed. He stood looking at her for a moment in silence, and then he said, in a voice that was perfectly calm, "I thought that matter had been settled between us, Anne. I thought you had promised me that your name should never again be spoken in connection with the stage."

"I did promise you," she said, "and I have kept my promise. But I did not know—I did not know that it would be like this."

"Like what?" he said. "You have everything that a woman can desire—position, wealth, the respect of the county. You have a home which any woman in England might envy you. What is it that you want?"

She turned away from him, and looked out of the window. "I want to live," she said, in a low voice. "I want to feel that I am something more than a piece of furniture in a great house. I want to use the gift that God gave me, before it is too late."

There was a long silence. Lord Ormsby walked to the fire and stood with his back to it, his hands clasped behind him. He was not a hard man, but he was a man of rigid principles, and he could not understand the nature of the temptation that beset his wife. To him the stage was a place of doubtful repute, associated in his mind with the frivolity and the laxity of the last century; and the idea of his wife—the mistress of Wrenscote—appearing before a London audience was as shocking to his sense of propriety as if she had proposed to walk naked through the streets of Northampton. He could not see that the art which she loved was a sacred thing, a gift of the spirit, a means of revealing to men the hidden beauty of the world; he could only see that it was a public exhibition, and that it was inconsistent with the dignity of his name.

"I cannot consent to it," he said at last. "I cannot and I will not. If you go, you go against my wishes and against my judgment. I shall not divorce you—I shall not even reproach you—but I shall never be able to forget it."

She turned and faced him. Her eyes were bright with a strange light, and her voice was firm. "Then I must go," she said. "I am sorry—more sorry than I can say—but I must go. I have been dead for five years, and I want to live again."

Lord Ormsby looked at her with an expression of profound and sorrowful surprise. He had never seen her like this before. He had known her as a gentle and compliant woman, who had seemed to accept his views with a docility that was almost touching; and he had supposed that her submission was the result of a genuine conviction. He did not know—how should he?—that the submission had been the result of a long and bitter struggle, and that the victory had been won at the cost of something that was dearer to her than life itself. He was silent for a moment, and then he said, in a voice that was not unkind, "You are not yourself, Anne. You are excited and unwell. We will talk of this another time."

But she knew that there would be no other time. She knew that the decision must be made now, and that it must be made by her alone. She went to the writing-table, and took up her pen, and wrote a few lines to Mr. Craddock, accepting his offer. Then she sealed the letter, and rang the bell, and gave it to the servant to post. And when she had done this, she turned to her husband, and said, with a smile that was half sad and half triumphant, "I have written to Mr. Craddock. I shall act for twelve nights, and then I shall come back—if you will have me."

Lord Ormsby bowed his head. He did not speak. He walked slowly to the door, and opened it, and went out. And Lady Ormsby, left alone in the great drawing-room, with the fire burning low and the wind moaning among the trees, sat down in her chair and covered her face with her hands. She had chosen—she had chosen at last—but she knew that the choice had cost her more than she had ever dreamed of losing. And yet, in the midst of her sorrow, there was a strange and secret joy, which she could not stifle, at the thought that she would once more hear the applause of the multitude, and feel the thrill of the footlights, and be, for a few brief nights, the queen of the stage.
