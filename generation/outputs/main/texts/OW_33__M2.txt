In the deep, velvet silence of the Palace of Whispers, where the air hung heavy with the scent of night-blooming jasmine and the dust of forgotten centuries, there lay a pool of water so still it seemed less a body of liquid than a pane of polished obsidian. In the centre of this mirror, resting upon a bed of submerged emeralds and rusted coins, floated a single white water lily. Her name, if such a name could be attributed to a flower that had never spoken, was Elara. She was not a creature of the earth in the common, muddy sense, nor entirely of the water, for she possessed a soul that was distinctly, and perhaps dangerously, aristocratic.

Elara had fallen in love with the Moon.

This was not a metaphor, as the poets of the court might have preferred it to be, nor was it a simple case of botanical instinct drawn toward light. It was a passion of the most refined and devastating kind, a love that recognized its own impossibility and found in that impossibility a sort of exquisite logic. The Moon, pale and indifferent as a diamond set in a ring that no one wore, hung above the palace roof. She was distant, cold, and utterly unattainable, which was, Elara decided, precisely what one should look for in a lover. To love something that could not love you back was the only form of romance that remained honest in a world that had made a trade of sentiment.

The other inhabitants of the pool, however, possessed no such subtlety. They were creatures of the immediate, the tangible, and the somewhat vulgar.

On the first night of her devotion, Elara unfurled her petals with a grace that was almost painful in its precision. She stretched her white arms toward the silver disc in the sky, waiting for a touch that would never come. Below her, in the murky depths, the goldfish swirled in their lazy, metallic circles. One of them, a creature of considerable girth and limited intellect, named Gaspard, stopped to regard her with an eye that was bulging and perpetually surprised.

"You are wasting your energy, Elara," Gaspard bubbled, his voice a wet, gurgling sound that seemed to rise from the very bottom of the pool. "The Moon is made of cheese, or perhaps of cloud, but certainly not of anything that can be eaten or kept. It is a useless object. Why do you stretch toward it? It is colder than the winter wind and further than the next kingdom. You are being absurdly impractical."

Elara did not look down. To look down would be to admit that the water was deep, and the distance to the sky was infinite. She kept her eyes fixed on the pale orb above. "It is not a question of impracticality, Gaspard," she said, her voice soft as the rustle of silk. "It is a question of aesthetic necessity. The Moon is the only thing in this garden that does not ask me for anything. It does not want my roots, it does not want my scent, it does not want to swallow me or lay its eggs in my leaves. It simply is. That is a far more romantic state than anything you, with your perpetual hunger, could ever comprehend."

Gaspard blinked, his fins fluttering in indignation. "Romance is for those who have nothing else to do," he retorted. "I have food to find and predators to avoid. You have a stem and a set of petals. Do not try to elevate your stagnation into philosophy. It is a trick of the light, nothing more."

From the lily pad beside her, a chorus of frogs began to croak. They were small, brown things with eyes like wet beads, led by a particularly loud specimen named Barnaby. Barnaby had a voice that cracked when he tried to sound authoritative, which only made him more insufferable.

"Hear, hear!" Barnaby croaked, slapping the water with his hind legs. "The flower is mad! She looks at the sky when she should be looking at the mud. The Moon is a liar, Elara. It promises light but gives only cold. It is a great, pale joke played upon us by the universe. Why do you not look at the stars? They are at least numerous. Or better yet, why do you not close your eyes and sleep? Love is a disease, and you are catching it from the very air."

"I am not sick," Elara replied, though a shiver of cold ran through her stem. "I am awake. That is all. The Moon is not a joke; it is a symbol. And I am not in love with the light; I am in love with the distance. It is the only thing that makes the longing beautiful. If the Moon were close, if it were here in the water, it would be just another puddle, another reflection. But as it is, it is a promise that can never be kept, and that is the purest form of truth there is."

The frogs laughed, a sound like stones dropping into a dry well. "Truth!" Barnaby sneered. "Truth is that you are a flower floating in a puddle in a palace garden, and you are pretending to be a goddess. You are ridiculous. We have seen better fools, though we admit we have not seen such a pretty one. But pretty is not a virtue, Elara. It is merely a decoration. And decorations get broken."

Elara said nothing more. She knew that to argue with the frogs was to lower oneself to their level, and to argue with the goldfish was to waste breath on things that had no lungs. She turned her face back to the Moon. The silver light fell upon her petals, turning them from white to a pale, ghostly blue. It was a cold light, yes, but it was clean. It did not carry the smell of decay or the taste of algae. It was pure, and therefore it was good.

Every night, she performed this ritual. She opened her petals to the sky, a gesture of surrender so complete it looked like prayer. And every morning, as the sun rose and the gardeners came with their nets and their buckets, she closed her petals in sorrow. The darkness of her own cup was a shelter, but it was also a tomb. She held her breath, waiting for the night to come, waiting for the cold eye of the Moon to look down upon her and see, perhaps, something in her stillness that the frogs and the goldfish could not fathom.

One evening, a young peacock, Sir Reginald, walked along the edge of the pool. He was a creature of immense vanity and profound boredom, his feathers a riot of electric blues and emerald greens that caught the dying light. He stopped to look into the water, not to see his reflection, but to see who might be looking at him. He saw Elara, floating in the center, her petals beginning to open as the first stars appeared.

"Good evening," Sir Reginald said, his voice a smooth, baritone purr. "You look melancholy. Is it the lack of company? Or perhaps the lack of rain? I hear the gardeners have been negligent with the sprinklers."

Elara turned her head, just slightly. "I am not melancholy, Sir Reginald. I am waiting."

"For what?" he asked, tilting his head with an affectation of curiosity. "For a poet to write a sonnet? For a prince to pick you up and put you in his boutonnière? Or for the rain, as I suggested?"

"For the Moon," Elara said.

Sir Reginald let out a laugh that was as elegant as it was cruel. "The Moon! You are in love with the Moon. How very... continental of you. We in this palace prefer our loves to be somewhat more solid. The Moon is a celestial body, Elara. It is not a suitor. It has no hands to hold you, no mouth to kiss you. It is a mirror that reflects nothing but itself. You are in love with a reflection. That is the height of foolishness."

"Is it?" Elara asked. "When you look in a mirror, Sir Reginald, do you see yourself, or do you see what you wish to be? The Moon does not wish to be anything. It simply shines. And I find that in a world of pretence, honesty is the only luxury I can afford."

Sir Reginald looked at her for a long moment. There was a flicker of something in his eyes, not quite pity, but perhaps a grudging respect for the audacity of her madness. "You are a strange flower," he said at last. "Most of you try to please the eye. You try to be sweet, or colorful, or fragrant. You try to be useful. You are the only one I have met who tries to be impossible. I suppose that is why you are so beautiful. It is the beauty of a mistake."

He turned and walked away, his tail trailing behind him like a train of royal purple. Elara watched him go, and then she turned back to the sky. The Moon was high now, full and round and utterly indifferent. It did not know her name. It did not know her pain. It did not know that she was a flower who had chosen the stars over the soil.

And perhaps that was the point. In a world that demanded answers, she had chosen the question. In a garden that demanded growth, she had chosen stillness. She opened her petals wider, letting the cold light pour into her heart, and for a moment, in the deep, dark water, she was not a flower in a pool, but a star fallen into the earth, burning with a light that was not her own, but which, in its silence, was hers.
