The autumnal equinox had passed, yet its tempestuous spirit lingered over the wild and melancholy wastes of Northumberland, shrouding in funereal gloom the lowly parsonage of Cragsmoor. The wind swept across the desolate heaths with a mournful cadence, and the rain beat fiercely against the latticed casement of that humble dwelling, as if the very elements sympathized in the secret agony that tore my soul. I was then a youth of one-and-twenty, possessed of a fervent imagination and a heart peculiarly alive to the affections, knowing no other parent than the venerable curate, Mr. Holloway, whose frail form and silver hair had been to me the visible embodiment of all paternal love and moral grandeur. I had believed myself his orphaned ward, the son of some obscure and penniless clerk, bequeathed to his care in infancy; and in my poverty and obscurity I had found a tranquil happiness, nourished by his precepts and the sublime solitude of our mountainous retreat. But the illusions of a quiet life are but as the fragile bark that dares the treacherous ocean of human destiny; and mine was destined to be rudely shattered upon the rocks of a terrible and unexpected truth.

A messenger had arrived that very evening from the neighboring seat of Lord Ashcroft, a nobleman whose vast domains and ancient name were as familiar to our ears as the howling of the winter storm. He had long been a recluse, shrouded in mystery and weighed down, as rumor whispered, by some hidden sorrow; but now the hand of death lay heavy upon him, and he sought, with the desperate eagerness of a departing spirit, to disburthen his conscience. The missive he sent was brief, yet it contained words that struck upon my heart with the stunning force of a thunderbolt. I was, it declared, no nameless foundling, but his own natural son, the offspring of a youthful and disastrous union, concealed from the world to preserve the pride of his lineage. As he lay upon his deathbed, the terrors of eternity pressing upon his view, he desired to acknowledge me openly, to bestow upon me his name, and to place me in the station to which, by birth, I was entitled.

I read the fatal paper, and the characters swam before my swimming eyes; the walls of the lowly parlour seemed to recede into an illimitable void, and I felt myself cast forth, a solitary and bewildered wanderer, upon the boundless ocean of life. My identity, so long a fixed and tranquil certainty, was in a moment dissolved, and I stood shivering on the brink of a new existence, terrified by the abyss that yawned beneath me. The voice of Mr. Holloway recalled me from the stunned stupor into which I had fallen. He had watched me as I read, his mild, blue eyes dilated with an agony far more poignant than my own; for while I was overwhelmed by the sudden revelation of a splendid and terrible destiny, he beheld in it the blasting of his fondest hopes, the severance of the tenderest tie that bound his declining years to existence.

"Edgar," he murmured, in a voice tremulous with suppressed emotion, extending his pallid hands as if to clasp me from some unseen precipice, "you will not—oh, tell me you will not—desert me in my age and helplessness! I have been to you a father; I have watched over your infancy with a mother's care; I have toiled to nourish your mind, to elevate your thoughts, to make you worthy of the respect of the virtuous and the good. And will you now cast me aside for the allurements of a world whose hollow splendors I have so often taught you to despise? Will you accept the name of him who has so cruelly neglected you, and abandon him who has been your guide, your protector, your only friend?"

The tears gushed from his eyes as he spoke, and his whole frame shook with the violence of his grief. I threw myself at his feet, and bedewed his hands with my tears; my heart swelled with a conflict of emotions, which rendered me incapable of utterance. I loved him with the profoundest filial affection; I revered his virtues; I shuddered at the thought of inflicting upon him a wound so mortal. Yet, there was a mysterious fascination in the revelation that had been made to me; a voice within seemed to whisper that I was called to a higher and more eventful sphere; that the blood which flowed in my veins was not of the sluggish current of obscurity, but of the fiery tide of ambition and enterprise. The pride of birth, which I had so often condemned as a childish prejudice, now asserted its dominion over my soul; I felt a strange, wild yearning to behold the dying man who claimed me as his offspring, to hear from his own lips the story of my origin, and to mingle, at last, among those whom fate and nature had destined to be my equals.

"Do not ask me to decide at this moment," I at length articulated, amidst the suffocating sobs that rose in my throat; "my brain is on fire; I know not what I feel. Would that I had never received this accursed message! Would that I might remain forever in this humble retreat, the companion of your solitude, the child of your affection! But he is dying—he calls for me—and can I refuse to obey the last summons of a father? Oh, do not weep, my dear, my only friend! Let me go to him; let me hear his confession; let me look upon his face; and then, if he demands that I should remain with him, I will not—I cannot—abandon you."

I left him buried in an agony of tears, and, wrapping my cloak around me, issued forth into the stormy night. The wind raved with redoubled fury, as if it would bar my passage to the halls of my ancestors; the rain descended in torrents, and the darkness was so profound that I could scarcely discern the rugged pathway that led across the moor. But I heeded not the tempest; a stronger passion than fear urged me onward; my bosom was a prey to contending elements, wilder and more tumultuous than those which raged without. I traversed the dreary waste, now stumbling over the fragments of rock, now plunging through the miry bogs, until at length the towers of Ashcroft Hall loomed through the obscurity, like the phantoms of some gigantic vision.

I was ushered into a spacious chamber, dimly lighted by a solitary wax taper, which threw a flickering and melancholy gleam upon the rich tapestries and gilded furniture. Upon a sumptuous couch lay the dying nobleman; his features, sharp and attenuated by disease, bore the stamp of a haughty beauty, which time and suffering had not wholly effaced. As I approached the bedside, he fixed upon me his large, dark eyes, gleaming with the intense light of approaching dissolution, and a smile of terrible exultation passed across his livid lips.

"You have come," he said, in a hollow and broken voice, raising himself with a painful effort upon his elbow; "you have come to receive the inheritance of my shame and my repentance. Look upon me, Edgar; behold in me the author of your being, the man who, in the madness of his youth, sacrificed the mother of his child to the demon of worldly pride. She died—God of Heaven! she died of a broken heart—and I lived on, a miserable spectacle of hardened guilt, denying my offspring, and feasting on the hollow praises of a world, whose esteem I would now purchase with the relinquishment of every acre that I possess. But the hour of retribution is at hand; the King of Terrors is upon my track; and I must repair, as far as lies in my feeble power, the wrongs I have inflicted. You are my son—the sole heir of my name and fortune; and I summon you to claim them, to assert your rights, and to take the station which belongs to you among the proud and powerful of the land."

He paused, exhausted by the effort of speaking, and his eyes wandered restlessly around the apartment, as if they sought to pierce the veil of the invisible world. I stood motionless and silent, overwhelmed by the tremendous weight of the communication; the conviction of my strange destiny forced itself upon my mind with a clearness that left no room for doubt, and I felt as if a new and mighty being had been born within me. Yet, even in that moment of exultation, a bitter pang shot through my heart, as the image of the lonely curate, weeping in his humble parsonage, rose before my mental vision. I thought of his devotion, his affection, his sublime self-denial; and I felt that, in accepting the splendid inheritance which was offered to me, I should be guilty of a baseness which would render me unworthy of the name I was about to assume.

"My lord," I replied, in a voice trembling with emotion, "I hear you; I believe you; I acknowledge the tie that binds us. But I cannot—I dare not—accept your bounty upon the terms you propose. There is one, in yonder humble dwelling, who has been to me more than a father; whose love has been the sole solace of my life; whose heart will break if I desert him. I must not—I will not—abandon him. Let me share your fortune, if you will; let me bear your name; but let me also discharge the sacred debt I owe to him who reared me in obscurity, and taught me the lessons of virtue and wisdom."

The nobleman gazed at me with an expression of mingled astonishment and admiration; his stern features relaxed into a look of softer feeling; and, extending his withered hand, he murmured, in a faint and broken accent, "You are, indeed, my son; the blood of the Ashcrofts flows in your veins, and with it the high and generous spirit that distinguishes them. Go, then; comfort your benefactor; tell him that you are still his child; and when I am no more, return to claim the inheritance which I bequeath to you, with the assurance that you will use it nobly, and prove yourself worthy of the name you bear."

I pressed his hand to my lips, and rushed from the apartment, from the house, into the free and tempestuous air. The storm had subsided; the clouds were breaking away; and a few stars twinkled faintly through the rifts, shedding a melancholy radiance over the scene. I turned my steps towards the parsonage, with a heart lighter, yet sadder, than it had ever been before; I felt that I had passed through a fiery trial, and had emerged from it purified and ennobled; but I knew also that the calm and happy days of my obscurity were gone forever, and that I was entering upon a new career, fraught with danger, with temptation, and with sorrow. Yet, come what might, I was resolved to bear myself as became the son of such a father, and the pupil of such a master; and to prove, in the great theater of the world, that the lessons of virtue and disinterestedness, learned in the solitude of Cragsmoor, were not without their fruit.
