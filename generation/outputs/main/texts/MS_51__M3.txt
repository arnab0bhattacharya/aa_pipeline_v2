The autumnal winds swept with a mournful cadence through the venerable elms of Blackwood Chase, bending their ancient boughs until they seemed to bow in sorrowful obeisance to the dying year. I stood alone in the oriel window of the gallery, watching the tempestuous sky as the sun, a lurid and blood-stained orb, sank beneath the Sussex hills, casting a transient and melancholy glory over the dreary expanse of heath and woodland that stretched towards the sea. In three days I was to become the wife of Lord Julian Ashcroft; the thought thrilled my very soul with an ecstasy so wild, so over-powering, that I almost feared my human frame could not sustain the divine burden of such exalted felicity. He was all that is noble, gentle, and heroic; his mind was a temple of purest light, and my love for him was an unreasoning idolatry, a devotion that swallowed up every other sentiment, every other duty, in its absorbing and limitless flame.

I turned from the window to seek him in the library, my heart bounding with the joyful anticipation of his presence. As I traversed the long corridor, my steps were arrested by a low, tremulous sob, which seemed to proceed from the small antechamber adjoining the chapel. The door was partially open; I paused, struck by an involuntary and mysterious awe, and gazed within. It was Eleanor, the governess to my young sister, a young woman of singular and melancholy beauty, whose pale cheeks and deeply-shadowed eyes had often attracted my compassionate notice, though a mysterious reserve had ever kept us distant. She was kneeling beside a small cabinet, upon which lay a miniature portrait, and her whole frame was convulsed by the agony of her grief. "O Theodore!" she murmured, in a voice broken by despair, "thee have I lost, and he who slew thee walks the earth in honour, while thy sister is a solitary mourner, weaving the web of her wretched existence in the very house of thy murderer!"

A cold shiver ran through my veins; an indefinable terror took possession of my senses. I entered the room; she started up, and attempted to conceal the portrait, but I gently, though firmly, took it from her unresisting grasp. It was the likeness of a youth of surpassing beauty, with dark, intellectual eyes, and a brow of noble expansion. "Who is this?" I demanded, my voice trembling with a nameless apprehension; "and who is he whom you accuse of murder?" She gazed at me with a wild, haggard stare, as if struggling with the phantoms of her own tortured mind; then, as if some irresistible impulse forced the confession from her labouring breast, she spoke. "It is my brother," she cried, "my beloved, my murdered brother! And he who slew him—oh, that I should live to speak the words!—is Lord Julian Ashcroft, the man whom you, alas! are destined to marry!"

The room reeled around me; the floor seemed to sink beneath my feet, and I clutched the nearest chair for support. She proceeded, with hurried and impassioned eloquence, to unfold the dreadful tale. Five years before, in a distant province of France, Julian, in a moment of misguided and passionate resentment, had conceived himself insulted by Theodore, a young man of humble but honourable birth, who was defending the reputation of his sister from the calumnious slanders of a profligate associate of Julian's. A duel ensued; Julian was a master of the sword, and Theodore, brave but unskilled, fell, pierced through the heart. It was, as Julian had often vaguely hinted to me, a dark passage in his youth, a deed of blood which had cast a shadow over his life; but I had never known the victim was a brother to the gentle being who now stood before me, a living monument of his fatal wrath.

When she had ceased, I remained for a space incapable of speech or motion. The idol of my worship was shattered; the radiant image of my hero was transformed into a stained and blood-stained murderer. Yet, even in that first burst of agony, a voice within me whispered of extenuating circumstances, of the false honour of the age, of a soul misled by fiery passion rather than innate cruelty. But the anguish of Eleanor, her desolation, her wrongs, rose up before me with overwhelming force. What was my love compared to the sacred claims of kindred blood, of injured innocence, of a sister's undying grief? I felt that I could not, must not, become the wife of him who had bereaved her of her only earthly stay.

I sought Julian in the library. He was seated by the fire, his noble countenance irradiated by a serene and thoughtful expression, his eyes bent on the glowing embers with a look of profound and pleasing reverie. At my entrance he rose, and advanced to meet me with that ineffable tenderness which had ever distinguished his manner towards me; but as he marked my pallid cheeks, my swollen eyes, and the wild disorder of my dress, a sudden apprehension seized him. "Clara!" he exclaimed, "what has befallen? You are ill—you have been weeping. Speak, I conjure you, by our mutual love!"

I placed the miniature in his hand. He gazed upon it for a moment, and a deadly paleness overspread his features; his limbs trembled, and he covered his face with his hands, groaning in the bitterness of his spirit. "Then you know all," he at length articulated, in a hollow and broken voice. "You know the dark and terrible secret of my life. I have never concealed from you that a shadow rested on my past; but I dared not name the deed, lest it should forfeit your esteem, and drive me from your presence. It was a fearful error, Clara; I believed him a villain, and I slew him in what I deemed a just defence of injured innocence; but when too late I learned his purity, his heroism, and my heart has never known a moment's peace since that fatal night."

His words, his accents of profound remorse, his appealing and agonized looks, thrilled through my soul with renewed and conflicting emotions. I remembered his virtues, his unwearied benevolence, the exalted nature of his mind; and I felt that to abandon him now, to rend his wounded spirit by a final separation, would be to drive him to despair, perhaps to self-destruction. Yet Eleanor's wrongs demanded a sacrifice. "Julian," I said, and my voice, though trembling, was firm, "I cannot become your wife while Eleanor remains in this house, an unredressed sufferer by your hand. You must repair, as far as possible, the evil you have done. You must acknowledge your error to her, you must devote your life to her service, and you must prove by your future conduct that your repentance is sincere."

He raised his head, and a ray of hope illuminated his tearful eyes. "I will do it," he said; "I will humble myself in the dust before her; I will be a brother to her, a protector, a friend. Only do not cast me off, Clara; do not deprive me of the only being who can make life endurable."

The storm without had now increased to a hurricane; the rain beat against the casements with a fierce and relentless fury, and the wind howled through the chimneys like a legion of evil spirits. The sublime and terrific tumult of nature seemed to echo the tumult within my breast; and as I stood there, gazing on the agonized countenance of the man I adored, and listening to the roar of the tempest, I felt how frail, how uncertain are all human joys, and how close upon the fairest scenes of earthly happiness broods the dark shadow of destiny.
