Of all the singular cases which passed through the hands of Mr. Hamilton Thorpe during the years in which I enjoyed his intimate confidence, there is none which so completely illustrates his extraordinary command over the hidden threads of human motive as that which arose in the autumn of 1893. The affair was conducted in absolute privacy at the express request of the First Sea Lord, and it is only now, after the lapse of years and the retirement of the chief actors, that I am permitted to lay the facts before the public.

It was upon a raw November morning that Miss Eleanor Vane was shown into our chambers in Vere Street. She was a young woman of remarkable composure, though the pallor of her cheeks and a certain restless movement of her gloved fingers betrayed the depth of her emotion. Her errand concerned her fiancé, Mr. Arthur Pendlebury, a junior clerk in the Naval Intelligence Department at Whitehall.

Pendlebury stood accused of a grave breach of trust. A confidential memorandum, detailing the coaling stations and cruiser assignments of the Mediterranean Fleet, had been offered for sale to a foreign agent by an intermediary. The intermediary had been arrested, and his confession had traced the document directly to Pendlebury’s desk. The young man, however, protested his innocence with the most passionate earnestness. He swore that the report had never left his sight from the moment it was placed before him until the moment he returned it to the departmental safe. He was presently suspended from his duties, and a public examination before the Treasury commissioners was imminent.

"The evidence is entirely circumstantial, Mr. Thorpe," said Miss Vane, her voice steady but low. "Yet it is so strong that I fear no one will believe him. Arthur is a man of the highest honour. He could not have sold the report."

"You have the details of the accusation?" Thorpe asked, tapping his long, thin fingers upon the arm of his chair.

I produced the sheaf of papers which Miss Vane had brought, for she had already forwarded them to me by the morning post. Thorpe ran his eye over them with the swift, predatory glance which I knew so well.

"The intermediary, a man named Cawley, states that he received the document on the evening of the fourth, folded within a copy of the *Evening Standard*," Thorpe read aloud. "He handed it to his principal, who copied it in his rooms at Charing Cross before returning it. Cawley swears he recognised the departmental stamp. Now, Mr. Pendlebury admits that he had the report in his possession on the afternoon of the fourth?"

"He had it from three o'clock until half-past five," Miss Vane replied. "He was alone in his office, copying certain extracts for his superior. He declares that no one entered the room, and that he carried the report back to the safe himself."

Thorpe leaned back and closed his eyes, a sure sign that his mental machinery was running at full speed. "The room," said he, "was it upon the ground floor?"

"No. It is upon the second, looking out upon the inner courtyard."

"And the window?"

"Open, owing to the heat of the afternoon, but guarded by an iron bar. Arthur is certain of it."

"Tell me," Thorpe continued, opening his eyes with a sudden flash of intentness, "does Mr. Pendlebury share his office with any other person?"

Miss Vane hesitated. "There is a copying clerk, a man named Gruber, who occupies a desk in the same room. But Gruber was absent on the afternoon in question. He had been given leave to visit a dentist."

"Ah! A dentist," murmured Thorpe, with the faintest suggestion of a smile. "A most useful profession. And this Gruber—he is a recent appointment?"

"Quite recent. He came to the department only three weeks ago."

"One or two more questions, Miss Vane, and I will detain you no longer. Does Mr. Pendlebury wear spectacles?"

"No. His sight is excellent."

"And Gruber—what manner of man is he?"

"He is a stout, middle-aged German, very precise in his habits. He is remarkably quick with the copying machine."

"And has he any peculiar habit—any trick of manner which has attracted your notice, or that of Mr. Pendlebury?"

Miss Vane looked surprised. "Now that you mention it, Arthur has spoken of his whistling. Gruber whistles continually—soft, tuneless little runs, quite unlike an English air. Arthur finds it irritating."

Thorpe sprang to his feet with the abrupt energy which always heralded decisive action. "Miss Vane," said he, "I think I can promise you that your fiancé’s name will be cleared. Watson—forgive me, Merritt—have your hat and coat ready. We are going to Whitehall."

It was past noon when we stood in the long, bare corridor of the departmental building. Thorpe had obtained admission through a note to Sir Hubert Leatherby, the permanent secretary, a harassed and apprehensive gentleman who conducted us at once to the vacant office. It was a plain room, containing two desks, a large copying-press, and a tall iron safe. The window, as Miss Vane had stated, was barred.

Thorpe walked straight to the copying-press. He examined the roller, the dampening pad, and the tray beneath. Then he dropped upon his knees and searched the floor with a pocket lens, moving inch by inch from the press to the desk which Pendlebury had occupied. Presently he rose, holding between his fingers a small, crumpled pellet of paper.

"What is it?" asked Sir Hubert, leaning forward.

Thorpe unfolded it with delicate care. It was a fragment of tissue paper, such as is used for tracing, and upon it were a few faint impressions in violet ink—the characters of the confidential report.

"It is a carbon impression," said Thorpe, "taken, I fancy, not from the original, but from the copying-press itself. You will observe, Sir Hubert, that your copying-press is of the old roller type. When a wet sheet is placed against a document and passed through, a reverse impression is left upon the damp cloth. If that cloth is not properly cleaned, a second sheet of tissue, laid upon it and pressed, will receive a faint but legible copy. A man of ordinary intelligence could reproduce an entire page in this manner without ever touching the original."

"But the original never left my sight!" cried Pendlebury, who had been sent for and now stood trembling in the doorway.

"Precisely," said Thorpe. "You returned it to the safe yourself. But you used the copying-press upon it first. And Gruber, returning from his fictitious visit to the dentist, found the damp cloth still warm in the press. He had only to lay his tissue paper upon it, turn the handle, and walk out with the copy in his pocket. The original remained inviolate. Your guilt, my dear sir, consists only in not washing the roller."

Sir Hubert stared. "But the evidence against Pendlebury—Cawley’s statement—"

"Cawley dealt with a man who hummed or whistled continually," Thorpe broke in. "You will find, if you examine the intermediary more closely, that he described his acquaintance as a foreigner of stout build. Gruber whistled, not for amusement, but to cover the sound of the copying-press handle. It is a habit common to those who wish to disguise a mechanical noise. The whistling was not a trick of manner; it was a tool of the trade."

Within two days Gruber was arrested at a lodging-house in Poplar. Among his effects were found not only copies of the Mediterranean report, but tracings of two other departmental papers which had passed through the press during his three weeks of employment. He was tried at the Old Bailey in the following January, found guilty, and sentenced to a term of penal servitude. Pendlebury was restored to his position, and the department adopted a new system of roller-cleaning which, one may hope, has rendered such exploits a thing of the past.
