The compartment smelled of damp wool and coal smoke, a scent that clung to the air like a shroud. Eleanor sat with her hands folded tightly in the lap of her black dress, the fabric of her gloves creaking softly with every contraction of her fingers. She had not wanted to take the train. The telegraph had arrived three days ago—a sharp, mechanical rattle of the ticker that had silenced the parlour—and the logistics of death had immediately begun to dictate the rhythm of her life. To get to Yorkshire, one must go to King’s Cross. To get to King’s Cross, one must take a cab. To get to the funeral, one must endure four hours of rattling steel and the forced proximity of strangers.

The door slid open with a violent clatter, breaking the sanctuary of her solitude. A young man stepped in, dragging a kit bag that looked far too heavy for his slender frame. He was dressed in the khaki of the New Army, the uniform still possessing a certain crispness that suggested he had not been in the trenches long, or perhaps he was on his way back to them. He slammed the door shut, shutting out the noise of the platform, and looked around the carriage. His eyes, a startlingly pale blue, flickered over Eleanor. He paused, noting the black of her dress, the veil pinned to her hat, the rigid set of her jaw.

“Begging your pardon, ma’am,” he said. His voice was rough, a Midlands accent sandpapered by shouting over engines or gunfire. “Didn’t mean to intrude.”

“It is a public carriage, soldier,” Eleanor said. Her voice sounded thin to her own ears. “There is no intrusion.”

He stowed his bag on the rack above, his movements efficient and practiced, then sat down on the seat opposite her. He kept his knees together, hands resting on his thighs, staring at the floor. The train gave a mighty shudder, steam hissing against the windows like escaping ghosts, and the station began to slide backward.

They sat in silence for the first twenty minutes. Eleanor watched the grimy backs of London terraces give way to the grey industrial sprawl of the Midlands. She thought of Clara. She thought of the last letter she had received, written in a hand that had grown shaky and small, describing nothing but the view from the bedroom window and the taste of the broth the nurse had brought. Clara had never mentioned the pain. She had never mentioned the illness. She had only written of the weather.

“It’s turned colder,” the soldier said suddenly.

Eleanor blinked, pulling her gaze away from the window. “Has it?”

“Feels like it,” he said, nodding toward the glass. “The sky’s got that iron look to it. Heavy.”

“Yes,” Eleanor agreed. “It is heavy. It looks like snow, though it is too early in the season.”

“Nature doesn’t keep to the calendar much these days,” he said. He looked out at the fields as they sped past. The November landscape was a wash of browns and greys, the trees skeletal and stark against the low clouds. “It’s a grim sort of country up here.”

“It is stark,” Eleanor corrected gently. “But it has its own beauty. If you look at the way the stone walls follow the contours of the hills.”

He turned his head, studying her with renewed interest. “You’re from up there? The North?”

“Near Northallerton,” she said. “I have been living in London for ten years, but I am going home.”

“Home,” he repeated, as if tasting the word. “I’m heading home too. Just for a week. Leave.”

“That must be a relief.”

“It is,” he said, though he didn't smile. “It’s quiet there. Different kind of quiet than the front.”

The train rattled over a set of points, the rhythmic *clack-clack* filling the silence. Eleanor found herself studying the landscape more intently than she had in years. She pointed toward a cluster of farm buildings nestled in a hollow of the land. “See there? The way the smoke rises from the chimney? It looks like it’s struggling against the wind.”

The soldier leaned forward, peering through the glass. “It’s fighting to get up,” he said. “That’s what it looks like. Fighting to get up and away.”

“Perhaps,” Eleanor murmured.

She watched him then, really watched him. She saw the way his hand trembled slightly when he reached up to adjust his collar. She saw the dark circles under his eyes, the shadows that seemed to have been painted onto his skin with soot. He was very young. Too young to have eyes that looked like that. She thought of Clara’s eyes in the end—glassy and distant, looking at something Eleanor couldn’t see.

“You’re travelling alone, ma’am?” he asked.

“I am.”

“Not a good time of year for it. The weather.”

“The weather is indifferent to my schedule,” she said, a sharpness entering her tone that she hadn't intended. She softened it. “But you are right. It is a bleak day for a journey.”

They spoke of the fields again. The soldier pointed out a flock of crows huddled in a bare oak tree. “They look like they’re waiting for something,” he said.

“Waiting for the thaw,” Eleanor said.

“No,” he said quietly. “They’re just waiting.”

Eleanor felt a sudden, irrational urge to tell him. To open her mouth and say, *My sister is dead. She died on Tuesday morning at half-past eight. I am going to bury her in the frozen ground of St. Jude’s churchyard, and I do not know how to live in a world where she does not exist.* The words pressed against the back of her teeth, heavy and hot. But she looked at his hands—calloused, dirty at the knuckles, the hands of a man who dug trenches or cleaned rifles—and she swallowed them back. He had his own ghosts riding in the carriage with them. She could see them sitting on the empty seats, whispering in the draft.

“Will it rain before we reach York, do you think?” she asked.

He looked at the sky again. “No. It’ll hold off. It’s saving itself. It wants to bury everything under a white sheet.”

“A white sheet,” Eleanor repeated.

“Clean slate,” he said.

“But the ground is still hard,” she said. “Nothing can grow yet.”

“No,” he agreed. “Not yet.”

The train slowed as it approached the junction at Doncaster. The platform was crowded with soldiers, nurses, and people rushing to make connections. The noise of the station swelled—shouting porters, hissing steam, the cry of a child. Eleanor watched the crowds, the chaotic swirl of life, and felt a profound sense of disconnection. She was a stone dropped into a river; the water flowed around her, but she could not move.

The soldier stood up to retrieve his kit bag as the train began to move again, picking up speed. “I’ll be getting off at the next stop,” he said. “Boroughbridge. It’s a short walk from there.”

“I know it,” Eleanor said.

He hesitated, his hand on the strap of his bag. He looked at her, and for a moment, the polite mask of the stranger fell away. The pale blue eyes were raw, exposed. “The countryside,” he said, his voice dropping to a near whisper. “It looks different when you know you might not see it again. The green of the grass. The way the light hits the water in the streams. You notice the little things.”

“I imagine you do,” Eleanor said.

“You notice the things you’d miss,” he said. “If you didn’t come back.”

Eleanor’s heart clenched. She knew then, with a certainty that defied logic, what he was telling her. He wasn't just talking about the beauty of Yorkshire. He was talking about the mud of the Somme. He was talking about the fragility of breath. He was telling her that he was terrified, and that he was ashamed of his terror, and that he was trying to memorize the world before it was taken from him.

“And you notice the things you can’t take with you,” Eleanor replied. She kept her voice steady, calm, like the surface of a frozen pond. “You notice the silence.”

He nodded slowly. He understood. He knew she wasn't talking about the silence of the carriage. She was talking about the silence of a house that was once full of voices. She was telling him that she, too, was leaving something behind in the earth, and that the world had become a foreign place.

The train began to brake, the brakes squealing in protest. “Boroughbridge,” the conductor called from the corridor.

The soldier pulled his cap down firmly over his brow. “Good day to you, ma’am.”

“Good day, soldier. I hope the weather holds for you.”

“I hope so too,” he said.

He slid the door open and stepped out onto the platform. Eleanor watched him through the window as the train began to move. He stood perfectly still for a moment, looking back at the carriage, his breath forming small clouds in the cold air. Then he turned and walked into the crowd, swallowed by the grey coats and the steam.

Eleanor sat back. The compartment felt larger now, emptier. She looked at the seat opposite where he had sat. She smoothed the fabric of her skirt. The train gathered speed, rushing northward toward the funeral, toward the cold churchyard and the white sheet of the sky. She pressed her hand against the cold glass of the window. She had not asked his name. He had not asked hers. But as the train carried her toward her grief, she felt, for the first time in three days, that she was not entirely alone in the dark.
