Miss Euphemia Clawthorne came to my office in Tooley Street on a Tuesday in November, bringing with her the damp chill of the river and a countenance marked by sleepless dread. She was a woman of perhaps eight-and-twenty, plainly dressed, her hands reddened from lye and cold. Her father, the Reverend Josiah Clawthorne, was the incumbent of St. Bede's, a crumbling parish of grey stone and damp mortar tucked into the lanes behind Bermondsey Wall, where the Thames at high tide sent its breath of sewage and coal tar through every cracked window. For eleven years he had preached there with a fury that shook the rafters, a man of iron constitution and unshakable conviction. Now he sat in his study like a figure carved from tallow, and would not speak of what he had seen.

She laid the matter before me with the practised economy of someone who had rehearsed the telling many times. Three weeks prior, on the last night of October, her father had gone to visit a dying parishioner in Nag's Head Court, a narrow rookery of lodging-houses and chandler's shops that bent toward the river along a lane called Saviour's Stairs. He had gone at the request of a note delivered to the vicarage door, written in a hand he did not recognise, begging him to come and administer the last rites to a sick man named Trundle. He had returned two hours later than he should have, and when Euphemia opened the door to him, he walked past her as though she were a stranger. His face was the colour of unwhitewashed plaster, his lips grey, his clerical collar dark with sweat. He went directly to his study and locked the door. He had not ascended the pulpit since. The curate, a timid fellow named Mr. Pellow, had been conducting the services, and the parishioners were beginning to talk.

Each night, she told me, the same ritual unfolded. At half past ten her father would light the lamp at his desk, take out a sheet of the heavy cream paper he used for parish correspondence, and write. She had once crept to the door and listened, and heard only the scratching of the nib, steady and unbroken, for upwards of an hour. Then would come the scrape of a match, and the particular silence that attends the consumption of paper by fire. By morning the grate would contain only a fine grey ash, indistinguishable from any other, and her father would be sitting in his chair by the window, staring at the wall of the churchyard with eyes that seemed to see nothing at all.

I asked her whether she had read any of the letters before they were burned. She hesitated, and a flush rose in her cheeks. Once, she admitted, she had intercepted the letter before he could set it alight, by walking into the study on the pretence of bringing him tea. She had seen only a few lines before he snatched it from her hand. The words she recalled were these: "I beg you to understand that I did not go down those stairs of my own choosing, and that what I found at the bottom was not what I had been told I would find. It was a face, and the face knew me." She had not been able to glean more. He had taken the letter, burned it then and there in the hearth, and afterward locked his door each night.

I agreed to make enquiries, though I told her plainly that I held little hope of discovering what had passed in a court of lodging-houses by the river, where people kept their own counsel and distrusted the questions of strangers. She pressed a sovereign into my hand, more money than I expected her to possess, and left without further ceremony.

The following evening I crossed Tooley Street and made my way into the lanes behind Bermondsey Wall, where the buildings lean together overhead and the cobbles run with water that has no certain source. Saviour's Stairs was a narrow passage, barely wide enough for two men to walk abreast, descending in broken steps toward the foreshore. The houses on either side were ancient, their timbers swollen and blackened, their upper stories jutting out over the lane so that the sky was reduced to a thin grey ribbon. At the bottom of the stairs, where the passage opened onto a small court, I found a public house called the Broken Anchor, its sign hanging from a single rusted chain. Inside, the air was thick with tobacco and the smell of boiled beef. A few men sat drinking in silence. The woman behind the bar, a heavy-featured person with arms like a stevedore, watched me without expression.

I ordered a glass of gin and sat near the fire. After a time I made enquiry about a man named Trundle, and was met with the particular silence that I had been advised to expect in such places. The woman behind the bar wiped the counter with a rag that had seen longer service than the British Empire. She said there was no Trundle in Nag's Head Court, nor had been in the fifteen years she had kept the house. I pressed her, and she repeated herself with a flatness that discouraged further questioning, but added, as though it were an afterthought, that the Reverend Clawthorne had been seen on Saviour's Stairs that night, coming out of number four, which was the last house on the east side of the court, the one whose back wall formed part of the old river wall. She said this as though it were a detail of no consequence, and turned to serve another customer.

I finished my gin and went out into the court. Number four was indistinguishable from its neighbours: four stories of blackened brick, a door that had once been green, and a single window on the ground floor that was boarded from within. I knocked and received no answer. I knocked again, and after some minutes the door was opened by a woman of indeterminate age, thin and pale, with the particular pallor of those who live in rooms without daylight. She wore a shawl over her head and held the door in such a way that I could see nothing of the room behind her. I asked her about the reverend's visit. She said nothing for a long moment, and then she told me that no one had sent for the reverend, that no one named Trundle lived there, and that if the reverend had come to number four, he had come of his own accord, or else had been led.

I asked her what she meant by led. She looked past me, toward the river, and said that there were those in the court who remembered the reverend from years past, from before he was a reverend, when he was a young man in the employ of a firm of ship-chandlers that had once kept a warehouse on the wharf at the foot of Saviour's Stairs. She said this carefully, as though selecting each word from a limited and dangerous supply. I pressed her further, and she told me that the warehouse had burned in the great fire of 1862, the one that had taken out the buildings between Tooley Street and the river, and that three men had died in it, two of them known to her, the third a stranger whose name had never been established. She said that the young man who would become the reverend had been among those who pulled the bodies from the wreckage, and that afterward he had turned to the Church with a suddenness that surprised everyone who knew him.

I returned to my office that night and sat with the information I had gathered, turning it over as one turns a coin in the palm, feeling for the edge that does not belong. The following morning I went to the Bermondsey parish registers and found that the fire of 1862 had indeed been recorded, and that three men had perished: two watermen of known address, and a third, unidentified, whose body had been recovered from the cellar of the warehouse. The register noted that the reverend, then a layman of twenty years, had been among the witnesses. I found nothing further.

I went to St. Bede's that afternoon. The vicarage was a narrow house of yellow brick attached to the church, its windows grimed with the soot of decades. Euphemia met me at the door and took me to the study, where her father sat in his chair by the window. He was a large man, diminished, his clerical collar loose about a neck that had grown thin. His hands rested on the arms of the chair, and they were the hands of someone who had once done manual labour, broad-knuckled and scarred. He looked at me without expression, and I saw in his eyes the particular vacancy of a man who is waiting for something he cannot name.

I told him what I had learned, and I asked him directly what he had found at the bottom of the stairs in number four. He was silent for a long time, and when he spoke, his voice was so low that I leaned forward in my chair to hear him. He said that the note had been written in the hand of a man named Trundle, who had been the warehouse foreman in 1862, and who had died in the fire. He said that he had gone to number four believing that someone wished to make a confession regarding the fire, and that he had found the room at the back of the house prepared as though for a meeting: a table, two chairs, a lamp. He said that he had sat and waited, and that after some time a door at the rear of the room had opened, and someone had entered, and that he had known the face at once, though he had not seen it in twenty-eight years. He said that the face was the face of the third man, the one whose name had never been established, and that the third man had spoken to him and told him things that he could not repeat, and that he had written them down each night because he feared that if he did not, he would forget them, and that forgetting would be worse than remembering.

I asked him what the things were. He looked at me then with an expression I cannot adequately describe, and he said that the third man had told him how the fire had started, and who had set it, and that the reverend himself had known, and had known for twenty-eight years, and that his turning to the Church had been not a calling but a flight, and that every sermon he had preached had been a letter to a dead man, and that now the dead man had written back. He said he burned the letters because they were addressed to no one living, and because there was no one to send them to who could grant him absolution. He looked at the wall of the churchyard then, and said nothing more, and I understood that the interview was at an end.

I returned to Tooley Street and wrote my report for Miss Clawthorne, setting down the facts as I had gathered them and omitting only those particulars which I judged would serve no purpose save to deepen her distress. I told her that her father had encountered, in the course of his visit, a reminder of a loss sustained in his youth, and that the grief had unsettled him in a manner not uncommon among men of his temperament and years. I advised patience, and rest, and the counsel of a physician. I did not tell her about the letters, or the face, or the dead man who had written back, because I did not know what to make of these things myself, and because there are truths that do not benefit from being spoken aloud, and because the river runs beneath all the streets of Southwark, and the past runs beneath all the present, and some men drown in it not once but every night, and write letters to the drowned, and burn them at dawn, and sit by their windows waiting for an answer that does not come.
