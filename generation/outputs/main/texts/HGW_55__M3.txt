It was in the particularly damp and gloomy November of 1893 that the peculiar nervous affliction of the London horses first manifested itself. I recall the initial rumours in the chronicles of the *Evening Courier*—dismissed, as such things invariably are, as the exaggerated gossip of suburban stable-boys. It was said that the draught-horses in the Deptford yards had developed a strange, vacant stare, followed by a sudden, violent repulsion towards the smell of iron. Within a fortnight, however, the phenomenon had spread with the rapidity of a gas explosion. The affliction was not merely physical, but a profound and terrible psychosis. The animals became subject to acute delusions of terror, imagining the very cobbles beneath their hooves to be yawning chasms. They would bolt wildly at the sight of their own shadows, drag omnibuses into shop fronts, and collapse in shivering heaps of muscular exhaustion, their eyes rolling white with a madness that no veterinary science could pacify.

By the end of the month, London was practically impassable. The streets were a chaotic litter of overturned hansoms, shattered drays, and the broken shafts of tradesmen’s carts. The authorities, acting with their customary blend of energetic panic and administrative muddle, issued directives for the immediate shooting of any beast displaying the primary symptoms. The noise of the police pistols echoed from morning till night down the wet, grey thoroughfares, punctuating the clatter of hooves and the distant bellowing of terrified men.

It was amidst this urban bedlam that I found myself drawn into the affairs of George Blencowe, a man I had known for some years as a driver of a particularly smart hansom cab. Blencowe was a heavily built, melancholic man, possessed of a deep, slow voice and a pragmatic, working-class philosophy that generally precluded any show of sentimentality. Yet he harboured one singular weakness: an extraordinary devotion to his chestnut mare, Bess. She was a fine, upstanding beast, with a coat like polished mahogany and a temperament of such steady reliability that she seemed almost human in her comprehension. To George, she was not merely a tool of his trade, but a partner, a confidante, and the sole beautiful and dependable thing in a life of fog, muddy crossings, and quarrelsome passengers.

When the madness crept into the stables behind the *Red Lion* in Brixton, George found himself confronted with a crisis that entirely shattered his practical composure. Bess had begun to pace her stall, her ears twitching at frequencies no human could hear, her flanks quivering with a nameless dread. But she had not bolted. She had not attacked the brickwork or bitten at her own flesh. She merely stood, trembling violently, looking at George with an expression of such bewildered appeal that his resolution was instantly and irrevocably made. He told me later, with a defensive gruffness, that he would sooner see the whole fabric of the Empire collapse than surrender her to the police pistols.

“How could I let them shoot her?” he said to me, as we sat in the cramped, gas-lit kitchen of his wife’s cousin’s house in Vauxhall. He leaned forward, his great hands gripping his knees. “She wasn’t mad, not in the vicious way. She was just frightened out of her wits by something we couldn’t see. It’s like a man going off his head with a fever; you don’t shoot him, you put him to bed. And I wasn’t going to let them put her down because the Commissioner of Police is in a panic.”

“But the law, George,” I protested, genuinely alarmed by the perilousness of his position. “The law is perfectly clear. Any animal harbouring the contagion is to be destroyed to prevent the spread. They are shooting them in the streets by the dozen. If they find you hiding her, they will lock you up.”

“Let them lock me up, then,” he replied, with a sullen, argumentative idealism that reminded me of a political agitator. “I’ve hidden her where they’ll never think to look. Let the city go to the dogs. I’ve done my duty by my own.”

His secret refuge was a place that, in the ordinary course of events, no sane cabman would have considered: the disused coal cellar beneath the very house in which we sat. The cousin, a Mrs. Gribble, was a frail widow who let lodgings, and she had been induced, partly by George’s earnestness and partly by a substantial monetary consideration, to allow the mare to occupy the subterranean vault. It was an extraordinary arrangement. The cellar was damp, cavernous, and communicated with the pavement above by means of a heavy iron trapdoor, usually employed for the delivery of coals.

I went down to see Bess that evening. The atmosphere was thick with the smell of damp earth and straw, and the only light came from a smoky paraffin lamp that threw gigantic, flickering shadows against the vaulted brickwork. The mare stood tethered to a stout wooden post, her great body rising and falling with quick, uneven respirations. She looked profoundly out of place, a creature of wind and open roads confined in a subterranean dungeon. Yet, at the sound of George’s boots on the flags, her ears pricked forward, and she turned her long head towards him with a soft, tremulous whinny.

He went up to her and laid his broad hand against her neck, murmuring in that slow, deliberate tone he used when driving in the heaviest traffic. “Steady, old girl. Steady, my beauty,” he said. It was a scene of bizarre, almost pathetic domesticity. Here was this stocky, practical man, who had spent his life navigating the relentless, mechanical bustle of the metropolis, reduced to hiding his only treasure in a coal hole while the city tore itself to pieces above his head.

For three weeks, this extraordinary state of affairs continued. Above ground, London was a city under siege by its own beasts. The newspapers, when they could be delivered at all, were filled with lurid accounts of runaway teams, demolished shop fronts, and the suspension of the omnibus services. The streets were left to the pedestrians and the scavengers. Men walked in the middle of the road, a novel experience in a metropolis accustomed to yielding the thoroughfare to hooves and wheels. The silence of the West End, shorn of its countless iron-shod rhythms, was said to be positively eerie.

Down in the cellar, George maintained his vigil. He spent his meagre savings on oats and hay, carrying the provender down through the trapdoor under cover of darkness. He groomed her meticulously, currying the chestnut coat and picking out her hooves, maintaining the routines of the stable as though they were sacred rituals. But the strain was telling upon him. He grew gaunt and irritable, his eyes taking on a restless, hunted look. His wife, a sharp-featured woman who viewed horses merely as a source of income, was profoundly unsympathetic, and their lodgings echoed with bitter recriminations.

“You’re a fool, George Blencowe,” she said to him one evening, as I sat by the fire. “A sentimental fool. They’ll find her, and then where will we be? Transported, I shouldn’t wonder. And for a beast that’s likely lost her mind entirely!”

“She hasn’t lost her mind,” George retorted, striking the table with his fist. “She’s just got a touch of the panic. It’s in the air, isn’t it? The whole city’s gone mad. Why blame the horses for catching it?”

His defence of the mare was absolute. He could not, or would not, admit that her condition was deteriorating. But I, visiting the cellar one afternoon, could see it plainly. The confinement and the strange, subterranean silence were aggravating her nervous disorder. She had begun to sway her head rhythmically from side to side, a monotonous, pendulum motion that is the surest sign of a broken spirit in a captive beast. She started at the slightest noise, her hooves clattering on the stone floor, her eyes wide and glassy with terror. She was, I realized with a sinking heart, going quietly and thoroughly insane in the dark.

I spoke to George about it, as gently as I could, the next day. We were standing by the river, watching a grimy, horseless barge being towed by a stooping, red-faced man on the towpath. The whole aspect of the city seemed to have altered, become harsher and more utilitarian without the familiar, graceful curves of the horses.

“She’s not right, George,” I said. “She’s losing her grip. It isn’t fair to her, keeping her down there. She’s a creature of the open air.”

He did not answer for a long moment. He stared at the muddy water, his jaw set in that stubborn line I knew so well. “She’ll come right,” he muttered. “She just needs time. When this blows over, I’ll have her out in the sun again. You’ll see.”

But it was not to be. The authorities, acting on a tip-off—George suspected his wife, though he never proved it—arrived on a wet Tuesday morning. I was not there, but he recounted the scene to me afterwards with a flat, deadened voice. There were two constables, a police sergeant, and a veterinary surgeon from the municipal board. They had come with a constable’s pistol and a canvas sheet.

George had barred the trapdoor, but they forced it open. He stood before them in the narrow passage, his great fists clenched, his face white with rage and despair. He argued with them, pleaded with them, offered them bribes, and finally threatened them with physical violence. But the sergeant was a calm, professional man, and the vet was merely doing his duty. They overpowered George, pinning him against the damp wall while the vet descended into the cellar.

“And they shot her?” I asked, when he had finished.

“They shot her,” he said. We were sitting in a public house, and he was staring into a glass of gin that he had not touched. “Right there in the dark. I heard her cry out, and then a thud. And then nothing.”

He was never the same man afterwards. He drove a motor-cab when they came into fashion, and made a decent living, but the spark of obstinate, argumentative life had gone out of him. He had discovered, as so many do, that it is a terrible thing to love something intensely in a world that is built upon practical expediency. The madness passed, as these things do, and the horses returned to the streets, but for George Blencowe, the heart had gone out of the metropolis forever. He would sit on his driving seat, staring ahead through the windscreen at the grey procession of traffic, and I knew he was thinking of a chestnut mare in a darkened cellar, waiting for a deliverance that never came.
