The letter lay upon my desk like a challenge flung down, its black seal broken, its every line an accusation of ingratitude. I had read it thrice, and thrice had the words “handsome salary,” “eligible situation,” “respectable English family” risen up before me, cold and correct as the columns of a ledger. Outside the tall windows of the _salle d’étude_, the October rain drove slantwise against the glass, and the wind, finding some crevice in the old masonry, moaned a low, persistent note, as if it too pleaded a cause I would not hear.

I am not, by nature, a woman of indecision. When I came to this Flemish city, five years since, a stranger with scarce a friend and but a few words of the harsh, clattering tongue that surrounded me, I came with a purpose fixed as iron in my soul: to earn my bread, to prove my worth, and to let no man nor circumstance turn me from the path I had chosen. I had found the work hard, the discipline severe, the girls at first suspicious, the masters exacting. Yet I had conquered. I had made myself understood; I had made myself respected. I had risen from the drudgery of elementary instruction to the charge of the senior class, and it was whispered among the mistresses—nay, I had heard it from the lips of the Directrice herself—that the order and the tone of the whole establishment owed more to Mademoiselle Everard than to any other single person beneath that roof.

And now England called me back. England, with her green lanes, her grey skies, her familiar speech, her higher wages, and—I confess it—her nearer prospect of that independence which is the dream of every woman who must make her own way in a world that grudges her the making. I should be nearer my sister, who was lately married and settled in a dull little town in the Midlands, and who wrote to me long, affectionate, unsatisfying letters, full of the price of coal and the teething of infants. I should be, in short, respectable, and perhaps at last at rest.

Why, then, did my hand tremble as I folded the letter? Why did I rise and walk to the window, and stand there watching the rain till my eyes ached, seeing nothing of the wet roofs and the grey, hurrying clouds, but only the faces of those who were to be left behind?

I knew the reason. I had known it before the letter came. It stood before me now in the person of a girl of sixteen, one Hélène Vandenberghe, the eldest daughter of a merchant of Ghent, a pupil whose intellect I had done my utmost to cultivate, and whose heart—I had flattered myself—I had not wholly failed to touch.

She came to me that very afternoon, as the dusk was gathering and the lamps were being lit in the long corridor. She did not knock, but entered with that noiseless, gliding step which I had noticed in her from the first, and stood before my desk with her hands clasped before her and her dark eyes fixed on my face.

“Mademoiselle,” she said, and her voice was low and steady, though I saw her lip quiver, “they tell me you are going away.”

I laid down my pen. “They tell you the truth, Hélène. I have received an offer of employment in my own country, and I am inclined to accept it.”

“Inclined!” she repeated, and a flash of something like anger lit her pale cheek. “You say _inclined_, as if it were a matter of indifference. Do you not know—have you not seen—” She stopped, and drew a hard breath. “We cannot spare you, Mademoiselle. The others may not say it; they are shy, and you are sometimes cold to them; but they feel it. Marie has wept all the morning. Louise cannot construe her Tacitus for thinking of it. And I—” She paused again, and then went on with a sudden, passionate directness that startled me. “I have no mother, Mademoiselle. You know that. You have been more to me than any teacher. If you go, there is no one left who cares whether I am wise or foolish, good or wicked.”

The words went through me like a sword. I had not thought myself so necessary. I had not thought—God forgive me—that in the dry discharge of my duty I had bound any heart so closely to my own.

“Hélène,” I said, and my voice was not so steady as I could have wished, “you overrate my importance. There are others here who will instruct you as well—nay, better. And you will forget me in a month.”

“Never!” she cried, with a vehemence that was almost fierce. “You do not know us, Mademoiselle, if you think so. You English are so cold—so self-possessed. You think because you do not show your feelings that you have none. But we have seen you—I have seen you—when you thought yourself unobserved. I have seen you stand at that window on a winter evening, looking out at the snow, with such a look on your face as I would not forget if I lived a hundred years. It was not the look of a woman who had no heart. It was the look of one who was lonely—lonely as I am—and who would not confess it.”

I turned away. The girl had touched the secret spring. I had been lonely. I had been proud. I had wrapped myself in my work as in a cloak, and told myself that it was enough. And now this child, with her clear, unerring instinct, had stripped the cloak away and shown me my own nakedness.

“You are a strange girl, Hélène,” I said at last, “to speak to your mistress so.”

“I am not speaking to my mistress,” she answered. “I am speaking to my friend—if I may call you so. You have never let me call you so. You have been kind, but you have held me at arm’s length. Why? Was it because you were afraid to let me come near?”

I could not answer her. I stood silent, and the silence was more eloquent than any words.

At that moment the door opened, and Mynheer Van der Meulen, the second master, entered with a bundle of papers in his hand. He was a man of forty, grave, silent, with a face that seldom changed, and a manner that I had always found somewhat dry and pedantic. He had never sought my society; I had never sought his. We had worked side by side for five years in a kind of neutral civility, and I had thought him as cold as the stones of the schoolroom floor.

He stopped short at sight of Hélène’s flushed face and my own, I doubt not, scarcely less disturbed.

“I beg your pardon,” he said, in his slow, careful English. “I did not know you were engaged. I will return at another time.”

“No,” I said, “come in. Hélène was just going.”

But Hélène did not go. She turned to him with a sudden appeal.

“Mynheer,” she said, “Mademoiselle is going to leave us. Will you not tell her that she must not?”

He looked at her, then at me. A curious change came over his face—a lightening, a softening, as of some hard frost yielding to a sudden thaw. He set down his papers on the table, and for a moment he seemed to struggle with himself.

“I have no right to detain Mademoiselle Everard,” he said at last, “against her inclination. But if I might speak as a colleague—as one who has had the honour of working with her for many years—I would say that her departure would be a loss to this school which I should feel very deeply.”

He spoke with difficulty, as if the words were dragged from him against his will. Then he added, in a lower tone, and with a certain awkwardness that was strangely touching: “I had hoped—that is, I had thought—that we might have gone on working together. I have learned much from you, Mademoiselle. More than you know.”

I looked at him. I had never seen him thus before. The mask of the pedant had fallen from him, and beneath it I saw a man—a lonely man, like myself—who had hidden his feelings under a cloak of reserve as thick as my own. And in that moment I understood many things that had puzzled me: his readiness to take the burdensome tasks that I disliked, his silence when I had been sharp with him, the way his eyes had followed me when he thought I was not looking.

The rain beat against the window; the wind moaned in the chimney; the lamp flickered on the table. And I stood there, between the girl who loved me and the man who had not the courage to say he loved me, and felt the whole weight of my lonely, self-sufficient life crumble into dust.

“I will consider of it,” I said at last. “I will not decide to-day.”

Hélène’s face flashed into a radiance that was like sunshine after rain. She caught my hand and pressed it to her lips, and then, without a word, she was gone.

Mynheer Van der Meulen stood looking at me. He did not speak. But his eyes said plainly, “And I?” And I—I turned away, and took up the letter from my desk, and held it over the flame of the lamp.

It burned slowly, with a little crackling sound, and the ash fell upon the table like a grey feather.
