The rain had not ceased for three days, and the drawing room of Mrs. Hester Vaughn’s establishment felt airless with the smell of damp wool and tallow. Miss Clara Bell sat on the edge of the horsehair chair, her gloved hands clasped so tightly in her lap that the kid leather creaked. She was a piano teacher of modest reputation, employed by several respectable families in Marylebone, and she had never before set foot in the premises of a private investigator. The room itself unsettled her: the filing cabinets, the framed maps of London parishes, the ink-stained blotter on the desk, and behind it, the man himself, Mr. Silas Croft, who listened with his chin resting on his steepled fingers and did not interrupt her once.

She had come because of Elara Vance. Elara was seventeen, the sole heiress to the Vance shipping fortune, and since the death of her parents in a railway accident two years prior, she had lived under the guardianship of her uncle, Mr. Aldous Vance, in his townhouse on Wimpole Street. Clara had been engaged to provide Elara with musical instruction twice weekly, a post she had held for eight months without incident. The girl was diligent, possessed of a good ear and a natural touch, and Clara had grown fond of her in the quiet way that teachers do, admiring her pupils without presuming to know them entirely.

The trouble began in October. Clara had arrived for a Thursday lesson to find Elara distracted, sitting at the Broadwood grand with her hands resting on the keys but making no sound. When Clara asked her to begin with the assigned Czerny exercises, Elara instead played a short melody Clara did not recognise. It was simple, almost folk-like, in a minor key, with a descending phrase that turned back on itself like a question left unanswered. Clara assumed the girl had heard it from a street musician or a music hall and asked where it came from. Elara said a stranger hummed it beneath her window at night.

Clara had thought little of it at the time. London was full of sounds, and the bedrooms at the rear of the Wimpole Street house faced the mews, where drivers and tradesmen came and went at all hours. She corrected Elara’s fingering on the Czerny and moved on. But the following Tuesday, Elara played another unfamiliar tune, this one slower and more angular, with intervals that sat strangely in the ear, as though the melody had been written for an instrument other than the piano. Again Clara asked, and again Elara gave the same answer: the stranger beneath her window. He came after midnight, she said, and hummed without words, always a different tune, and she could not see his face because her window was on the second floor and the mews below was dark.

By the third lesson, Clara had grown uneasy. She had brought sheet music to every session, and Elara had not opened a single page. Each time, the girl sat and played something new, something Clara had never heard in any recital hall or parlour, and each piece was unlike the last. One was brisk and rhythmic, almost a march, but with a harmonic strangeness that made the chords feel lopsided. Another was languid, with long held notes and a wavering, ornamented line that reminded Clara of something liturgical, though she could not place it. The melodies were fully formed, not fragments. They had beginnings and endings, structural integrity, as though someone had composed them deliberately and with skill.

Clara had attempted to write them down. She was not a gifted transcriber, but she had a serviceable ear and had brought manuscript paper to the last two lessons, notating what Elara played as faithfully as she could manage. She produced the sheets now from her reticule and placed them on Croft’s desk, four pages of notation in her careful, slightly rigid hand. Croft drew the first page toward him and studied it. His expression did not change.

Clara told him she had spoken to Elara about the stranger, gently at first, then with more directness. The girl was not frightened, which Clara found more alarming than if she had been. Elara described the humming as beautiful, said she looked forward to hearing it, and had begun to leave her window open at night regardless of the cold. She said the stranger never called up to her or attempted to enter the house. He simply stood in the mews and hummed, and each night the melody was new. Clara asked whether the servants had heard anything, and Elara said she had not mentioned it to them, nor to her uncle, because she did not wish to be forbidden from opening the window.

It was the uncle who brought the lessons to an end. On the Thursday before Clara’s visit to Croft, she had arrived at the Wimpole Street house and been met not by the parlourmaid but by Aldous Vance himself, a tall, grey-faced man who conducted his business from the study and rarely intruded on the household’s domestic routines. He had spoken to Clara in the hall, briefly and without courtesy, informing her that Elara’s musical education would be discontinued. He did not give a reason. Clara, flustered, had asked whether she might say goodbye to her pupil, and Vance had refused. The door was closed on her with a sound that was not quite a slam but carried the same finality.

Clara told Croft she had walked to the end of Wimpole Street and then stood on the pavement, uncertain what to do, and had looked back at the house. She could see the second-floor windows from where she stood. One of them was open, despite the rain, and she thought she saw a shape there, a pale oval that might have been Elara’s face, though it was too far away to be certain. She had gone home and sat at her own piano and played through the transcriptions, and the music had unsettled her in a way she could not articulate. The tunes were not the work of a fanciful girl inventing melodies at the keyboard. They were too finished, too various, too particular. They sounded as though they had come from somewhere, or from someone, and the thought of that someone standing in a dark mews beneath a wealthy girl’s window, humming compositions night after night and then vanishing before dawn, had kept Clara awake for a week.

Croft asked whether Elara had seemed unwell, and Clara considered the question. The girl had been pale, she said, paler than when the lessons began, and there had been a heaviness about her eyes, a bluish discolouration beneath the lower lids that spoke of sleepless nights. But she had not seemed melancholy or distressed. If anything, she had seemed rapt, absorbed, as though the music were the only thing that occupied her mind fully. Clara had wondered, in her more anxious moments, whether the girl was being prepared for something, though she could not say what, and the vagueness of her own suspicion was part of what had driven her to seek professional help.

Croft asked whether she had noticed anything else unusual in the house, any change in the servants’ demeanour, any signs of renovation or rearrangement, any visitors Clara had not seen before. Clara said she had noticed nothing, but added that her access had been limited to the music room and the hall, and that Aldous Vance had always kept the upper floors and the study closed to visitors. She had never been beyond the ground floor. Croft asked whether she knew the names of the servants, and Clara gave them: a cook named Mrs. Plim, a parlourmaid called Nell, and a valet, Fossett, who attended Mr. Vance personally. There was also a groom, she thought, though she had never learned his name, as the stables were at the far end of the mews and she had no occasion to go there.

Croft picked up the second page of notation and held it beneath the lamp. He studied it for a long moment, and Clara watched his eyes move across the staves, following the melody as though reading a sentence. When he set it down, he asked whether Elara had ever played the same tune twice, and Clara said no, never, not even when she had asked the girl to repeat a phrase so she could notate it correctly. Elara had tried, but the melody had slipped away from her, and what she played the second time was close but not identical, as though she were remembering it rather than reading it from memory.

Croft placed the four pages together and squared their edges against the desk. He told Clara he would look into the matter, and that she should not return to the Wimpole Street house under any pretext. He asked her to leave the transcriptions with him, and she did, watching him place them in a folder and label it with a date and a name she could not read from where she sat. He did not tell her what he thought, or what he intended to do, or whether he believed the stranger beneath the window was real. He only thanked her and rose to show her out, and as she stepped into the stairwell with its smell of damp plaster and gaslight, she heard him close the door behind her and turn the lock.
