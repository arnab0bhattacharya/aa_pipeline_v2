In the slow decay of St. Chads at Trenthall, where the dampness of the Staffordshire clay crept upward through the clunch and rubble, there was much work for a mason who knew the difference between a merely crumbling buttress and one that threatened the nave. Josiah Brindley was such a man, possessing a broad, patient hand that could coax a rosette from a block of gritstone as easily as he could square a lintel. He was a man of few words, having learned in his youth that a chisel is a more precise instrument than the tongue; yet those who observed him closely knew that beneath his weathered stillness lay a vast, silent ledger of judgments, recording the injustices of the parish with the same accuracy he applied to the measurement of plumb-lines.

The restoration of the south aisle had been undertaken at the sole expense of Mrs. Davenant, whose late husband, Squire Philip, lay in the vault beneath the chancel. It was generally understood in the village that this expenditure was a penitential offering, a sort of architectural indemnity against the judgments of Heaven, for Squire Philip had been a man whose economy in human affairs was matched only by his extravagance in the stable. He had drawn rents from half-starved tenants with the relentless regularity of a tide, and when the enclosures came, he had turned out the widow Hewitt and her lame son to make room for sheep, which were profitable and, unlike men, did not require parish relief.

It was into this context of quiet moral accounting that the Reverend Arthur Wynell entered one crisp October morning, finding Josiah at work on a slab of white Sicilian marble. The vicar was a gentle, timid man, whose broad learning was perpetually at war with his narrower clerical duties, and who, in his desire to soothe the ruffled feathers of his wealthy parishioners, often found himself preaching doctrines that sat uncomfortably on his conscience.

"Good morrow, Josiah," said the vicar, drawing his cloak closer against the draught that crept through the broken clerestory. "I see you are making progress with the memorial. Mrs. Davenant is anxious that it should be completed before the frost sets in."

Josiah paused in his work, wiping the fine dust from his hands. "I am cutting the letters, sir," he said, "as fast as I can without doing violence to the grain of the stone. But I am not cutting the inscription."

The vicar blinked, as a man blinks when a fact he has long dreaded is suddenly presented to him in the plain light of day. "Not cutting the inscription? But it is all arranged—Mrs. Davenant herself composed the words. 'To the beloved memory of Philip Davenant, Esquire, a just and generous landlord, a faithful friend, and a true Christian gentleman.' It is very moderate, Josiah, very moderate indeed."

"Moderate it may be, sir," said Josiah, returning his chisel to its place, "but it is not true. And I will not set a lie in marble."

The vicar sat down heavily on a pew that had been pushed against the wall. He recognized, with that acuteness which sensitive men bring to their own discomfort, that he was now standing on the boundary between the practical and the moral, a region he had spent his life avoiding. He drew a long breath, and his mind, trained in the Cambridge schools, sought about for a distinction that might serve as a bridge across this sudden chasm.

"Come, Josiah," he said, with a mild authority that sat awkwardly on him, "you must not be harsh in your judgments. The dead are beyond our correction, and it is the office of charity to dwell on the virtues rather than the failings of those who have departed. Mrs. Davenant is paying for the stone, and she has the right to determine what shall be written upon it."

"I am not denying her right, sir," said Josiah, with the dogged quietness of a man who has thought long in silence. "She may write what she pleases on paper and paste it on the church door, for all I care. But my hands have cut stone for thirty years, and they have never cut a lie yet. I knew Squire Philip. I worked on his boundary walls. I saw him ride past the cottages that were falling into ruin while his barns were full. I saw the Hewitts go—and I will not carve 'just and generous' over the bones of a man who was neither."

The vicar was silent, feeling the pressure of an argument that, in its very simplicity, defied the elaborate evasions he had been constructing in his mind. He was a man who genuinely wished to be good, but his goodness was of that sort which requires the cooperation of circumstances, and which falters when those circumstances turn adverse. He looked at Josiah, at the broad, scarred hands resting on the marble, and he felt, with a pang, the contrast between the mason's certainty and his own wavering.

"You speak plainly, Josiah," he said at last, "and I do not say you are wrong. But tell me this—does truth belong on gravestones? Is it given to us to weigh the dead in the balance, and to set down our verdict in letters that will outlast our own brief lives? The churchyard is full of monuments that flatter the deceased; if we were to strip them all of falsehood, we should have bare stones and silence."

Josiah considered this, turning the chisel slowly in his hand, his eye falling on the worn pavement where generations of Trenthall feet had trodden. "I am not the judge of the dead, sir," he said. "But I am the judge of my own work. And if I carve those words, I make myself a party to a falsehood that will stand in God's house when you and I are dust. There is a truth that belongs on gravestones, sir, and it is the truth of what a man was—not what his widow wishes he had been. If the Squire was not a Christian gentleman, then let the stone say only his name and his dates, and leave the rest to God, who knows the whole of it better than any of us."

The vicar rose slowly, his face bearing that expression of mingled relief and uneasiness which comes to a man when a burden he has long carried is suddenly shifted to other shoulders, and he perceives, with a clarity that is almost painful, that the burden has not been lifted but only refused. He laid his hand on the cold marble, feeling the grit of the unfinished letters beneath his fingers.

"I will speak to Mrs. Davenant," he said. "I cannot promise what she will say. She is a woman who does not easily accept contradiction."

"She may say what she will, sir," said Josiah, and his voice had in it that deep, unhurried resonance which belongs to men who have measured themselves against hardship and found their own dimensions. "But while I have hands to hold a chisel, they will cut no man a lie to sleep under."

And so the vicar went out into the grey October morning, his footsteps echoing on the worn path, carrying with him the weight of a conversation he knew he must repeat, in softer and more diplomatically couched terms, to the widow. He walked past the row of cottages that Squire Philip had neglected, and he thought of the Hewitts, and of the many small, unrecorded griefs that had accumulated in the parish like sediment at the bottom of a slow stream. And he recognized, with the humility that was the best part of his nature, that the question Josiah had posed was not one of stone or inscription at all, but of the whole intricate, troublesome relationship between the living and the dead, between the truth that men owe to one another and the comfort they seek in forgetfulness. It was a question, he suspected, that he would turn over in his mind for many years to come, long after the marble had been set and the mason's hands had returned to dust.
