The news arrived, as most of Papa’s news did, like a belated and slightly bewildered bird. It fluttered from his study, borne on a puff of pipe smoke and the rustle of papers, and landed squarely in the lap of my mother, who was attempting to mend a tear in his second-best coat. “My dear,” he said, his voice a gentle rumble from the doorway, “we are to have a guest for dinner. A Mr. Silas Thorne. A most illuminating man. He has a theory regarding the tessellation of Roman mosaics in the lower Thames valley that is quite revolutionary.”

Mama’s needle paused in mid-air. Her face, a landscape of mild and perpetual anxiety, grew suddenly stark. “Tonight, Mr. Finch?”

“This very evening,” Papa confirmed with a happy smile, retreating back into the sanctuary of his books. “He is travelling to Gloucester and I insisted he break his journey with us. A fine mind, my dear. A very fine mind.”

The door clicked shut, sealing him off from the consequences of his announcement. Mama and I were left in the parlour, the silence thick as the dust motes dancing in the weak afternoon sun. The clock on the mantelpiece ticked with a deliberate cruelty, counting down the hours. I knew what she was thinking, for I was thinking it too. The larder, after the long winter, was a monument to bare shelves and frugal hope. We had a heel of cheese, hard as a rock, a few potatoes sprouting pale, sightless eyes, and a half-loaf of bread that was already planning its retirement.

“We cannot,” Mama whispered, her voice tight. “We simply cannot. To be found out… the shame for your father. He would be mortified.” But she knew, as I did, that Papa would not be mortified. He would be utterly oblivious, discoursing on Roman grouting while a guest chewed on a crust of dry bread.

My name is Eliza Finch, and I am, by circumstance and temperament, the practical heart of our small, scholarly household. While Papa communed with the ancients and Mama fretted over the present, I had learned to navigate the treacherous waters of genteel poverty. I took the situation in hand.

“Leave it to me, Mama,” I said, my voice sounding more confident than I felt. “Mr. Thorne need never know.”

First, I went to the garden. It was a barren, windswept patch, more a home for Papa’s philosophical contemplations than for vegetables. But I found treasures: a handful of sorrel, sharp and green, hiding beneath a stone; a few sprigs of thyme, clinging stubbornly to life; and a small, woody turnip that had evaded the autumn harvest. These I gathered, along with a handful of chives, and carried them to the kitchen like a general bearing the spoils of war.

Then, I went to see Mrs. Gable, the parsonage’s one and only treasure, a woman whose loyalty to our family was surpassed only by her skill with a pot. She was in the scullery, her hands red from scrubbing, and listened to my tale with a grim set to her jaw.

“A guest? Tonight?” She wiped her hands on her apron. “Lord help us. The man will be dining on air and conversation.” She eyed my meagre harvest. “Well, it’s a poor sort of feast, Miss Eliza, but we’ll make a show of it. There’s a bit of suet in the cold pantry, and a cup of currants. We’ll make a pudding. And I’ve a secret weapon.” She beckoned me closer, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. “The Vicar’s chickens have been laying like fury. I’ve a dozen eggs hidden in the coal scuttle. We’ll have a mushroom omelette, if you can find the mushrooms.”

The hunt was on. While Mrs. Gable began the alchemy of turning suet and currants into a pudding, I took a basket and headed for the meadow behind the church. The air was crisp and smelled of damp earth and new growth. I knew the secret places, the spots where the pale, delicate caps of field mushrooms pushed through the grass after a rain. It was painstaking work, my back aching as I stooped, but I was rewarded. I found enough for a decent omelette, their earthy scent filling my basket.

Back in the kitchen, a plan was forming. The potatoes, boiled and mashed with a little precious butter and the chives, would be a bed for the mushrooms, which I would sauté with the thyme. The sorrel would make a sharp, bright sauce. The omelette would be the centrepiece, golden and fluffy. The pudding would be our triumphant finale. It was a meal of illusions, a conjuring trick performed with a handful of eggs and a bit of imagination.

The hours flew by in a flurry of peeling, chopping, and frantic polishing. I laid the table myself, using Mama’s best linen and the silver that was only ever aired for the Bishop. I placed a single, perfect sprig of rosemary from the garden in a small vase, a tiny, defiant flag of elegance. As the clock struck seven, I surveyed our work. The fire was crackling, the room was warm, and a faint, savoury aroma, a promise of good things to come, hung in the air. Mama, dressed in her best gown, looked pale but composed.

Mr. Thorne arrived precisely on time. He was a tall, spare man with kind eyes and a face that seemed perpetually on the verge of a pleasant thought. Papa, of course, greeted him with a hearty welcome and immediately launched into a discussion of Roman pottery shards. Mr. Thorne listened with an air of genuine interest, and I felt a pang of guilt for the deception we were about to perpetrate.

Dinner was served. My heart hammered against my ribs as I carried in the omelette. It was a masterpiece, a golden crescent flecked with green, sitting proudly on its bed of snowy potatoes. Mr. Thorne’s eyes widened slightly. “My dear Mrs. Finch, Miss Finch,” he said, his voice warm with appreciation, “this is a most unexpected and delightful spread. I had anticipated a simple repast, but this is a feast.”

Papa beamed, utterly unaware of the culinary heroics that had produced his dinner. “My wife and daughter are remarkable managers, Mr. Thorne. Remarkable.” Mama managed a tight, gracious smile. We ate, and we talked. Mr. Thorne, it transpired, was as charming as he was intelligent. He told us of his travels, of the strange and wonderful things he had seen, and he listened with flattering attention to Papa’s theories. He even drew a chuckle from Mama with a wry observation about the temperamental nature of stagecoach horses.

He ate with relish, savouring every bite. “This sorrel sauce,” he remarked, “is a revelation. A perfect counterpoint to the richness of the eggs. Your cook is to be commended.”

“Our Mrs. Gable is a treasure,” I said, my cheeks flushing with a mixture of pride and relief. I felt a strange sense of victory. We had not only fed him; we had charmed him. We had created an evening of warmth and plenty out of next to nothing.

The currant pudding was a triumph, light and sweet, served with a thin, pale custard made from the last of the milk. As we retired to the parlour for tea, I felt a profound sense of peace settle over me. The danger had passed. The illusion had held.

Mr. Thorne was preparing to leave, his coat over his arm, when he paused by the door. He looked from Papa to Mama, and then his gaze settled on me, a glimmer of understanding in his eyes. He gave a small, almost imperceptible nod.

“Mrs. Finch, Miss Finch,” he said, his voice soft. “I must thank you for a truly memorable evening. It is rare to find such genuine hospitality.” He then added, so quietly that only I could hear, “And such a remarkable feat of generalship. You have a gift, Miss Finch. The Romans would have made you a quartermaster.”

He gave me a final, knowing smile, and was gone. I stood in the hallway, the silence of the house settling around me. Papa was already back in his study, murmuring about tessellation, but Mama came and placed a hand on my arm. Her eyes were shining. We had not been found out. The gentleman had been fed, and more than that, he had understood. The parsonage, for one night, had been a place of abundance.
