I had believed, in the complacency of a life regulated by method and prosperous industry, that the passions were but the ornament of youth, and that the heart, once disciplined by the sober lessons of experience, could survey the world with the calm of a philosopher. In this persuasion I had dwelt for fifteen years in the town of Harrowgate Wells, where I held the office of principal partner in the bank of Gresham and Sable, and where my name was synonymous with probity and order. I was esteemed, I was trusted; and if I was not beloved, it was because the character of the banker is one of granite, and men do not embrace the column that supports their temple. My wife, Eleanor, was the light of my hearth—a woman of a spirit so gentle, so self-effacing, that she seemed to move through our rooms like the fragrance of a flower rather than a presence of flesh and blood; and I loved her with the deep, unexamined fondness of a man who has never had cause to question his felicity.

It was on a Tuesday in the autumn of the year 1834 that the first stone was cast into this placid pool. I was seated in my private room at the bank, the ledgers open before me, when my clerk announced a visitor—a young woman of about two-and-twenty years, plainly but decently attired, who desired to speak with me upon a matter of the utmost urgency. She entered with a composure that struck me as singular in one so young and evidently of no great fortune; her countenance was pale, but her eyes were steady, and there was about her a gravity that spoke of a spirit acquainted with sorrow. She gave her name as Miss Clara Thornton, and said she had come from Leeds, having been employed there as a governess in a private family, and that she sought my counsel upon a matter of inheritance.

I bade her be seated, and with the courtesy I have ever shown to those who seek my aid, I inquired how I might serve her. She was silent for a moment, as if collecting her resolve, and then she said: “Sir, I am told that you are a man of honour, and that your discretion is as unimpeachable as your credit. I am come to you because I have no other friend in this town, and because I believe that you may know the person whom I seek.” I asked whom she sought. She replied, “My mother. I was born in this town three-and-twenty years ago, and was placed in the care of a woman named Mrs. Hodge, a laundress, who has since died. I have but lately learned that my mother yet lives, and that she is a lady of some station in this place. I have no wish to disturb her peace, nor to claim aught but the knowledge of her name and the assurance that she is well. I ask only to see her once, and to be assured that she does not repulse me utterly.”

There was a trembling in her voice as she spoke, and a moisture in her eyes which she strove in vain to repress. I felt a stirring of compassion, and yet, even as I spoke words of comfort, a cold presentiment seized upon my heart. I inquired what proofs she had of her birth, and she produced a small packet, wrapped in faded silk, which she placed upon the table. I opened it with a hand that I struggled to keep steady, and within I found a locket of plain gold, containing a miniature portrait of a young woman—and I knew the face, though it was younger by many years than I had ever seen it, for it was the face of my Eleanor. There was also a lock of hair, fine and dark, and a scrap of paper bearing the words, in a hand I knew too well, “For my child, if she lives—E.”

The room swam before me. I set down the locket and rose from my chair, and walked to the window, and looked out upon the street, where the people passed to and fro in the ordinary business of their lives, unconscious of the earthquake that had just rent the foundations of my own. I have heard it said that in the moment of supreme calamity the mind becomes preternaturally clear; but mine was clouded with a thousand conflicting thoughts. I thought of Eleanor, my wife, the mother of my children, the companion of my hearth—she who had never spoken to me of her past, and whom I had never questioned, taking her as a gift of Providence, perfect and unblemished. I thought of my own name, my credit, my standing in the community, which was the very air I breathed. And I thought of this young woman, who stood before me with her patient, sorrowful eyes, and who was, by the evidence of that locket, the child of my wife’s youth—a child born in shame, given away in secrecy, and now returned to demand the truth.

I turned to her and said, with a voice that I strove to make calm, “Miss Thornton, this is a matter of great delicacy. I must ask you to leave this packet with me, and to call again upon me tomorrow at this hour. I will make inquiries, and I will do what I can to serve you.” She looked at me with an expression of gratitude so touching that I felt myself a traitor even as I spoke. She thanked me with a warmth that cut me to the soul, and departed.

When she was gone, I sat for a long time in silence, the locket in my hand, and I confess that I wept—I, who had not wept since boyhood. I wept for the wife I thought I knew, and for the stranger I now beheld in her; I wept for the child who had been cast off, and for the children of my own blood who might be shamed by the revelation. And then I wept for myself, for I saw that I stood at a crossroads from which there was no return, and that whatever choice I made, some portion of my happiness must be sacrificed.

That evening I returned to my house with a heavy heart. Eleanor met me at the door with her accustomed smile, and inquired after my day; and I, who had never deceived her, found myself speaking of trivial matters while my mind was consumed with the image of that locket and the words “For my child, if she lives.” I watched her as she sat at the tea-table, her hands busy with the cups, her face soft in the lamplight, and I asked myself: was this the woman who had borne a child in secret, and given it away to a stranger? Was this the woman who had carried that burden of shame and silence through all the years of our marriage, and never once betrayed it? And I felt a strange, aching pity for her, mingled with a sense of betrayal that I could not stifle.

At length, when the servants had withdrawn and we were alone, I took the packet from my pocket and laid it upon the table between us. I said, “Eleanor, a young woman came to me today, and left this in my keeping. I think you know what it contains.” She looked at the packet, and her face became as white as the linen on the table. She did not speak; she did not move; she sat as one turned to stone. And then, slowly, she reached out her hand and touched the faded silk, and a cry broke from her—a cry so wild, so full of anguish, that I started from my chair. She fell upon her knees before me, and clasped my hands, and cried, “Forgive me, forgive me! I was young, I was betrayed, I was abandoned! I thought the child had died—they told me she had died! I have carried this sin in my heart for three-and-twenty years, and I have never dared to speak of it, even to you, whom I love more than my own soul!”

I raised her up and held her in my arms, and for a long time neither of us spoke. The storm of her grief spent itself against my breast, and I felt the tears she had so long suppressed moisten my own cheek. And in that moment I knew that I could not cast her off, nor could I turn away the child who sought her. The world might call it weakness; the world might whisper and point; but I had looked into the face of my wife’s sorrow, and I had seen there the image of a suffering far greater than any shame that the world could inflict. I said to her, “Eleanor, tomorrow you shall see your daughter. And I will stand beside you both, and let the world say what it will.”

She looked up at me with eyes in which gratitude and terror struggled for mastery, and she said, “You are too good to me—I do not deserve it.” And I answered, “We none of us deserve the mercy we receive; but we may give it, one to another, and so make the world less cold than it is.” And so it was that on the morrow, in the privacy of my own parlour, my wife embraced the child of her youth, and I, who had thought only to protect my name, found that I had gained a daughter, and that the name I had guarded was the richer for it.
