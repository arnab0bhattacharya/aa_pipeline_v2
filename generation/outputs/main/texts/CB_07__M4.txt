The rain had not ceased for three days when I first set foot in Marlow’s End, and it seemed to me that the very heavens wept in protest at my coming. The coach set me down at the crossroads, and I stood with my portmanteau in the mud, watching the wheels churn the black water, and feeling that I had been abandoned to a country whose language I did not speak—though it was England, and the tongues that cursed me were English.

I had come to serve as curate to Mr. Hargreaves, a man whose name was spoken in the parish with a reverence that bordered on fear. He was the mill-owner’s brother, and the living he held was in the gift of that same mill-owner, Mr. Josiah Hargreaves of Marlow Mill. I was to be the spiritual arm of the enterprise, or so I gathered; and I was to be paid a pittance for the privilege of lending heaven’s sanction to the profits of the loom.

The parishioners—if I may call them so, for they were not mine—regarded me with a suspicion that hardened quickly into contempt. I was the master’s man, the parson who ate at the master’s table and prayed for the master’s health and the master’s machines. When I walked through the village, the women drew their shawls tighter and the men turned their faces to the wall. The children, more honest, threw stones. I do not think I was a coward; but I confess that I flinched, and that the flinching taught me something of the weight of a stone.

It was in the parish school that I first met Mrs. Ellwood. The school was a low, stone building, damp and dark, and she kept it with a discipline that was fierce and a tenderness that was fiercer still. She was a widow, perhaps forty years of age, with hair that had gone grey before its time and eyes that missed nothing. She did not despise me; she did something worse—she pitied me, and she told me so.

“You will not last the winter,” she said, on the third occasion of my visiting the school. She said it without heat, as one states the weather. “You are too soft for this place, Mr. Markham. You have a conscience, and a conscience is a luxury that Marlow’s End cannot afford.”

I was stung. “You think me weak?”

“I think you honest,” she replied, “and honesty is weakness here. The masters will use you, and the workers will hate you, and you will stand between them like a fool between two millstones, and you will be ground to powder. And then,” she added, with a certain grim satisfaction, “you will go back to your father’s house and tell him that the ministry is not for you.”

I had not told her of my father, nor of his house, nor of the long years of disappointment that had driven me from it. I stared at her, and she looked back at me with a calm that was almost insolent.

“You have the look of a man who has been told he is a failure,” she said. “I know the look. My husband had it, before he died.”

I learned that her husband had been a weaver, and that he had died of a fever caught in the mill, and that Mr. Josiah Hargreaves had sent a guinea to the funeral and a warning to the widow not to expect more. She had taken the guinea and thrown it in the canal. “It was not pride,” she said. “It was the only way I could keep my soul.”

I began to visit the school more often than my duties required. I told myself that I went to teach the children their catechism, but the truth was that I went to hear her speak. She was the only person in Marlow’s End who did not lie to me. The masters told me that all was well; the workers told me nothing at all. But Mrs. Ellwood told me the truth, and the truth was bitter.

“There is a child in this school,” she said one afternoon, “who has not eaten for two days. His father is a piecer at the mill, and his wages have been cut because he was late. He was late because his wife was dying, and he stayed to hold her hand. Mr. Hargreaves has a rule: late is late, and dying is no excuse.”

I felt the blood rise to my face. “I will speak to Mr. Hargreaves.”

“You will do no such thing,” she said sharply. “You will speak to no one. You will go home and pray, and tomorrow you will come back and teach the child his letters, and you will not mention the hunger, because if you do, the child will be turned out of the school, and then he will have neither food nor learning.”

I was silent. She looked at me with a kind of exasperation.

“You think I am hard,” she said. “I am not hard. I am careful. There is a difference. If I were hard, I would not keep this school. If I were hard, I would not feed the children from my own table. But I have learned that you cannot fight the masters with fire. You must fight them with water, and wear them down.”

“And what of the child?” I asked. “What of his hunger?”

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she said, “You may give me a shilling, Mr. Markham, and I will put it in the poor-box, and no one will know where it came from. That is how you fight here. You do not speak. You do not protest. You give, and you are silent.”

I gave her the shilling. I gave her many shillings after that, and then pounds, and then more than I could afford. She never thanked me. She took the money as one takes a stone from a shoe—with relief, but without gratitude. And I found that I did not want her gratitude. I wanted her respect, and that was harder to earn.

The winter came, and with it the winds and rains of November. The mill worked day and night, and the children in the school grew thin and quiet. I preached on Sundays to a congregation of masters and their wives, and I felt the words turn to ashes in my mouth. I spoke of charity, and I saw Mr. Josiah Hargreaves smile. I spoke of justice, and I saw his brother frown. I spoke of the kingdom of heaven, and I saw the workers look at me with a contempt that was now mixed with something else—a kind of weary recognition, as if they had known all along that I was one of them, and had only been waiting for me to admit it.

It was Mrs. Ellwood who forced me to admit it. She came to me one evening in December, when the snow was falling, and she stood in the doorway of my little lodging and said, “They are turning out the Websters. They cannot pay the rent, and Mr. Hargreaves has given them a week. The wife is with child, and the child is sick. You will come with me.”

I went with her. I do not know what I expected—perhaps that I would speak to Mr. Hargreaves, and that he would listen, and that all would be well. But when I stood in the Websters’ cottage, and saw the cold hearth and the empty cupboard and the child on the bed, I knew that no words of mine would move the master. And I knew, too, that I could not stand by and do nothing.

“You must write to the bishop,” said Mrs. Ellwood. “You must tell him what is happening here. You must tell him that the mill is a scandal, and that the masters are tyrants, and that the church is silent.”

“I cannot,” I said. “The bishop is Mr. Hargreaves’s friend. He will not listen.”

“Then you must speak from the pulpit,” she said. “You must tell the truth, and let them do what they will.”

I looked at her. She was standing in the snow, with her shawl wrapped around her, and her eyes were bright with a fire that I had not seen before. She was not a young woman, and she was not beautiful, but in that moment she seemed to me the most magnificent creature I had ever known.

“You are asking me to destroy myself,” I said.

“I am asking you to be a Christian,” she replied. “You have been a curate for six months, and you have not yet been a Christian for a single day.”

I went home and wrote my sermon. I wrote it by candlelight, with the wind howling in the chimney, and I felt the words come not from my mind but from my heart. I spoke of Lazarus at the gate, and of Dives in his fine linen, and of the mill that ground men’s lives for profit, and of the church that blessed the grinding. I did not name Mr. Hargreaves, but everyone in that congregation knew whom I meant.

The next morning, I was dismissed. Mr. Hargreaves himself came to my lodging, with a face like flint, and told me that I was no longer curate of Marlow’s End. I was to be gone by nightfall. I packed my portmanteau, and I walked to the school to say goodbye to Mrs. Ellwood.

She was sitting at her desk, with a pile of copybooks before her, and she looked up as I entered. She did not seem surprised.

“You spoke,” she said.

“I spoke.”

She nodded slowly. “And now you will go.”

“And now I will go.”

She rose and came to me, and she took my hand in hers. Her hand was rough and warm, and it trembled slightly.

“You are not a failure, Mr. Markham,” she said. “You are a fool, and a stubborn one, and you will never have a living in this county again. But you are not a failure.”

I looked at her, and I felt the tears rise in my eyes. “What will you do?” I asked. “They will turn you out too.”

“They may try,” she said. “But I have been here longer than they have, and I have a few friends left. I will manage.”

I wanted to say more—to tell her what she had become to me, to tell her that she was my conscience, my compass, my only honest friend. But the words would not come. I could only stand there, holding her hand, and feeling the silence between us fill with all the things I could not say.

“Go,” she said softly. “Go, and do not look back.”

I went. I did not look back. But I have looked back a thousand times since, and I have seen her standing in the doorway of that damp little school, with the snow falling around her, and her eyes bright with a fire that I have never seen in any other human face. She was not beautiful, and she was not young, and she was not gentle. But she was the only Christian I have ever known.
