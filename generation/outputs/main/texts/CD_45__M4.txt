The name of the young gentleman was Arthur Wilding, and if you had met him upon the lawn at Marchmont Priory, with the sun upon his fair hair and his hand resting lightly upon the back of a wicker chair, you would have said—as half the county said—that here was a young man whom Providence had fashioned in a kindly hour. He was two-and-twenty, well made, open of countenance, with a smile that came readily and a manner that had been polished, at considerable expense, by a tutor, a dancing-master, a fencing-master, and the careful example of his patron, Mr. Josiah Marchmont of Marchmont Priory, who had taken him, a charity boy of nine, from the parish school at Wexcombe, and had raised him to the condition of a gentleman.

Mr. Josiah Marchmont was a man of sixty, stout and ruddy, who wore his watch-chain like a decoration and his benevolence like a second coat, and who was wont to say, in his hearty way, that he had made his fortune by the honest application of capital to the soil, and that a man who could not look his fellow-creatures in the face after forty years of industry had best have stayed abed. He said this so often, and with so open a countenance, that the county believed him; and the county, having once believed him, repeated it at dinners, and the thing became a fact of the neighbourhood, like the spire of Wexcombe church or the bad drainage of the lower town.

It was in the library of the Priory, upon a certain October afternoon, that Arthur Wilding learned the truth of it. He had come for a book—the second volume of a history of the West Indies, which Mr. Marchmont kept upon his shelves because a cousin of his had written it—and, taking it down, he found the pages uncut, and, cutting them, found himself reading of the sugar islands, and of the trade in human creatures which had made them profitable, and of the great houses of Bristol and Liverpool that had grown rich upon that trade, and of one house in particular, Marchmont and Wilding—the second name being that of Arthur's own father, who had died in the service of the firm when Arthur was a child.

He read until the candles were brought in, and then he read by candlelight; and when he had done, he closed the book and sat very still, and the fire burned down in the grate, and the clock upon the mantelpiece struck the hours, and he did not hear them.

The estate, he learned, was not the estate of a country gentleman who had farmed his acres well. It was the purchase-money of a plantation in the island of St. Christopher's—a plantation of which Mr. Marchmont had been part-owner, and from which, at the time of emancipation, he had received a compensation so considerable that it had bought the Priory, the park, the farms, the livings, and the education of Arthur Wilding himself. The whole fabric of his life—the lawn, the wicker chair, the dancing-master, the fencing-master, the fair hair and the open countenance—stood upon that foundation, and the foundation was a thing he could not look at without a sickness at the heart.

He went to Mr. Marchmont the next morning. The old gentleman was at breakfast, in his dressing-gown, with the newspaper propped against the teapot, and he received his protégé with his usual heartiness, and pressed upon him a second chop.

'You are pale, Arthur,' said he. 'You want exercise. Ride over to Wexcombe and see the new drainage.'

'Sir,' said Arthur, 'I have been reading the history of the West Indies.'

Mr. Marchmont's hand paused upon the teapot. 'Ah,' said he. 'My cousin's book. A dry fellow, my cousin, but accurate.'

'Sir, I have read of the plantation at St. Christopher's.'

There was a silence. Mr. Marchmont set down the teapot, and looked at the young man over the top of the newspaper, and his countenance was not the countenance of the hearty old gentleman of the lawn, but something shrewder and colder, as though a curtain had been drawn back from a window.

'Well,' said he, 'and what of it? The thing was done in the way of business, and the law allowed it, and the Parliament compensated it. You have eaten of it, Arthur, and you are the better for it, and I am not ashamed of it. There is no man in England whose hands are clean of the sugar-trade, if you go back far enough. Do you mean to give up your dinner because a black man grew it?'

'Sir,' said Arthur, 'I mean to give up the dinner, and the house, and the education, and the name of gentleman, if they are not mine to keep.'

Mr. Marchmont stared at him, and then he laughed—a laugh that had less mirth in it than the laugh of the lawn. 'You are a fool, Arthur,' said he, 'and a young fool, which is the worst kind. What will you do? Go back to the parish school? Dig? You have no trade in your hands but the trade of a gentleman, and I have paid for that, and you owe it to me to use it.'

'I owe it to you,' said Arthur, 'and I will pay it. But I cannot pay it in the coin you have given me. Let me go, sir, and let me work, and let me pay back what I can, and let me not be a gentleman upon such a foundation.'

The old man rose from the table in a gust of anger, and walked to the window, and stood with his back to the room, and his hands clasped behind him; and when he spoke again his voice was not angry but weary, and there was in it something that Arthur had never heard before—a note of the man who has built a house upon sand and hears the water in the cellar.

'Go, then,' said he. 'Go, and be a fool, and starve, and see how the world treats a man who will not eat his dinner because of a scruple. I have done my best by you, Arthur, and you repay me with a sermon. Go.'

And Arthur went.

He went to London, and he took with him nothing but a small portmanteau and the clothes he stood in, and he left behind him the watch that Mr. Marchmont had given him on his twenty-first birthday, and the horse, and the books, and the name of Wilding, which he had no right to, and took instead the name of his mother, which was Pike, and a lodging in a back street off the Strand, where the landlady was dirty and the lodgers were noisy and the water was cold. He sought employment, and found it, after many weeks, in the counting-house of a merchant in the City, at a salary of ninety pounds a year; and he rose at six, and walked to his work, and came home at eight, and ate his supper in his little room, and was as happy, I believe, as any young man in London, though he was very thin, and his coat was very shiny at the elbows, and the fair hair had lost something of its gloss.

But it is not to be supposed that Mr. Marchmont sat down under his defeat. He was a man who had made a fortune, and a man who has made a fortune does not relinquish his hold upon it because a young fool has a scruple. He wrote to Arthur—kindly at first, then sharply, then with a lawyer's pen—and receiving no answer, he came to London himself, and sought out the counting-house, and stood in the street outside it in the rain, a stout old gentleman with his watch-chain and his benevolence, and watched the clerks go in and out, and saw Arthur among them, thin and shabby and upright, and went away without a word.

He came again, a month later, and this time he waited for the young man at the corner of the street, and took his arm, and walked with him, and said nothing for a long while.

'Arthur,' said he at last, 'I have been thinking. There is no man in England whose hands are clean, as I told you. But there is a difference between a man who knows it and a man who does not. I did not know it, or I would not let myself know it, and that is the truth, and I am ashamed. I have sold the Priory, and I have given the money—a great part of it—to a society for the education of the blacks in the islands, and I have kept enough to live upon, and no more. And I have come to ask you to forgive an old man who was a fool in his prosperity, and to come back to him, not as a gentleman upon a plantation, but as a son upon a stool.'

And Arthur Wilding—or Arthur Pike, as he was then—took the old man's hand in the rain in the Strand, and held it, and could not speak for a moment; and then he said, 'Sir, I will come; and I will bring nothing with me but what I have earned; and if you will have me, I will be your son upon those terms.'

So the two fools—for so the county called them, when the story got abroad—lived together in a small house in a dull street in Bloomsbury, and were as happy as two men can be who have given up a great deal and kept a little, and who know, at the end of the day, that the little is their own.
