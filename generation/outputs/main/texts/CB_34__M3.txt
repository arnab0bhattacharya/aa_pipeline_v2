The grey October rain lashed the leaded casements of the pensionnat in the rue de la Croix, and the vast, bare dormitories above echoed with the violence of the storm. It was the dinner-hour, and the long, stone-paved study below was wholly abandoned to its chilling shadows. I had returned to seek a needle-book I had left forgotten in my desk—a trivial errand, yet one which Providence, or that darker fate which often entangles the footsteps of the solitary, made the instrument of a sudden and severe trial. 

As I crossed the dreary expanse of the room, my ear caught a sound—the faint, convulsive rustling of paper—proceeding from the deep window-recess at the upper end. I turned. Crouched against the frosted glass, her slight form shrouded by the heavy damask curtain, was Clara Vane. She was an English pupil, the daughter of a Lancashire manufacturer, and barely fifteen; a creature whose extreme youth, pale, expressive features, and silent, dejected demeanour had often drawn my thoughts toward her, reminding me, with a sharp and haunting poignancy, of my own early days of exile and anguish. 

She did not hear my approach. Her whole soul was absorbed in a letter she was reading, her trembling fingers crushing the paper’s edges, her breath coming in short, unequal gasps. I stood beside her. I spoke her name. 

She started violently. The colour rushed to her face, then left it pallid as marble. With a swift, convulsive movement she crumpled the document and thrust it into the bosom of her frock. Her large, dark eyes, dilated with a wild, affrighted terror, fastened themselves upon my face. 

"Give it to me, Clara," I said. I did not speak harshly, but with that low, decided tone which authority, even when reluctant, instinctively assumes. 

She stood mute, trembling like a snared bird. 

"You know the rules of this house," I continued. "Madame Daubray has forbidden all unlicensed correspondence. If I am silent, and this is discovered by other means, your doom is fixed. Give it to me."

A heavy sob broke from her. She drew forth the crushed paper, and placed it in my hand. Her head sank upon her clasped fingers. I unfolded the sheet. The characters were unformed, written in a boyish, vehement hand—the scrawl of some stripling sweetheart, full of crude, passionate protestations and foolish vows of eternal attachment. It was a trivial thing in itself, a mere boy's letter; but in the severe, cloistral economy of Madame Daubray’s establishment, it was a crime of the deepest dye. 

I read it through in silence. When I raised my eyes, I found hers fixed on me with an expression that pierced my very heart. It was the look of a hunted creature that knows no earthly refuge. 

"You must go to your dinner, Clara," I said. "I shall keep this. Return to the refectory, and do not betray yourself by any unwonted sadness or confusion. I will consider what is to be done."

She murmured a broken, inaudible appeal, and, obeying the gesture of my hand, glided from the room. 

I was left alone in the cold, dim study. The wind moaned without; the fire on the great grated hearth was nearly extinguished. I stood gazing at the blotted page in my grasp, and a tempest, wilder than that which raged without, began to sweep through my own breast. 

My duty was clear, starkly and uncompromisingly clear. I was an English teacher in this foreign school—a dependent, earning, by severe and unceasing toil, the crust that kept me from absolute want. My position was precarious; my salary was small; but it was mine, honestly gotten by the surrender of my liberty and the endurance of much daily humiliation. Madame Daubray was a woman of rigid, uncompromising discipline. To conceal this letter was to violate the trust she reposed in me. It was to risk my situation, my bread, my character. The path of duty, of stern, unyielding probity, lay straight before me. I had but to carry the paper to her cabinet, and lay it on her table, saying, "I found this in the pupil’s possession." My hands would then be washed of the matter. Clara would be punished—expelled, perhaps, with public ignominy—but I should be safe. 

And yet—and yet! As I stood thus debating, the cold present melted away, and the burning past rushed upon me. I saw, as in a vision, my own former self—lonely, orphaned, cast upon a bleak, unfeeling world, sickening for a word of human kindness, perishing of a starvation that was not of the body. I remembered the intolerable dreariness of my own school-days in a similar foreign town; the icy discipline; the crushing of the natural affections; the slow, despairing agony of youth denied its rightful warmth. I remembered how, in those dark days, a single word of sympathy from a stranger would have been as water in the desert. And this girl—this poor, trembling child—was suffering the same pangs. She had sinned against a rule; but her sin was the offspring of a craving for affection, planted in her breast by God Himself. To betray her now would be to harden her heart, perhaps for ever. It would be to drive a nail into her young coffin. 

I walked to the black, smouldering hearth. I looked at the dying embers. A fierce, rebellious fire began to glow within me. What was this iron system? What was this social law that bound the weak to the chariot-wheels of the strong? Why should I, who had suffered, become the executioner of another? Because I ate the bitter bread of dependence, was I to sell my soul to the taskmaster? Was I to be a spy, an informer? 

For ten minutes I wrestled with the demon of selfish fear. I saw my own exposure, my own destitution, staring me in the face. If I sheltered her, I sheltered her at the peril of my own life. I thought of the bleak streets of Bruges, of the winter approaching, of the gaunt phantom of starvation. It was a terrible risk. But the face of Clara Vane—the white, agonized face, with its dumb, entreaty—rose before me, shutting out the grim prospect. 

"No!" I cried aloud, striking my hand against the cold mantel-piece. "I will not do it! I will not be a Judas for a crust of bread! Let the consequence be what it may, I will shield her. If I must fall, I will fall with a clear conscience. I will not betray the helpless." 

My resolution was taken. I struck a match, and, applying it to the corner of the letter, held it until the flame consumed it, dropping the black, crumbling ashes into the grate. I ground them to powder with my foot. The evidence was destroyed. 

I knew my danger. Madame Daubray’s vigilance was sleepless; her espionage ubiquitous. But I was armed with a desperate courage. I had thrown the die; I was prepared to abide by the issue. I would watch over Clara; I would warn her; I would be her friend, her shield, though the heavens should fall. And if, in the inscrutable wisdom of Providence, my frail bark was to be wrecked on this rock, I felt that I should not wholly sink. The God who tempers the wind to the shorn lamb would not utterly forsake the solitary creature who braved ruin rather than betray a sacred trust.
