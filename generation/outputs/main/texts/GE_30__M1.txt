There is no man in the parish of Ashby-under-Wold, from the squire in his stone house to the ploughboy who whistles to the rooks in the high elms, who does not know the sound of Amos Croft’s cart. It is not the rattle of the wheels alone, though they are stoutly ironed and well-greased, but the particular rhythm of the horse, a steady, plodding beast named Dobbin, whose hoof-fall on the flinty track is as familiar as the tolling of the church bell. Twice a week, on market days at Melton, Amos traverses the same route, a winding artery of rutted lane and high-hedged road that connects the outlying farms and hamlets to the beating heart of commerce. And in this capacity, he is more than a mere carrier of goods; he is the unwitting confidant, the silent repository of the hopes, fears, and small deceits of an entire countryside.

His cart, with its canvas tilt faded to the colour of a winter sky, is a mobile archive of provincial life. In it ride the salted bacon from Squire Harding’s smokehouse, the bolts of calico ordered by the vicar’s wife, the new scythe-blade for old Mr. Thorne at the Grange, and, tucked into the pocket of his greatcoat, a constant stream of letters. These he carries for a penny, or for nothing at all if the sender be a widow or a child. He is a man of business, and his word is as solid as the oak of his cart’s frame. He does not read the letters, nor does he inquire after their contents. His business is conveyance, not comprehension. Yet, it is impossible to sit upon the high box-seat, observing the slow turn of the seasons and the faces of those he serves, without gathering a kind of wisdom—a knowledge not of secrets, but of the currents of feeling that run beneath the placid surface of village life.

It was on a raw morning in late October, when the mist clung to the hollows and the beech trees were a blaze of copper against a pewter sky, that young Martha Pargeter came to him. She was the daughter of the tenant farmer at Little Acre, a girl of seventeen with a bloom on her cheeks that seemed a defiance of the autumnal chill. She waited for him at the crossroads, her shawl pulled tight around her, her hands twisting the corner of her apron.

“Mr. Croft,” she began, her voice barely a whisper above the jingle of Dobbin’s harness, “I have a letter.”

Amos pulled the horse to a halt. “Aye, Martha. For the market, is it?”

“No, sir. Not for the market. It is for Mr. Harding’s boy. For young Mr. Edward.” She would not meet his gaze, fixing her eyes instead on the worn leather of his glove.

Amos felt the familiar weight of a confidence being placed upon him. Edward Harding was the squire’s eldest son, a young man of good family and certain prospects, who was soon to be married to a lady of property from Lincolnshire. Martha Pargeter was a farmer’s daughter. The chasm between them was as wide and unbridgeable as any in the county’s social geography.

“He is at the Hall,” Amos said, his voice carefully neutral. “A letter can be sent by the Hall’s own boy.”

“No!” The word came out with a force that surprised them both. She coloured deeply. “Please, Mr. Croft. It must be private. No one must know. I will pay you.” She pressed a shilling into his palm. It was a great sum for her; he could feel the warmth of it, and of her desperate hope.

Herein lay the crux of the matter, the fulcrum upon which Amos Croft’s simple, ordered world now balanced. On one side was the inviolable principle of his trade: he was a man who carried what he was paid to carry. It was a contract as clear and unambiguous as a line of Latin. He had taken her money; he was bound to perform the service. To refuse would be to break his word, to introduce an element of judgement into a profession that demanded none. He was a carrier, not a confessor, not a moral arbiter. His duty was to the parcel, not to the soul of the sender.

On the other side was his conscience, that quiet, persistent faculty which, in a man of Amos’s temperament, is less a voice of thunder than a constant, low-toned hum of apprehension. He saw the probable end of this affair. He saw the ruin of a good girl’s name, the quiet dismissal of her family from their farm, the bitter disappointment of the squire, and the certain misery of the young couple, for no love affair conducted in such secrecy ever concluded in a village wedding. He was being asked to be the instrument of this destruction. To carry the letter was to be an accomplice, to lend his cart and his sterling reputation to the service of folly. Was a man’s conscience not engaged by the foreseeable consequences of his actions, even if those actions were, in themselves, merely mechanical? To be a mere conduit for another’s folly was, perhaps, a form of folly itself.

He looked at the girl’s face, at the tremulous hope in her eyes, which held not the light of girlish fancy but the hard, desperate glint of a passion that had already considered the risks and found them, for the moment, irrelevant. He knew that to lecture her would be useless. To refuse her would not stop the correspondence; it would merely force it into less reliable, perhaps more dangerous, channels. He would be abandoning her to her fate, rather than standing by to witness it.

Slowly, Amos Croft placed the shilling in his waistcoat pocket. He then held out his gloved hand.

“Let me have it, then,” he said, his tone flat.

A wave of relief so profound it seemed to buckle her knees washed over Martha’s face. She fumbled in her reticule and produced a small, folded paper, sealed with a plain wafer. She placed it in his hand as if it were a holy relic. It was light, almost weightless, yet he felt its burden as a physical thing.

“Thank you, Mr. Croft. Oh, thank you,” she breathed, and then turned and hurried away down the lane, her figure soon swallowed by the mist.

Amos sat for a long moment, the letter resting on his knee. The hum of his conscience was louder now, a discordant note in the familiar symphony of the morning. He had chosen. He was a man of business. He had been paid to carry a letter, and he would carry it. But as he clucked to Dobbin and the cart lurched forward on its journey to Melton, he knew that he was also carrying something else entirely: the fragile, doomed secret of a young girl’s heart, and the full weight of its unseen, and perhaps unseeable, consequences. The road ahead was the same as it always was, but he felt he was travelling it now in a new and troubled country.
