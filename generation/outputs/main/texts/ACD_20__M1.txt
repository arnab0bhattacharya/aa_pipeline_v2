It was a raw and gusty morning in the month of November, when the fog, that yellow enemy of the Londoner, hung in thick, choking banks along the Embankment and crept into the very bones of the houses. My companion, Mr. Julian Thorne, was in one of his silent and contemplative moods, which, I had learned, were the surest prelude to some period of intense mental activity. He stood before the window of our Baker Street sitting-room, his long, thin form silhouetted against the grey light, watching the gas-lamps blink uncertainly through the murk.

"A day for indoor pursuits, Penhaligon," he remarked, without turning. "The very air is thick with untold stories. Every cab that rattles past is a closed volume, every footstep on the pavement a potential chapter."

His words were scarcely spoken when Mrs. Hudson announced a visitor. The man who was shown in was of a breed I knew well: stout, ruddy-faced, and wearing the severe livery of a great railway hotel. He carried himself with the deferential air of one in service, but his eyes were sharp and intelligent.

"Mr. Thorne?" he inquired, his gaze flicking from my companion's lean frame to my own.

"I am he," said Thorne, turning from the window. "And you are from the Great Western Terminus Hotel, if I am not mistaken. A porter, but of some seniority. Your boots are stout and well-polished, but the cut of your coat suggests a position of some authority over the luggage-handlers."

The man's face registered a flicker of surprise. "That is so, sir. My name is Briggs. I am the head porter. I have come to you, sir, on a matter of some delicacy."

"Pray, be seated, Mr. Briggs," said Thorne, gesturing to a chair. "And state your case without preamble. The fog is thick, but my time is not unlimited."

Briggs sat, placing his cap upon his knee. "It is this way, sir. Yesterday morning, a guest checked out of the hotel in a great hurry. A Mr. Alistair Finch, he called himself. He paid his bill in cash and departed, leaving behind a single leather portmanteau. He said he would send for it, but no one has come. It is not the first time a guest has left a bag, but there is something about this one that has troubled the manager. He is a man of a nervous disposition, and he fears a scandal."

"Continue," said Thorne, his eyes fixed upon the porter.

"Well, sir, the manager has had the bag brought to his office. This morning, he could stand the suspense no longer and, on my advice, we opened it. What we found inside has put us in a proper quandary. We dare not go to the police, not without cause, for the guest is gone and the name is likely false. But we cannot let it lie. It is a matter that requires a more delicate touch than Sergeant MacPherson of the Yard can offer."

Thorne steepled his long fingers. "You have found something of a compromising nature."

"We have, sir. First, there is a pair of lady's evening gloves, of fine grey kid, with small pearl buttons at the wrist. They are soiled, as if they have been dropped in the street. Then, there is a revolver, a small Webley, and one of the chambers has been discharged. There is the smell of cordite still clinging to it. And lastly, there is a railway timetable, for the London and North Western line. A single train is underlined in pencil."

My heart gave a little leap at the recital. Thorne, however, remained impassive. "The address he gave?"

"A house in Kensington, sir. Number 14, Ashworth Gardens. But the manager, being a cautious man, sent a boy round. There is no such number. The street itself does not exist."

Thorne rose and began to pace the room, his hands clasped behind his back. "This is a matter of some interest. The haste of the departure, the false address, the lady's gloves, the discharged weapon... it forms a sequence. A story, as I was saying to my friend, Dr. Penhaligon. You have the portmanteau with you, Mr. Briggs?"

"It is waiting in a four-wheeler at the door, sir."

"Excellent. Have it brought up at once."

The portmanteau was a well-worn item of black leather, with brass fittings that were dulled with age. It bore no initial. Thorne had it placed upon the floor and knelt beside it, his sharp eyes scanning every inch of its surface.

"A good quality bag," he murmured. "Belonging to a man who travels, but not a man of great wealth. The handle is worn on the right side, suggesting he is right-handed. Observe, Penhaligon, the fine scratches around the lock. Not made by a key, but by some small, sharp instrument. Our Mr. Finch was not the original owner, or he had lost his key and was forced to gain entry by other means."

He opened the bag. The contents were as Briggs had described. Thorne lifted the lady's gloves first, holding them to the light. He examined them with the minute attention of a jeweller. "Grey kid, size six-and-a-half. A lady of slender hands. See here, a slight tear in the thumb of the left glove. And the soiling is not mere city dust, but a fine, dark earth, with a trace of clay. This is not the soil of a London park."

He laid them down and picked up the revolver. He sniffed the barrel, then spun the cylinder. "A Webley 'Bulldog' model. A popular weapon for self-defence. It was fired some eighteen to twenty hours ago. The bullet is not lodged in the barrel. It was discharged at some distance from the target, or at a soft one. The weapon was handled by someone with a firm grip, but not a trained shot. The butt is clean, but for a single smudge of what appears to be rouge."

Finally, he took the railway timetable. It was a common pamphlet, much-thumbed. One entry was heavily underlined in a bold, decisive hand: the 10:15 p.m. service from Euston to Holyhead, on the previous evening.

"The 10:15 to Holyhead," Thorne mused. "The Irish Mail. It connects with the steamer for Kingstown. A man in flight would choose such a route. But why underline it so boldly, as if to advertise his destination? It is the action of a man who wishes to be followed, or a man who is a fool. And our Mr. Finch, though careless enough to leave this bag, is no fool. No, this is a misdirection."

He sat back on his heels, his gaze distant. "Let us assemble the facts. A man, calling himself Finch, checks into a railway hotel. He is in haste. He leaves a bag containing the effects of a woman and a weapon recently fired. He gives a false address and vanishes. The bag itself shows signs of forced entry. What is the simplest explanation?"

"That he is a scoundrel," said Briggs, stoutly. "He has done some poor woman a harm and is now fleeing the country."

"Possibly," said Throse. "But it is a clumsy scoundrel who leaves his evidence in a hotel cloakroom. Consider the alternative. What if the bag is not his own? What if he is the one in flight, but from a different peril entirely? The bag was forced open. Perhaps he took it from another. The gloves and the revolver are not his, but the spoils of his escape. The timetable was already in the bag, underlined by its true owner. Mr. Finch, in his panic, simply fled, taking the first bag he could lay his hands on."

My mind reeled at the swift deduction. "But why would he take such a bag?"

"To create a false trail, my dear Penhaligon! To send any pursuer on a wild goose chase to Holyhead. He is a clever man, but under pressure, he has made a grave error. He has left behind the one object that tells the true story."

Thorne's eyes fell upon the inside of the portmanteau's lid. He ran his fingers along the silk lining. There, in one corner, was a tiny, almost invisible slit. With the nail of his little finger, he prised it open and drew out a small, folded piece of paper. It was a cloakroom receipt from Charing Cross station, dated two days prior.

"Charing Cross," said Thorne, a thin smile touching his lips. "Not Euston. The game is afoot, gentlemen. Mr. Finch is not in Ireland. He is, I suspect, much closer at hand. And he is not the villain of this piece, but a frightened witness, or perhaps a thief who has stolen the wrong man's luggage. Come, Penhaligon. Our first port of call must be the cloakroom at Charing Cross. We have a bag to claim."
