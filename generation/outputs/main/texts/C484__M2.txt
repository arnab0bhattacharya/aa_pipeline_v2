The rain in London did not wash the city clean; it merely made the grime slicker, a dark varnish that caught the gaslight in Regent Street and turned the cobblestones into treacherous mirrors. Inside the shop of Madame Elara Vane, however, the air was dry, scented with lavender and the sharp, metallic tang of fresh thread. Elara stood before her fitting mirror, her hands trembling slightly as she held a tape measure against the waist of a young lady who had fainted twice that morning. The client, Mrs. Thorne, was a minor but a wealthy one, and her distress was a crisis Elara could not afford.

"They are gone, Madame," Mrs. Thorne whispered, her eyes wide with a panic that seemed disproportionate to the mere absence of a pair of earrings. "The sapphire drops. My mother’s. They are in the lining. I felt them this morning. I do not."

Elara’s face remained a mask of professional sympathy, but her stomach turned over. She had felt the absence too, not of the jewelry, but of the order. The book on her desk, the leather-bound ledger where she recorded every measurement, every alteration, every secret pocket and hidden seam, had been open. She had found it at dawn, the page for Mrs. Thorne’s evening gown blank, the ink of the measurements erased with a wet cloth. It was not just a theft of information; it was a violation of the sacred trust between tailor and client.

"I am sorry, Mrs. Thorne," Elara said, her voice steady. "It must have been a clumsy mistake by the maid. We will check the lining again."

It was a lie, and they both knew it. The maid, a girl named Prudence with a head like a pin-cushion, had been asleep in her room for the past two days, recovering from a fever. And this was not the first time. Three weeks prior, Lady Halloway’s emerald brooch had vanished from the bodice of her dress. A week after that, the Countess of Ashford’s ruby choker had disappeared from the hidden compartment in her hem. Each time, the theft occurred at private balls, each time the victim had been wearing a gown designed by Elara. Each time, the thief had known exactly where to look.

Elara dismissed Mrs. Thorne with a promise of a full refund and a new gown, her mind racing. She needed help, but she could not go to the police. The Met would ask questions, they would inspect the shop, they would find the missing pages in the ledger. And if the world learned that Madame Elara Vane, the finest dressmaker in London, was being picked clean by a ghost, her reputation would shatter faster than a diamond under a hammer. She needed someone who understood the mechanics of theft, but who could also keep a secret as tightly as a corset.

She rang the bell for her assistant, a quiet man named Julian, and told him to send for Mr. Silas Thorne. Not the client, but the man with the same name, a private investigator who operated out of a cramped office in Whitechapel. He was not a gentleman, nor was he a gentleman’s man. He was a man who smelled of stale tobacco and old paper, who looked at the world with the suspicion of a dog that had been kicked.

When Silas arrived an hour later, he did not announce himself. He simply appeared in the doorway, a tall, lean figure in a coat that had seen better decades. He took in the shop, the racks of silk, the scattered pins, and the anxious face of the famous Elara Vane, with a single, unreadable nod.

"You look like a woman who has lost something more than a jewel," he said. His voice was gravel over glass.

"Three jewels," Elara said, leading him to the back of the shop, where the fitting room was. "And my ledger. Someone has been entering my shop, erasing measurements, and stealing from my clients at their homes. They know the dresses. They know where I hide the valuables."

Silas walked to the ledger on her desk. He picked it up, turning it over in his hands. He did not look at the blank page. He looked at the binding, at the spine, at the way the leather had been stretched.

"Who has access to this room?" he asked.

"Only me, my assistant, and the maids," Elara said.

"And the clients?"

Elara hesitated. "Sometimes, when they are waiting for fittings, they wait here. But they never touch the books."

Silas smiled, a thin, jagged expression. "Ladies who wait, Madame? Or ladies who watch?"

Elara frowned. "What do you mean?"

"I mean that you are a master of concealment, Madame. You hide jewels in hems, in bodices, in the lining of sleeves. You make the invisible visible only to those who know how to look. But you have made a mistake. You have made your secrets too obvious."

Silas moved to the fitting mirror. He looked at his reflection, then at the mirror’s frame. He reached up and touched the ornate gilding. He pulled a small, flat object from his pocket—a magnifying glass—and held it up to the glass.

"Look," he said.

Elara leaned in. On the surface of the mirror, barely visible against the silver backing, was a smudge. Not dust, but a faint, grey residue.

"Graphite," Silas said. "Pencil lead."

Elara stared at the smudge. "Why would anyone leave pencil marks on a mirror?"

"Because they are measuring," Silas said. "Not with a tape measure, which leaves no trace, but with a pencil. They are marking the height of the jewels. They are recording the exact location of the hidden compartments. They are mapping your work."

Elara felt a chill that had nothing to do with the London rain. "Who?"

"Someone who has been here often. Someone who has the time to study the glass. Someone who knows that you, a woman of your stature, would never think to check the reflection for clues."

Elara’s mind raced back through the last month. Who had been in the fitting room? The maids? No, they were too busy, too terrified of her. Her assistant, Julian? He was honest, but he was also clumsy. He had dropped a needle on Silas’s shoe that morning. But there was another person. A woman who had visited last week, a client who had spent an hour in the fitting room while Elara was out. A woman who had admired the mirror, who had stood before it for a long time, who had whispered to herself as she adjusted her gloves.

"Madame Elara," Silas said, his voice softening. "You hired me to find a thief. But I am beginning to think you have hired me to find a student."

"A student?"

"Someone who is learning your trade. Not from a book, but from the source. They are not stealing your jewels for the money, Madame. They are stealing them to prove they can find them. They are testing their skills against yours."

Elara closed her eyes. The realization was a physical blow. The thief was not a common criminal. They were a rival. A competitor. Someone who wanted to be the next Madame Vane. And they had been watching, learning, and stealing, not for greed, but for validation.

"Who is it?" Elara asked, her voice barely a whisper.

Silas looked at the smudge on the mirror, then at Elara. "The woman who came last week. The one who asked about the lining of the sleeve. The one who touched the glass."

Elara remembered. It was Miss Clara Vane. Her own sister. The sister she had not spoken to in ten years, the sister who had left their family for the streets, the sister who had returned last week, thin and pale, with eyes that held a fire Elara had not seen in a decade.

"You are joking," Elara said.

"I am not a man who jokes, Madame," Silas said. "I am a man who looks. And I see what is there. The smudge on the mirror is not from a thief. It is from a lover of the craft. A sister who has been watching her own handiwork, and who has grown tired of watching."

Elara’s hands shook. She thought of Clara, who had always been the cleverer of the two. Who had always been the one to see the hidden pattern in the chaos. Who had left not in anger, but in desperation, because she could not be second to Elara. And now, she had returned, not to beg for forgiveness, but to take her place.

"What do I do?" Elara asked.

Silas put the magnifying glass back in his pocket. "You do what you always do, Madame. You dress for the occasion. But this time, you dress for the trap. You set the gown. You hide the jewels. And you wait. The thief will come. And when she does, you will know exactly where she is looking."

Elara looked at the mirror, at the faint smudge of graphite. It was a signature. A claim of ownership. And it was terrifyingly beautiful. She straightened her spine, the steel in her back returning. She was a dressmaker. She knew how to hide things. And she knew how to reveal them.

"Send for Miss Vane," Elara said. "Tell her I have a new gown for her. And tell her to come alone."

Silas nodded, turning to leave. As he reached the door, he paused. "And Madame? Do not trust the mirror. It reflects more than you think."

Then he was gone, leaving Elara alone with the rain, the gaslight, and the ghost of her sister’s ambition, waiting in the shadows of the fitting room.
