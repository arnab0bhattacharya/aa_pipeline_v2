The discovery came about, as such things generally do, by the merest accident—a cracked lens and a fit of economy. I had been photographing the Sunday promenade at the foot of the West Pier, and a gust of wind, coming off the sea with that peculiar spite Brighton winds affect in March, had overturned my tripod and put a spider’s web of fracture across the front element of my best portrait lens. The replacement from London would take a fortnight and cost, I was informed, four pounds eleven and sixpence. I therefore presented myself at the establishment of Mr. Abel Groat, optician, of Ship Street, with the damaged article and a hopeful expression.

Mr. Groat was a small, dusty man with spectacles of his own manufacture so thick that his eyes swam behind them like specimens in a jar. He turned my lens over, clicked his tongue, and said the thing might be ground out and a new front fitted, but that he had by him, if I cared to try it, a lens of his own grinding, “a particular piece of work,” he called it, that he had made for a gentleman of the Royal Society who had died before taking delivery. It was, he said, of a new composition of glass, and the grinding of it had occupied him the better part of a winter. The price was a guinea. I am a practical man—one must be, in a studio that depends upon the sixpenny portrait for its bread and butter—and I took it.

The first plates I exposed with it were of my regular customers, and I noticed nothing. The portrait of Mrs. Harbottle, the butcher’s wife, was sharp and true; so was that of her three daughters, arranged in a descending scale of height and plainness. It was only when I came to develop the plate of old Mr. Pinnock, the retired schoolmaster, that I observed something I could not at once account for. Behind the old gentleman’s chair, in the shadow of the drapery, there stood a faint, vertical smudge, as though the plate had been touched with a greasy thumb. I set it down to a fault in the emulsion and thought no more of it. But the next day, in the portrait of the Misses Cattermole, there were two such smudges, and in that of a young man of the name of Wibberley—a clerk in the London and Brighton Railway, who came to be photographed for his sweetheart—there was a shape so nearly definite that I confess I started when the image swam up in the developing dish. It was a figure, or the suggestion of one, standing at the young man’s shoulder, and the attitude of it—for all that it was no more than a greyness upon grey—was somehow attentive. It appeared to be looking at him. I examined the lens. I examined the plates. I exposed fresh ones. The result was the same.

Now, the extraordinary thing—the thing that I want to set down plainly, for I am not a man of fancies—is that my customers were delighted. Mrs. Harbottle, on receiving her proofs, gave a little scream and declared the smudge behind her chair to be the spirit of her dear departed mother, who had always favoured that particular chair. The Misses Cattermole, who were of a romantic turn, pronounced the two shapes to be the shades of their sailor brothers, lost in the China Sea. Young Wibberley, when I showed him the figure at his shoulder, went very white and then very red, and said it was his poor sister, who had died of the diphtheria the winter before, and he had been thinking of her all the morning. He paid me half a crown extra and begged for a dozen copies.

Within a fortnight the thing had become the talk of Brighton. I had to take on a boy to keep the door, and the waiting-room was full from ten to six. The ladies of the Kemp Town set came in their carriages, and the gentlemen of the hotels came in their hundreds. They did not come to be photographed; they came to see what would be behind them. It was a novelty, and a novelty in Brighton is worth more than gold. I heard it said at the Aquarium that it was “the latest thing in psychical photography,” and a lady of my acquaintance, who wrote paragraphs for the *Gazette*, described it as “a glimpse through the veil.” I said nothing. I took the money. I am a practical man.

But I was not easy. There was a thing that I had noticed and that I kept to myself. The shapes were not the same in every plate. In the earliest portraits they were faint and far, mere suggestions at the edge of the shadow. But as the weeks went on they grew clearer and nearer. The smudge behind Mrs. Harbottle’s chair, which in the first plate had been a mere blur, was in the plate I took of her a month later a distinct grey figure, and it had moved from the shadow into the light. The two shapes behind the Misses Cattermole had drawn together, as though they conferred. And young Wibberley, who came again in April for a portrait to celebrate his engagement, was attended by no fewer than three figures, all of them close about him, and one of them—I give you my word—had the suggestion of a hand raised toward his shoulder.

I began to make experiments. I photographed a chair with no one in it, and there was nothing. I photographed myself, and there was nothing. But when I photographed a sitter, the shapes were there, and always nearer. I took to watching the sitters as they sat, and once or twice I thought I caught, out of the corner of my eye, a movement in the shadow of the drapery—a stir, a shifting—that was not my boy’s doing. I said nothing to my customers. What could I say? That the spirits they had come to see were real, and were closing in? They would have laughed at me, or worse, they would have been pleased. That was the devil of it. They would have been pleased.

It was on the eleventh of May that the thing came to a head. A gentleman of the name of Ashdown—a fleshy, comfortable man, a wine merchant from the London Road—came to be photographed with his wife and their infant. I arranged them, I focused, I put in the plate, and just as I was about to draw the shutter I looked up and saw, behind the group, not one shape nor three, but a whole grey assembly of them, standing in a half-circle in the shadow, and all of them, every one, leaning forward toward the child. I did not take the photograph. I made some excuse—the light, the plate, the shutter—and sent them away. Mr. Ashdown was disappointed. His wife was inclined to be angry. I did not care. I sat down in my empty studio with my heart going like a kettle-drum and my hands cold as the sea.

That evening I took the lens out of its mount and put it in a box, and the box in a drawer, and the drawer under lock and key. I sent to London for the new lens, and I have never used the other since. But I have not destroyed it. I have thought of destroying it a hundred times, and I have not. It is there still, in its box, in the drawer, and sometimes of a night I hear the drawer creak. It is probably the wood. It is probably nothing at all. I am a practical man, and I have my living to make, and I do not believe in spirits. But I have noticed this: that since I put the lens away, my customers have fallen off. They say the new lens has no “atmosphere.” They say the old one had a way of making a portrait “interesting.” Mrs. Harbottle has gone to a man in Hove. Young Wibberley has not been near me since his wedding. And I find myself, in the evenings, sitting in the studio with the lamp lit, and looking at the shadow of the drapery, and wondering—only wondering, you understand—whether the shapes I saw were coming closer, or whether it was I who was going nearer to them.
