The fog in the Vale of White Horse did not lift so much as it dissolved, surrendering to the pale, watery light of a November morning. Elara stood on the wooden platform at the edge of the playground, her breath pluming in the cold air, her hand resting on the iron rope of the bell. It was a ritual that felt less like a duty and more like a prayer, a repetition meant to ward off the silence that had swallowed the rest of the village.

For six years, the Great Withering had taken the adults first, then the elders, and finally, with a slow, crushing inevitability, the children. The towns of Oxfordshire had become open-air museums of decay, their stone cottages standing hollow-eyed against the grey sky. But here, in the small village of Aldbury, the school remained. It was a stubborn anachronism, a brick rectangle with a slate roof that had survived the wind and the rot.

Elara pulled the rope. The bell, a heavy bronze relic from the Victorian era, shuddered and let out a deep, mournful *gong*. It echoed off the empty church spire three streets away, a sound that seemed too large for the desolation.

One by one, the doors of the school opened. First came Thomas, a boy of seven with eyes that held an age beyond his years, clutching a tattered copy of *The Wind in the Willows*. Then, Mara, who had not spoken since the fever took her parents, walked with a limp, her gaze fixed on the mud. Finally, young Leo, the youngest, ran in with a joy that frightened Elara more than the sorrow did. He stopped in the doorway, looking out at the fog, and smiled.

"Good morning, Miss Elara," Thomas said. His voice was quiet, trained by habit not to raise it against the quiet.

"Good morning, Thomas," Elara replied, her voice steady. She turned to face the classroom, the dust motes dancing in the single beam of sunlight that pierced the boarded-up window. "Open your books to page forty-two. We are discussing the concept of permanence."

It was a lie, of course. They were discussing survival, though they dressed it in the language of literature. Elara watched them take their seats on the mismatched chairs, the wood worn smooth by generations of children who would never grow up to leave this village. She felt the weight of the government’s latest decree in her pocket, a cold, folded piece of paper that tasted of bureaucratic indifference.

The knock at the door was not gentle. It was the sharp, authoritative rapping of someone accustomed to being obeyed. Elara looked at her three students. Thomas did not look up from his book. Mara stared at her hands. Leo looked at Elara, his smile fading into a wary frown.

"Stay," Elara whispered. "Read."

She walked down the hallway, the floorboards groaning under her weight. The office was a small, cramped room at the end of the corridor, filled with the smell of old paper and damp wool. When she opened the door, a man stood there, blocking the light. He wore a high-vis jacket over a grey suit, a symbol of the state’s attempt to maintain order in a world that had largely ceased to care.

"Miss Elara Vance," he said. His name tag read *Inspector Halloway*. His face was thin, drawn, and devoid of warmth. "I’m afraid we need to have a chat."

"Inspector Halloway." Elara kept her voice level. "You’re early. We don’t usually expect visitors."

"The Ministry of Education has reviewed the staffing levels and the enrollment numbers in the southern district," Halloway said, stepping into the room. He did not offer a handshake. He leaned against the desk, looking out the window at the fog. "The population of Aldbury is now three. The school has no funding, no viable future, and no staff to speak of. You are the only teacher on the roster for this sector."

"And the children are here," Elara said. "They are in class."

"Exactly. And in six weeks, they will be moved to the central care facility in Oxford. It’s more efficient. More humane. You’re just a caretaker at this point, Miss Vance. A relic."

Elara felt a surge of anger, hot and sharp. "They are not relics. They are students. They have a curriculum. They have rights."

Halloway turned to face her, his eyes cold. "Rights to what? To a dead building? To a bell that rings into an empty village? The state cannot support a school with three pupils. The resources are needed for the infrastructure in the cities. The towns are gone, Elara. You know this. The sickness didn’t stop. It just moved on. This place is a ghost town."

"It is home," Elara said.

"It is a liability," Halloway corrected. He pulled a tablet from his pocket, tapping the screen. "You have forty-eight hours to pack your personal effects and the children’s belongings. The transport will arrive at 09:00 on Thursday. Do not make this difficult for me. I have three other schools to close today. Two in Berkshire, one in Wiltshire. You are the last one. The last school in the county that still pretends to be a school."

Elara looked at him, really looked at him, for the first time. She saw the exhaustion in his eyes, the same hollowed-out look she saw in the mirrors every morning. He was not a villain; he was a functionary, a cog in a machine that was trying to keep the machinery of the state running in a world that no longer existed. But his indifference was a blade.

"I will inform the children," Elara said.

"Thank you," Halloway said, turning to leave. He paused at the door. "Don’t think about it as a loss, Miss Vance. Think of it as a transition. The world is smaller now. It’s safer."

He left, the door clicking shut behind him. Elara stood in the silence of the office. The paper in her pocket felt heavier now, a physical weight of finality. She walked back to the classroom, the steps familiar, the dust motes still dancing.

Thomas looked up as she entered. "Is it over, Miss Elara?"

Elara closed the door and locked it. The click of the latch was loud in the quiet room. She looked at the three children. Thomas, with his ancient eyes. Mara, with her silent grief. Leo, who was still waiting for the morning to begin.

"No, Thomas," she said, her voice soft but firm. "It is not over. It is only a change of subject."

She walked to the blackboard, picked up a piece of chalk, and wrote a single word in large, block letters: *Memory*.

"Today," she said, turning to face them, "we are going to learn about how things stay. Not because they are forced to, but because we choose to remember them. The fog will lift. The bell will still ring. And we will be here."

Leo raised his hand. "Will we go to the city?"

"Perhaps," Elara said. "But first, we finish the lesson."

Outside, the fog began to break, revealing the grey stones of the village, the bare branches of the trees, and the empty streets. But inside the classroom, the air was warm. The bell did not ring again that morning, but the sound of it lingered, a ghostly echo that refused to fade. Elara looked at her three students, three small flames in a vast, darkening world, and she realized that the school was not a building. It was the act of looking at one another and refusing to look away.

She picked up her chalk again. "Page forty-two," she said. "Let us begin."
