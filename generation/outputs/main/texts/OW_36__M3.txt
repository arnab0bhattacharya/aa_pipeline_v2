In the city of Valerius, where the pursuit of beauty was regarded as the only excuse for being born, and the avoidance of boredom the sole justification for living, there lived a clockmaker named Elias Vane. His workshop was situated in a narrow, crooked street near the palace, a street that seemed to have entirely forgotten where it was going, which is, perhaps, the only proper state of mind for a street in a royal city. The shop window was filled with the most wonderful timepieces. There were watches that ticked out sonnets in silver, and clocks that struck the hour with a sound like falling water. For time, as Vane was fond of observing, is merely Nature’s way of preventing everything from happening at once, which would be terribly inconvenient for society.

Vane was a man of considerable genius, and, like all men of genius, he was entirely misunderstood by his wife. He had no wife, of course, which is the only sensible way for a man of genius to be misunderstood by her. He lived alone, surrounded by pendulums and mainsprings, and the quiet, relentless ticking that was to him what the applause of crowds is to an actor, or the bay of hounds to a king.

King Aldous, who sat upon the throne of Valerius, was a man of exquisite taste and absolutely no morals. He had the great advantage of being entirely without conscience, which allowed him to appreciate the finer things in life without any of those tedious fits of remorse that so spoil the enjoyment of the wicked. He was fond of saying that only the truly vulgar ever pay their debts, for a gentleman always leaves something to be settled by his heirs, and a monarch leaves everything to be settled by history. He collected beautiful things and beautiful people, and discarded both with equal rapidity when they ceased to amuse him. But lately, a profound melancholy had settled upon the King. He had realised, with the horror of a man who discovers his wine is watered, that he was growing old.

To grow old is, of course, the one inexcusable error of modern life. It implies a lack of foresight, a want of attention to one's toilette, and a general indifference to the prevailing mode. King Aldous had no intention of committing so gross a breach of etiquette. He sent for Vane.

The clockmaker was brought into the great amber-coloured audience chamber, where the King was reclining on a couch of purple silk, tossing a ripe pomegranate into the air and catching it with a bored, precise movement of his thin, white hands. The room was filled with a heavy scent of white lilacs and burning cedar, and the light that filtered through the stained-glass windows threw patches of ruby and emerald across the marble floor, like scattered jewels that had lost their setting.

"So you are the man who makes the toys," said the King, without looking up.

"Your Majesty's ministers are the men who make the toys," replied Vane, with a low bow. "I merely make the mechanisms that tell them when their playtime is over."

"They tell me," said the King, "that you have constructed a clock that can stop time itself."

"They exaggerate, as all courtiers must," answered Vane. "I have constructed a clock that can stop the appearance of time. Time itself is merely an illusion, somewhat like virtue, which only exists because people pretend to believe in it."

The King laughed, a clear, cold sound like crystal breaking. "I desire that you should install this clock in my private chamber," he said. "I am tired of the sun setting just as the conversation is becoming amusing, and of the seasons changing merely to accommodate the wardrobe of the peasantry. I wish to live forever."

"Forever is a very long time, Your Majesty," said Vane, his voice taking on a solemn, cadenced quality. "It is the one compliment that Nature refuses to pay to beauty. To stop the clock is to stop the pulse of the world. The flowers will not open, and the fruit will not ripen. A bird caught in the air will hang there like a painted ornament. It is a beautiful thing to arrest the flight of a swallow, but the swallow will not thank you for it, for it is in the nature of swallows to fly, as it is in the nature of men to die."

"You talk like a philosopher," said the King, impatiently. "Philosophers are merely people who have failed as poets. I do not wish to die. Death is so middle-class. It is the ultimate surrender to the commonplace. I shall pay you ten thousand gold crowns, and you shall set the clock to this very moment, and never wind it forward again."

"The price," said Vane, looking at the King with his deep, grey eyes, "is not paid in gold."

"Nonsense," said the King. "Everything is paid in gold. Gold is the only thing that understands every language, and the only thing that never tells the truth about its own weight."

"The price," repeated Vane, "is a heartbeat. For every hour of stopped time, a heartbeat must be given. Not yours, Your Majesty, for your heart is far too valuable to risk on anything so practical as eternity. But a heartbeat must be spent. The clock must feed on the living. It is a charming piece of mechanism, but it is also terribly hungry."

"I have a whole kingdom of hearts," said the King, with a smile. "The hearts of the people are the only currency a monarch truly possesses. Install the clock."

Vane bowed and departed. That night, the great clock was installed in the King’s private chamber. It was a magnificent piece of workmanship, its face made of white enamel and black onyx, its hands of wrought silver, fashioned like the slender branches of a winter tree. At its centre was a small, polished ruby, which pulsed with a faint, warm light, rather like the last glow of a dying fire. The King thought it vastly pretty.

The King turned the silver key, and the ticking of every other clock in Valerius ceased. The great bell in the palace tower hung silent in mid-toll. In the gardens, the fountains paused, their waters hanging in the air like strands of spun glass. The King looked out of his window. A butterfly hung motionless above a white rose. A courtier, who had been walking across the courtyard, stood frozen with one foot in the air, looking perfectly ridiculous, as people always do when they are caught off their guard.

"How delightful," cried the King. "I am immortal. I shall have an eternity of purple silks and pomegranates."

For a while, the King was vastly amused. He rearranged the frozen courtiers into more pleasing poses, placing a fan in the hand of a lady who had never held one, and turning the head of a tedious ambassador towards a statue, so that he appeared to be listening to someone much more interesting than himself. He ate of the finest dishes, which the chefs had been in the very act of preparing, and drank from crystal goblets of wine that was perfectly chilled, for the ice had been caught just at the point of melting. He slept in a bed of blue satin, and awoke to find the light exactly as he had left it, a soft, golden afternoon that would never fade into dusk.

But the clock ticked on, its silver hands moving with a slow, relentless purpose, feeding upon the heartbeats of the city. In the streets, the people stood frozen, their faces locked in expressions of joy, or sorrow, or simple, stupid surprise. A child chasing a ball would never catch it. A young woman looking at her lover would never hear him speak. The King had achieved the impossible. He had created a perfect society, where nothing ever happened, and nothing ever changed.

After a week, which was, of course, only a week for the King, the city had been drained of its warmth. The flowers in the gardens had lost their scent, though they retained their colour. The King plucked a red rose, and found it cold to the touch, like a piece of carved coral. He threw it aside, and it shattered on the marble floor. He went to his mirror, and found that his own face was exactly as it had been, smooth and unlined, but there was a look in his eyes that had not been there before, a look of profound, unutterable boredom.

To live forever is the greatest tragedy that can befall a creature whose only joy is in the novelty of things. The King had exhausted his wardrobe, his wine, and his solitude. He walked through the silent palace, and found that the frozen smiles of his courtiers, which had once seemed so amusing, now appeared to him to be masks of a terrible, mocking agony. He tried to read a book, but the words on the page seemed to dance and mock him, for they would never carry him to the end of the story. He had reached the middle of everything, and there was no end in sight.

He sent for Vane, but Vane was, of course, frozen in his workshop, his hand resting on the mainspring of a silver watch, his grey eyes looking into eternity with a calm, detached expression, as one who has always known the price of a heartbeat.

The King ran back to the amber chamber, and seized the silver hands of the clock. He tried to turn them back, but they would not move. He pulled at the silver branches, but they were as immovable as fate. The ruby at the centre pulsed with a steady, merciless light. The King looked at the frozen world, and understood, at last, that the only thing worse than dying is being unable to live.

With a cry of rage, and a gesture of magnificent despair, he struck the clock. The glass shattered, and the silver hands snapped. The ruby pulsed once, a fierce, red glow, and then went dark. The clock stopped.

And in that moment, time resumed. The great bell in the tower finished its toll. The fountains splashed back into the basins. The courtier in the courtyard completed his step, and looked around, wondering why he felt so suddenly cold. The butterfly fluttered away from the white rose. The King stood in the amber chamber, surrounded by the shards of glass and silver, and felt his heart beat, once, twice, and then, with a sudden, terrible pain, it stopped.

They found him there, lying among the broken mechanism of the clock, his face as beautiful in death as it had been in life. The courtiers said that he had died of a surfeit of pomegranates, which was a very regal and romantic way to die, and quite in keeping with the traditions of Valerius. The ambassador, who had been turned towards the statue, remarked that the King had always had excellent taste in sculpture.

Vane, the clockmaker, was found in his workshop, quite well, though somewhat puzzled as to why his silver watch had stopped. He was sent for by the new King, who was a very sensible young man, and entirely without imagination, which is the first requirement of a successful monarch.

"I am told," said the new King, "that you made a clock that could stop time."

"I did, Your Majesty," replied Vane.

"And it killed my predecessor."

"Not at all, Your Majesty," said Vane, with a polite bow. "It merely cured him of the desire to live forever. It is a very dangerous thing, Your Majesty, to try to improve upon Nature. Nature is an artist, and she resents interference. She has arranged for us to die, so that we may have the pleasure of being pitied by our enemies, and the satisfaction of avoiding the extreme old age of our friends. To die is the only truly democratic act a man can perform."
