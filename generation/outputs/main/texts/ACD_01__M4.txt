I had been some three years in the service of the London and Provincial Bank in Cheapside when the affair of the old gentleman and the sovereign first engaged my attention. It was my habit, as the most junior of the counter clerks, to be at my post a full quarter of an hour before the doors were opened, and it was therefore I who, morning after morning, admitted the earliest of our depositors. Among these, with a punctuality that might have been regulated by the Observatory itself, was a small, spare man of perhaps seventy years, habited always in the same suit of rusty black, with a high stock and a hat of an antiquity that defied fashion. He would enter at eight minutes past nine precisely, lay a single gold sovereign upon the counter, and request that it be placed to the credit of his account. In the evening, at four o’clock to the minute, he would return, present his pass-book, and withdraw the identical coin. The transaction, in its way, was perfect; the bank gained nothing and lost nothing, save the labour of two entries in a ledger.

My colleagues regarded him as a harmless eccentricity, and the senior clerk, Mr. Pemberton, was wont to observe that so long as a man’s money was sound, his wits were no concern of ours. Yet I could not rid myself of the conviction that there was method in the old man’s madness, and I took to observing him more closely than my duties warranted. His hands, I remarked, were not the hands of a poor man; they were slender and well-kept, and upon the little finger of the left there was a mark where a ring had lately been worn. His boots, though ancient, were of the finest leather, and his linen, though coarse, was spotless. Here, I reasoned, was a gentleman fallen upon evil days, or one who played a part with a most particular object.

Upon the fourteenth of March, the old gentleman did not appear. I confess that I felt a sense of something amiss before the morning was half spent, and when four o’clock came and went without the familiar tap of his stick upon the tiled floor, I was thoroughly uneasy. I made inquiries of the messenger, of the porter, of the constable at the corner, but no one had seen him. His address, as it appeared in our books, was a lodging-house in a narrow court off the Minories, and thither I betook myself that same evening after the bank had closed.

The house was kept by a widow of comfortable presence, Mrs. Gammidge by name, who informed me, with many expressions of concern, that her lodger, Mr. Josiah Crane, had not been seen since the previous afternoon, when he had gone out as usual and had not returned. His room, she said, was in perfect order, his few possessions untouched, and his rent paid a month in advance. She had notified the police, but they had shown little interest in the disappearance of an elderly man of no known connections. It was then that I bethought myself of the private inquiry agent whose name I had seen upon a brass plate in a by-street near the Mansion House, and whose advertisements in the evening papers had often amused me by their confident promises.

Mr. Abel Marriott received me in a small, close room that smelt of tobacco and old ledgers. He was a thin, sharp-featured man of middle age, with a manner that suggested he had heard every variety of human folly and had ceased to be surprised by any of it. He listened to my story without interruption, his fingers drumming softly upon the arm of his chair, and when I had finished he put a question or two that showed me he had missed nothing.

“You say the coin was always a sovereign,” said he. “Was it always the same sovereign?”

I confessed that I could not swear to it.

“Then we must assume it was,” he returned. “A man who deposits and withdraws the same coin daily for a period of years is either a fool or a man who wishes the coin to be seen. Now, a fool would not have kept it up so long, and a man who wishes a coin to be seen wishes it to be seen by some particular person. You, my young friend, were the person who saw it. What did you see?”

“Only a sovereign,” said I.

“Precisely. And what is a sovereign? It is a coin of the realm, and upon its face is the image of the Queen. But it is also a thing that may be hollowed, or stamped, or marked in a manner invisible to the casual eye. I fancy, Mr. Halliwell, that your old gentleman was using your bank as a post-office, and that the sovereign was a letter which he delivered and collected each day, and which he did not dare to keep about his person. The question is, to whom was the letter addressed?”

The notion seemed to me fantastical, but Mr. Marriott was not a man to be argued with. He rose, took down his hat, and desired me to conduct him to the lodging-house without delay. We found Mrs. Gammidge in a state of considerable agitation, for she had that afternoon received a visit from two men of rough appearance, who had inquired for Mr. Crane and had, upon being told he was from home, searched his room in a manner that left no doubt of their purpose. They had found nothing, she said, and had gone away with threats. Marriott asked to see the room, and we went up together.

It was a bare chamber, neat as a monastery cell. Upon the table lay a Bible, a spectacle-case, and a copy of the *Times* of the previous day. Marriott examined everything with the most minute attention, even to the ashes in the grate and the dust beneath the bed. At last he uttered an exclamation and drew from beneath the mattress a small chamois-leather bag. It contained a single sovereign, and upon the face of it, in letters so fine that they could only be read with a magnifying glass, were scratched the words: *“Warehouse, Friday, eleven.”*

“The last message,” said Marriott, “and the old man did not live to deliver it. He was to have met his correspondent at the warehouse on Friday at eleven, and the coin was to tell him where. But the coin never reached him, and the old man has been taken. We must be at that warehouse before eleven to-morrow, or we shall be too late.”

The warehouse proved to be a disused building in a court off Thames Street, and at the appointed hour we were concealed in a doorway opposite. At five minutes to eleven a tall, military-looking man emerged from the fog and stood before the door, glancing about him with an impatience that betrayed his anxiety. At eleven precisely, a second figure appeared from the opposite direction—a heavy, bull-necked fellow, whom I recognised at once as one of the two men who had searched Mr. Crane’s room. There was a brief exchange of words, a scuffle, and the tall man was seized and pinioned against the wall.

Marriott stepped forward, and in a voice of quiet authority announced that he was acting for the Bank, and that the game was up. The bull-necked fellow turned to run, but found his way barred by a constable who had been posted in the court. Within the hour, Mr. Josiah Crane was liberated from a cellar in the same building, shaken but unharmed, and the whole singular business was laid bare.

The old man, it appeared, had been for many years the confidential clerk of a firm of diamond merchants, and had been privy to a fraud by which the bull-necked fellow and his confederates had sought to obtain a consignment of stones. The sovereign was the means by which he communicated with the one honest partner in the firm, who dared not be seen to meet him. When the fraud was ripe, the old man had been seized to prevent his giving evidence, and the message which he had carried to the bank each day was the only thread that could have led to his rescue.

“It is a small matter,” said Marriott, as we parted at the corner of Cheapside, “but it has its points. I have observed, Mr. Halliwell, that the most remarkable crimes are often concealed in the most ordinary transactions, and that a sovereign is a very small thing to hang a man’s life upon. I wish you good-morning.”
