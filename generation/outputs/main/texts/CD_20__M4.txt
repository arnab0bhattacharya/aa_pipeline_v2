Mr. Theophilus Wren was a young gentleman of six-and-twenty, who had taken a second-floor back in a lodging-house in the Borough, and who believed himself destined to be a great poet. He had a pale, eager face, hair of a reddish hue that would not lie flat, a coat the elbows of which had grown acquainted with his writing-desk, and a small black volume in which he inscribed those thoughts that he considered too precious to be entrusted to the common air. He had lived in this apartment for the space of five months and three days, during which period he had composed the first canto of an epic poem upon the subject of the Deluge, seventeen sonnets upon the subject of the moon, and a tragedy in blank verse concerning a Roman emperor, which he had read aloud to his landlady, Mrs. Grubb, who had remarked that it was very long, and that she hoped there would be no call for the police.

Mrs. Grubb was a widow of a certain age, with a face like a winter apple, and a manner that combined the deference of the landlady with the authority of the creditor. She stood in the doorway now, her arms folded beneath her apron, and regarded her lodger with an expression that was not entirely encouraging.

“Mr. Wren,” said she, “I don’t wish to be unpleasant, but the rent is now three weeks overdue, and my feelings, sir, are not made of iron.”

“My dear Mrs. Grubb,” returned the poet, laying down his pen with a gesture of magnificent weariness, “you speak of rent. I speak of immortality. Which of us, think you, has the better part?”

“I couldn’t say, sir,” replied Mrs. Grubb, “but I know which of us has the better chance of being turned into the street, and it is not the one that sits at home in his shirt-sleeves writing about the Deluge.”

The poet sighed, and ran his fingers through his hair, which immediately stood up in a manner that suggested a small and agitated hedgehog. “You do not understand, madam. The world does not understand. But the day will come—the day will come, Mrs. Grubb—when this hand shall have written that which shall make the name of Theophilus Wren a household word, and when the critics who now neglect me shall weep over the very pages for which you demand payment.”

“I don’t want them to weep, sir,” said Mrs. Grubb, with some asperity. “I want them to pay. And I may tell you, Mr. Wren, that Mr. Pumblechook, the butcher, has been here twice, and he is not a man to be put off with poetry.”

At this juncture there was heard upon the stairs a light and rapid footstep, and there entered, without ceremony, a young woman of a brisk and wholesome appearance, whose name was Miss Clara Pibbs, and who kept a small shop over the way for the sale of gloves, ribbons, and such small commodities as are dear to the female heart. She carried in her hand a pair of worsted gloves of a modest grey, and she held them up as one holds up a trophy.

“Mr. Wren,” said she, “my brother has finished these, and he says he shall not do another stitch until he is paid for the last. He is a very determined character, is my brother, and he has a great contempt for what he calls book-learning.”

“Miss Pibbs,” said the poet, with dignity, “I perceive that you are come to persecute me. It is a conspiracy. The whole world is in league against the poet. Mrs. Grubb demands her rent, your brother demands his money, and I—I alone, in this desert of Philistines—am expected to produce a masterpiece upon an empty stomach.”

“La, Mr. Wren,” returned Miss Pibbs, with a toss of her head, “you are always talking of your stomach, and I am sure it is the only thing about you that ever seems to be full. If you would but take a situation in a counting-house, now, like my cousin Bob, you might be earning a respectable wage, and not be always waiting for what you call inspiration, which I take to be another word for idleness.”

“Inspiration,” said Mr. Wren, rising and pacing the narrow room, “is not idleness. It is the divine afflatus. It is the breath of the gods. It is that mysterious visitation which comes to the chosen few, and which no counting-house can either give or take away.”

“It seems to me,” observed Mrs. Grubb, “that it might visit you a little more regular, sir, and at more convenient hours. Last night, if I may make so bold, you was a-walking up and down this floor till two in the morning, and a-rolling of your eyes in a manner that would have frightened a horse.”

“That was the third act of my tragedy,” said the poet, with a melancholy smile. “I was in the very throes of composition. The Emperor was upon the point of expiring.”

“I wish he had expired a little sooner,” said Mrs. Grubb. “My other lodger, Mr. Bumble, what has the front room, says he can’t get a wink of sleep for the noise.”

“Mr. Bumble,” said Theophilus, with a withering glance, “is a tallow-chandler.”

“And a very respectable trade it is,” said Miss Pibbs, warmly. “Better than writing rubbish that nobody reads.”

The poet turned upon her with the air of a man who has received a mortal wound. “Rubbish, madam! Do you call the ‘Lament of the Deluge’ rubbish? Do you call the ‘Sonnets to the Silver Orb’ rubbish? Have you read them?”

“I have not, sir,” said Miss Pibbs, “and I don’t intend to. But I have heard you read them to Mrs. Grubb through the wall, and I never heard such a deal of nonsense in all my life. ‘O moon, O moon, why dost thou hide thy face?’—why, because it’s cloudy, to be sure. Any fool could tell you that.”

Mr. Wren cast himself into a chair, and pressed his hand to his forehead. “It is ever thus,” he murmured. “The poet is ever the butt of the vulgar. The world has no ear for the music of the spheres. It is a wilderness of Pumblechooks and Bumbles and Pibbsses, and the one green spot in it is the grave.”

“There is a green spot in the Borough,” observed Mrs. Grubb, “and it is the patch of grass in front of the church, and I wish you was in it, sir, if you don’t pay your rent.”

At this moment there came a thundering knock at the street-door, followed by a heavy tread upon the stairs, and there appeared in the doorway the portly form of Mr. Pumblechook, the butcher, a man of a red countenance and a loud voice, who carried in his hand a small piece of paper which he waved like a banner.

“Mr. Wren,” said he, “I have come for my money. I have been put off with promises too long. I am a man of business, sir, and I don’t understand poetry.”

“You are not alone in that, sir,” said Miss Pibbs, with a laugh.

“I have a wife and seven children, sir,” continued the butcher, “and they must be fed, and they can’t be fed upon sonnets. If you can’t pay me in silver, sir, I’ll take it in goods. I’ll take it in that there book you’re always a-writing in, and I’ll wrap up a pound of sausages in it, and it’ll be the only use it’s ever likely to be put to.”

The poet sprang to his feet, and clutched the manuscript to his bosom. “Never! Never! You may tear me limb from limb, sir, but you shall not touch a single line of the ‘Lament of the Deluge’!”

“Then pay me what you owe,” said the butcher, “or I’ll have the law on you.”

“I have no money,” said the poet, with a wild look. “I have nothing but my genius.”

“And a very poor substitute it is,” said Mrs. Grubb, “for a leg of mutton.”

There was a pause, during which the poet looked from one to the other of his persecutors with the air of a stag at bay, and Miss Pibbs, who had a kind heart beneath her sharp tongue, felt a momentary pang of pity.

“Come, Mr. Wren,” said she, “be sensible. Give up this nonsense, and take a place in my cousin’s counting-house. He wants a young man of a good hand, and I dare say he would take you on trial.”

“And give up my epic?” cried the poet. “My tragedy? My sonnets?”

“Give them up for a month,” said Miss Pibbs, “and see how you feel. You can always go back to them, you know, if you find you can’t do anything else.”

The poet looked at her, and then at the butcher, and then at Mrs. Grubb, and then at the little black volume in his hand. He sighed a sigh that seemed to come from the very depths of his being.

“Very well,” said he, “I will go. But I tell you, Miss Pibbs, and you, Mrs. Grubb, and you, Mr. Pumblechook, that you are crushing a flower beneath your feet. You are trampling upon a genius. The day will come when you will be proud to say that you knew Theophilus Wren.”

“I shall be proud to say it,” said Mrs. Grubb, “when I have got my rent.”

And so it was arranged that the poet should go to the counting-house upon the morrow, and the butcher went away grumbling, and Miss Pibbs went away laughing, and Mrs. Grubb went away to see about the dinner, and Theophilus Wren sat down in his chair, and looked at the little black volume, and sighed again, and then, with a sudden impulse, dipped his pen in the ink, and wrote upon the first page of a new sheet the words: “O Moon, O Moon, why dost thou hide thy face?” and then he crossed them out, and sat staring at the fire, and wondered whether the counting-house would have a window looking out upon the street.
