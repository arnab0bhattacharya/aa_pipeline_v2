It was at the close of the Michaelmas term, when the Cam ran chill and grey beneath a sky that seemed to have borrowed its colour from the stone of the colleges, that Theodore Ashcombe learned he had not won the Whitcombe Prize. The announcement was made in the senate-house at noon, and by two o'clock the news had travelled, as such news will, through the quadrangles and along the cobbled lanes, until it reached even the humbler lodging-house in Mill End where his guardian, the Reverend Octavius Grey, had taken rooms for the week. The old clergyman had come up from Wrenford St. Mary—a village set so deep in the flat fen country that the tower of its church seemed to rise out of the very water-meadows—with no other object than to see his ward receive the honour which he had been assured was almost within his grasp. He had denied himself a new greatcoat that winter, and had sold a folio of Archbishop Leighton's sermons which he had prized since his own undergraduate days, that Theodore might have the books and the leisure which the competition required. And now the prize had gone to a young man from Trinity, whose father was a baronet, and who had, it was whispered, written his essay upon a theme which he had discussed beforehand with the examiner at a dinner-party.

Theodore came into the sitting-room at Mill End with a face in which mortification and a certain reckless gaiety struggled for mastery. He was a tall young man, with a forehead too broad for his years and a mouth too finely cut for content, and he carried his defeat with the air of one who flings a broken sword upon the table.

"It is over," he said. "I shall not try again. I have been a fool to try at all. The whole thing is a farce—a solemn academic farce, with the prizes given to the man who has the longest purse or the pleasantest manners. I am going abroad."

Mr. Grey was seated by the fire, a thin, stooping man of sixty, with spectacles pushed up upon his forehead and a book of Euclid open upon his knee—for he had been endeavouring to follow his ward's mathematical studies, in the fond hope of being able to converse with him upon them. He looked up slowly, and his hand, which had been tracing a diagram, became quite still.

"Abroad?" he repeated, in a voice that was rather the echo of the word than a question.

"Yes—abroad. To Italy, to Greece, to the East, if I can get so far. I have wasted three years over Greek particles and conic sections, and what have I got for it? Nothing. I might have been seeing the world—the real world, not this little world of dons and proctors and prize-essays. I shall go. I have made up my mind."

The old man took off his spectacles and polished them with his handkerchief, a habit of his when he was much moved, and which had the effect of giving him a moment in which to collect his thoughts. Then he said, quietly: "You have thought of this seriously, Theodore?"

"Seriously enough. I have thought of nothing else for a week. I knew I should not win. I felt it from the first. And I am not sorry. I am glad. It has opened my eyes. I am not made for this life. I am made for action—for movement—for the great world."

There was a silence. The fire crackled in the grate, and a coal fell with a little crash into the pan. Mr. Grey looked at the young man with an expression in which there was no anger, no reproach, nothing but a profound and patient sadness—the look of one who has long foreseen a sorrow and has schooled himself to meet it without a cry.

"I see," he said, at last. "And when do you propose to go?"

"Next week. I have money enough for the journey to Brindisi. After that I shall shift as I can. I can teach English, or write for the papers, or—"

"Theodore," said the old man, "you know that I have no fortune to give you. What I have is yours while I live, but it is little. I had hoped—I had hoped that the prize might have been the beginning of a career which would have justified—" He stopped, and then went on, with a careful gentleness: "But I do not say this to reproach you. You are free to choose your own life. I have no claim upon you but that of affection, and affection, if it is worth anything, must be content to be silent when it cannot help."

The young man had expected reproaches. He had armed himself against them. He had prepared a hundred bitter and brilliant things to say in defence of his conduct—things about the narrowness of the clerical mind, the futility of prizes, the hypocrisy of the academic world. But this quiet acceptance disarmed him. He stood looking at the old man, and something in his breast seemed to tighten and grow hot.

"You are very good," he said, awkwardly. "But I cannot stay. I should only be a burden to you. I must go."

"I have not asked you to stay," said Mr. Grey. "I have only asked you to consider. If you go, I shall not hinder you. But I would have you think whether it is the prize you have failed to win, or something else, that you are running away from."

Theodore flushed. "I am running away from nothing. I am going in search of something—experience, life, reality. I have been fed on words long enough. I want things."

"Things," repeated the old man, softly. "Yes. I remember when I was your age—" He paused, and a faint smile passed over his thin lips. "I remember that I too wanted things. I wanted to be a great preacher, to move multitudes, to be heard in London pulpits. And I was sent to a little village in the fens, where the people are mostly labourers and the church is damp, and the sermon is listened to by twelve old women and a dog. I thought it was failure. I have thought so for thirty years. But I have learned—I have learned that a man may be a failure in his own eyes and yet not be altogether useless. The twelve old women have needed me. The dog—the dog has been a great comfort."

He smiled again, and then his face grew grave. "I do not say this to hold you back. You must go if you must. But I would have you go with your eyes open. You are angry with the world because it has not given you what you expected. That is natural. But do not let your anger turn into contempt. The world is not a prize-essay. It is a very mixed and difficult affair, and the men who succeed in it are not always the best, nor the happiest. If you go, go humbly. And if you fail—as you will fail, Theodore, many times—do not be ashamed to come back."

The young man stood silent. The firelight played upon his handsome, discontented face, and upon the old man's patient, worn one. He had come into the room with a speech ready—a speech full of fire and defiance—and now he had nothing to say. The silence of his guardian was more eloquent than any reproach. It seemed to him that he had never before understood the depth of the old man's affection, or the extent of his own ingratitude. He had been thinking of himself, of his own ambitions, his own disappointments; and he had forgotten the lonely years at Wrenford St. Mary, the economies, the sacrifices, the hopes that had been centred upon him.

"I will write to you," he said at last, in a voice that was not quite steady.

"I shall be glad to hear from you," said Mr. Grey. "And, Theodore—" He hesitated, and then took up his spectacles again and began to polish them. "The Whitcombe Prize is not the only prize in the world. There is a prize which is not awarded in any senate-house, and which is given to those who have learned to bear failure without bitterness. I hope you may win it."

The young man turned away and went to the window. The Cam was still grey, and the sky was still cold, but a faint gleam of sunlight had broken through the clouds and lay upon the water like a promise. He stood there for a long time, looking out, and the old man sat by the fire, with his Euclid unheeded on his knee, and waited. He did not speak again. He had said all that he could say, and he knew that words are sometimes a barrier rather than a bridge. The silence between them was full of things unsaid—of memories, of hopes, of a love that was too deep for speech. And Theodore, standing at the window, felt that silence weighing upon him more heavily than any anger could have done. He had expected a storm, and he had found a calm; and the calm was harder to bear than the storm. He knew that he would go—that he must go—but he knew also that he would carry with him the memory of that quiet room, and the old man's patient face, and the words that had not been spoken. And in after years, when he had seen many cities and many men, and had learned something of the world's mixed and difficult affair, he would remember that his guardian had let him go without a reproach, and that the silence had been the hardest thing of all.
