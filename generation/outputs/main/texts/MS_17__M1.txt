It was with a heart divided between the exaltation of my purpose and an aching tenderness for the gentle being who had resolved to share my fate, that I stood upon the deck of the Aurora, and watched the white cliffs of England fade into the grey mists of the horizon. My expedition was one of science and of glory; I had dreamed for years of the volcanic fires of Iceland, of the sublime desolation of its glaciers, and I had burned to add my name to the roll of those men who dared to wrest from Nature her most terrible secrets. But my wife, my Emmeline, had looked upon my preparations with those large, dark eyes, so full of thought and feeling, and had declared that she would not be left behind. Her spirit, so much finer and more ardent than my own, revolted at the thought of a long and dreary separation; she pleaded that to share my dangers was the only joy she coveted, and that my discoveries would be but half a triumph if she were not there to witness them.

How could I deny her? To see her cheek pale, her sweet mouth tremble, was to undo me; and so, against the prudent counsel of my friends, who spoke of the rigours of the northern latitudes and the perils of the sea, I consented. I told myself that I would be her shield against every hardship, that my strength and knowledge would be her constant guardians. Yet as the ship plunged through the swelling waves of the North Atlantic, and the cold, salt spray stung our faces, I began to perceive that I had not brought a delicate ornament to be cherished, but a soul as capable of endurance and as keenly alive to the wonders of the world as my own. She, who I had feared would be ailing and fearful, would stand for hours at the rail, her gaze fixed upon the play of light upon the water, or the solemn march of the clouds, her mind, I could see, absorbing every new impression with a quiet, rapturous intensity.

Upon our arrival at the desolate coast of Iceland, and in the shadow of the great mountain that the natives call Hekla’s Sentinel, my illusion of my own superiority was finally and utterly dissolved. We had pitched our tents upon a plain of black sand and moss, and my work of measuring and cataloguing began in earnest. Yet it was Emmeline who first noticed the peculiar crystalline structure of the obsidian shards we gathered; it was her hand that traced the subtle patterns of the lichen upon the lava fields, patterns I had dismissed as mere chance. While I was consumed with the grand architecture of the caldera, she was moved by the delicate life that persisted in its shadow. She would point to a tiny, hardy flower pushing through the ash, and speak of its courage with a poetry that shamed my more prosaic observations. Her senses were more finely tuned than my instruments; she would feel a change in the wind, a tremor in the ground, long before I had noted any alteration in my barometer or my compass.

One evening, as a storm of terrible grandeur broke over our camp, and the sky was torn asunder by lightning that illuminated the whole of that infernal landscape, I found her not cowering in fear, but standing at the entrance to our tent, her face alight with an awe that bordered on the divine. The wind whipped her dark hair about her face, and the rain fell in torrents, yet she was motionless, a figure of sublime contemplation against the chaos of the elements. In that moment, I saw not my wife, but a kindred spirit to the very forces we had come to study. It was she who, in the calm that followed, described to me the precise sequence of the lightning’s forked descent and the way the thunder rolled and echoed among the peaks, a description so vivid and accurate that it rendered my own jottings in my journal flat and lifeless.

Now, as we prepare to return to England, and I sit in our cabin, the great manuscript of my findings spread before me, I am torn by a conflict more agonizing than any storm. The paper is destined for the Royal Society; it will be my testament, the work that will secure my reputation and my place among the men of science. Every observation is recorded, every conclusion drawn with the rigorous logic that is my pride. Yet as I read the words, I feel a profound and shameful emptiness. For the soul of this discovery, the keen eye that saw the truth in the minute detail, the poetic heart that understood the sublime meaning of the desolation—this was all her. To put my name alone upon this work feels like a theft, a sacrilege against the very truth I purport to champion. It is her intellect, her sensibility, that has breathed life into these pages.

To add her name, Emmeline’s, beside my own—what a storm of derision and scorn it would provoke! The world of men, with its rigid and unyielding prejudices, would not see a partnership of minds, but an absurdity, a husband’s foolish indulgence of his wife. They would mock her, and in mocking her, they would diminish me. And yet, to deny her credit is to deny the very essence of our shared endeavor. It is to stand in the full light of the sun and declare that I alone have created the day. My ambition, which I had thought so noble, now appears as a sordid and grasping thing. I look at her now, as she sleeps, her brow smooth and untroubled by the world’s judgments, and I know that I cannot rob her. Her spirit would not permit it, and my own conscience, though long accustomed to the solitary pursuit of glory, cries out against the injustice. The paper lies before me, a monument to my pride and her genius, and I must decide whether to face the ridicule of the world as her equal, or to bear the silent, corrosive shame of being her thief.
