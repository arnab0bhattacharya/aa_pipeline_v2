The snow fell upon the city of Aurelian like a benediction that had lost its way, covering the crooked streets and the sharp spires with a whiteness so absolute that it seemed less a weather than a conversion. It was the sort of snow that made the beggars beautiful and the palaces remote, and it lay particularly thick and treacherous upon the great marble steps of the royal residence, where the sentries stamped their feet and dreamed of summer.

It was there, in the blue hour before the lamps were lit, that the child called Lira found the comb. She was a seller of matches, a trade which in that northern kingdom was less a profession than a slow and respectable form of starvation, and she was making her way homeward through the gathering dark with her tray of unsold wares, when her foot struck something hard beneath the fresh drift. She knelt, and with fingers already numb she drew it forth, and the world, which had hitherto shown her only the backs of hands and the bottoms of doors, opened like a jewelled book.

It was a comb of amber and gold, wrought in the likeness of a flowering branch, and set along its spine with seven stones that held within them the deep and troubled red of a sunset imprisoned in ice. Lira had never owned anything beautiful. She had owned a spoon, once, and a doll made of a clothes-peg, and a stone with a hole in it which she had believed to be lucky until the winter it failed to save her brother. But this—this was a thing from another order of existence altogether, a thing that had no business in the cold hands of a match-girl, and she looked at it for a long time in the failing light, and her breath made small ghosts in the air above it.

She knew, of course, what it was worth. She knew that a single stone might be sold for bread enough to last a month, and that the whole might buy her a room with a window, and a fire, and perhaps even a coat that closed. She knew also, with that curious and inconvenient morality which the poor possess precisely because they cannot afford to be cynical, that it belonged to someone. And so she wrapped it in a scrap of sacking, and hid it beneath her coat, and went home to the cellar where she slept, and lay awake all night with her hand upon it, like a miser or a saint.

The princess Vespasia had lost the comb on the morning of her birthday, and by the evening the whole city knew of it. It had been her mother’s, and her mother’s mother’s before that, and it had come from a country so distant that its very name was a kind of poetry, and the princess had worn it in her hair at the great reception, and then it was gone. The court was in an uproar. The guards were questioned, the maids were threatened, the jewellers of the city were visited and their ledgers examined. A reward was posted—a reward so enormous that it caused a small riot in the market square, and men who had never stolen anything in their lives began to look at one another with sudden and speculative interest.

But the princess herself did not wait for the guards to find it. She was a girl of seventeen, with hair the colour of winter wheat and eyes that had never been taught to look away from unpleasant things, and on the second day she put on a plain grey cloak and went out into the city to look for it herself.

She went into the low places, where the snow was grey and the people were grey also, and she asked her questions with a courtesy that astonished everyone who received it. She did not, as her chamberlain had advised, take a guard. She took instead a purse of silver and a small and stubborn hope, and she walked through the winding streets until her fine boots were soaked through and her hands were raw with cold, and still she did not find the comb.

It was on the third day that she came to the cellar where Lira lived, not because she had any reason to think the comb was there, but because she had grown tired of asking and had begun simply to look at faces. And Lira, standing in the doorway with her tray of matches, saw the princess and knew her at once, for the whole city had seen the princess’s portrait in the window of the print-shop, and there was no mistaking that hair or that particular way of holding the chin.

The princess asked, as she had asked a hundred times, whether the child had seen a comb of amber and gold, and Lira, who had rehearsed a hundred lies, found that she could not speak any of them. She stood there in the cold, and the princess waited, and the snow fell between them like a curtain that might be drawn either way.

‘I have it,’ Lira said at last.

The princess did not exclaim. She looked at the child for a long moment, and something in her face changed, and she said, very quietly, ‘Will you give it to me?’

And here Lira discovered that the great moral crises of which the preachers speak are not, in fact, difficult. They are terrible, but they are not difficult. The difficulty is that one cannot be two people at once, and that the girl who sold the comb and bought the fire was not the girl who stood in the snow and looked into the princess’s eyes, and that one of them must die. She went into the cellar, and she came out with the scrap of sacking, and she put it into the princess’s hands.

The princess opened it, and the seven stones caught the light, and for a moment the mean street was transfigured, as though a window had been opened in heaven and the angels were looking out. Then she wrapped it up again, and she said, ‘What is your name?’

‘Lira.’

‘Lira,’ said the princess, ‘you have given me back my mother’s comb, and I have nothing to give you that is worth the giving. But I have a great many things that are worth the having, and I should like to give you one of them. Will you come to the palace?’

And Lira, who had never in her life been offered anything that did not have to be paid for twice, said that she would.

The princess smiled, and it was a smile that had in it something of the snow and something of the fire, and she said, ‘You must know that I am not a good person. I am vain, and idle, and I have never in my life done a day’s work, and I am told that I am beautiful, which I believe because it is pleasant. But I am not a fool, and I know that you have done a thing that I should not have done, and that the world is a place where such things are rare. So you shall come to the palace, and you shall be my maid, and you shall wear a grey dress, and you shall learn to read, and in time you shall be as useless as I am. And that,’ she added, ‘is the best that I can do for you, and it is a great deal better than bread.’

So Lira went to the palace, and she learned to read, and she became in time a person of some consequence in the household, and she never sold matches again. And the comb was returned to its place in the princess’s hair, where it shone as it had always shone, and no one ever knew how nearly it had been lost.

But the princess knew, and she never forgot, and sometimes in the long winter evenings she would say to Lira, ‘Tell me again how you found it,’ and Lira would tell her, and they would both laugh, though neither of them could have said precisely why. For there are some stories that are better than the morals that are drawn from them, and some gifts that are greater than the thing given, and the snow falls on the just and on the unjust alike, and makes them both beautiful for a little while.
