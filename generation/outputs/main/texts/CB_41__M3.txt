The morning broke sullen and grey over the moors beyond Grimstone, yet within the heavy, rattling confines of the travelling carriage, a strange, almost oppressive warmth seemed to prevail. I sat opposite my employer, Mr. Everton, watching the steadfast profile of a man who, for three years, had been to me less a companion than a granite cliff—rugged, inaccessible, and casting a long, dark shadow over my solitary existence as governess to his daughter, Clara. Beside me sat the child herself, wrapped in her cloak, her small face pale with the excitement and trepidation of the journey. We were conveying her to a school in London, a vast, dreary establishment in Harley Street, chosen by her father for its severe reputation.

I had expected the journey to be silent. Mr. Everton was a man who dealt in commands, not conversation. Yet, perhaps it was the profound quietude of the child, who presently fell into a slumber against my shoulder, or the melancholy, sweeping aspect of the autumn landscape, that wrought a sudden, unlooked-for change in him. He looked up from the letter he was perusing, folded it methodically, and fixed upon me a gaze that, instead of piercing me with its usual coldness, seemed to weigh me with a solemn, searching curiosity.

"You have had a heavy burden, Mrs. Thorne," he said abruptly, his voice vibrating with a deep, unfamiliar resonance. "Heavier, perhaps, than I have acknowledged."

I started. I was not accustomed to being thus addressed by the master of Grimstone. "I do my duty, sir," I replied, with such meekness as I could summon. "I am grateful for the post."

"Duty!" he repeated, and a sudden, bitter smile curled his lip. "A word vastly convenient for those who would mask their indifference under a cloak of virtue. Do not speak to me of duty, Mrs. Thorne. I know too well how that word can be made a scourge. I have seen you toil in my house, submitting to the caprices of a dead man’s ghost, and I have said nothing. I have let you bear it. You must have thought me stone."

"Dead man's ghost, sir?" I asked, bewildered.

"My late wife," he returned, his brow darkening like the sky over the moors when a storm gathers. "Her spirit still haunts the nursery, does it not? Her unreasonable demands, her jealous whims, perpetuated in the instructions she left for Clara’s upbringing. I have been a coward, Mrs. Thorne. I shrank from the conflict, and let you face the brunt of it. You have a heart that feels deeply, and a spirit that has been sorely tried. I have watched you. I have seen the fire in your eye when injustice was done to the child, though your lips remained sealed."

I was struck dumb. The sudden flash of his eye, the tremor in his voice, bespoke a man whose inner life was a volcano of suppressed feeling. For the next hours, as the coach jolted over the rough Yorkshire roads, we spoke as equals. He questioned me of my youth, of the struggles that had cast me upon the world, of my views on education, on religion, on the very nature of the grief that binds humanity. His intellect was keen, a rapier that tested the temper of my own mind. I found myself answering him with a fervour that surprised us both. The grey miles flew past unheeded; the bleakness of the landscape was forgotten in the glow of an intellectual and moral fellowship such as I had never dared to dream existed.

We deposited Clara at the school. The parting was tearful, and the child clung to my neck with a desperate grip that tore my heart. Mr. Everton stood by, a stern, motionless figure, yet when the door of the establishment closed upon us, and we were once more in the street, he drew a long, shuddering breath, and I saw that his eyes were wet.

We re-entered the carriage for the return journey. The weather had broken; a furious wind howled through the streets, driving the rain in sheets against the glass. The interior of the coach was now a cavern of gloom. I expected the conversation of the morning to resume, or at least some lingering echo of our sudden fellowship. But Mr. Everton had retreated into himself. He sat in his corner, muffled in his greatcoat, his hat drawn low over his brow, his face averted. He was no longer the man who had spoken to me with such passionate candour; he was once more the master of Grimstone, cold, impenetrable, a being of another sphere.

I ventured a remark about Clara’s comfort. He answered in a monosyllable. I tried to speak of the road. He grunted. The silence that fell between us was not the peaceful silence of earlier in the day, but a heavy, crushing weight, a wall of ice. I felt a chill that had nothing to do with the weather creep into my very soul. What had I done? Had I spoken too freely? Had my plain speech offended him? I reviewed every word I had uttered, searching for the phrase that had wrought this miraculous transformation, but I could find none.

As the miles lengthened, and the night deepened, a fierce, burning indignation began to kindle within me. It was cruel, this alternate thaw and freeze. Why had he roused me from the deadness of my habitual submission, only to cast me back into it more roughly? I was not a stock or a stone, to be played with and then discarded. I had a soul, and he had wounded it. The injustice of his mood stung me like a physical blow. I sat in my corner, my hands clasped tightly in my lap, my heart swelling with a bitterness that was nigh unto rebellion.

When at last the carriage drew up before the bleak, dark pile of Grimstone, the rain was still falling. He alighted first, and stood under the porch, waiting for me. As I stepped down, the light from the hall door fell upon his face. It was haggard, worn, infinitely sad. He did not look at me.

"Go to the fire, Mrs. Thorne," he said, his voice hollow and spent. "You are cold."

He turned and strode into the house, leaving me standing on the wet flags, the rain beating upon my bonnet. I understood nothing. I only knew that the man who had spoken to me as an equal that morning was dead, and in his place stood a stranger, walled in by some impenetrable sorrow, a sorrow I was forbidden to share.
