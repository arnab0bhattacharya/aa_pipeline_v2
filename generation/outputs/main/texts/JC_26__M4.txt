The premises in Cross Street had been a coachbuilder’s in the days when that quarter of Islington still echoed to the clang of the forge and the rumble of wheels upon the cobbles, and the ghost of that vanished industry lingered, I fancy, in the smell of oil and heated iron that met one at the foot of the stairs, mingled now with the sharper, more acrid odours of printer’s ink and the damp, sour breath of paper long stored in darkness. It was a narrow, grimy house of three storeys, its windows opaque with the deposit of years, and at the top of the second flight one came upon the door of the office of the *Vanguard*, a journal of revolutionary opinion, which, if it had never commanded a wide circulation, had at least the merit—or the misfortune—of being read with attention in certain quarters where such publications were regarded with a disfavour not unmingled with alarm.

Miss Harriett Verrall had been employed there some eighteen months as typist and general assistant, and had come to regard the establishment with that curious blend of loyalty and familiarity which a small and struggling enterprise breeds in those who serve it. She was a slight, dark girl of six-and-twenty, with a pale, serious face and hands that moved with a swift precision over the keys of the machine, and she had brought to her work a devotion which she would have been the last to call by so fine a name. She believed in the *Vanguard*. She believed in Mr. Sebastian Orme, its editor, a man of middle age, with a fine forehead and a manner of melancholy courtesy, whose leading articles, written in a close, fervid hand, she had copied out week after week with a growing sense of their power and sincerity.

It was upon a Thursday afternoon in November, when the fog had thickened in the street and the gas-jets in the composing-room burned with a dull, yellow flare, that she made her discovery. Mr. Orme had gone out, as was his habit at that hour, and she had been left to attend to the correspondence and to prepare the copy for the coming issue. Among the letters upon the desk—a rickety piece of furniture of mahogany, much scarred—she found one which had been torn open and hastily thrust aside, as though the reader had been interrupted, or had thought better of the matter. It bore a foreign postmark, and the address was in a hand she did not know. The sheet within was covered with a close, neat script, and it began without any form of greeting: “The sum agreed upon will be forwarded as before, upon receipt of the list of names and the copies of the correspondence. You will understand that we require the fullest particulars concerning the persons frequenting the office, and in especial any who may be lately arrived from the Continent. The service you have rendered is appreciated, and will not go unrewarded.”

She read it twice before the meaning of it penetrated the dull lethargy of her mind, and then she sat quite still, the paper trembling in her hand, while the presses in the room below sent up their monotonous, thudding rhythm through the floor. It was not the first time she had seen that handwriting. She remembered now—with a sudden, sickening clearness—a letter which had lain upon the desk a month before, and which Mr. Orme had slipped into his pocket with a hurried word of apology; she remembered, too, certain absences of his, unexplained and irregular, and the visits of a man in a dark coat who had come twice to the office and had spoken with him in low tones at the head of the stairs. The whole fabric of her faith seemed to sway and settle, like a wall undermined, and she was conscious only of a great coldness and a sense of shame, as though she herself had been detected in some base action.

She put the letter back where she had found it, and went on with her work mechanically, copying out the paragraphs of intelligence with a hand that moved, it seemed to her, of its own volition, while her mind ran hither and thither in a blind confusion. She thought of the men whose names had appeared in the *Vanguard*—the exiles, the refugees, the poor enthusiasts who had trusted the journal with their addresses and their hopes—and she thought of the dark, narrow stairs, and the man in the dark coat, and the polite, melancholy voice of Mr. Orme, and it seemed to her that the very room was tainted, and that the air she breathed was heavy with a treachery that she had been too dull to perceive.

When Mr. Orme returned, an hour later, she observed him with a new and terrible lucidity. He came in shaking the fog from his coat, and his face, in the gaslight, wore its usual expression of weary kindliness; he asked her whether the copy was ready, and made some trifling remark about the weather, and she answered him in her ordinary voice, and marvelled that she could do so. He sat down at the desk, and his hand—that fine, nervous hand which she had watched so often—rested for a moment upon the very letter she had read. He did not open it; he seemed, indeed, to avoid it, and presently he pushed it aside among the other papers with a gesture that was almost furtive, and fell to writing, with his head bent low beneath the gas-jet.

She went home that night through the interminable streets, and the fog, which had thickened to a yellow obscurity, seemed to her the very image of the moral darkness in which she now found herself. She was not a person of heroic temper, and the thought of exposure terrified her—the thought of the scene, the questions, the publicity, the ruin of the man whose kindness had been her daily bread. And yet, to keep silence—to sit there day after day, copying out the fine phrases of liberty and justice, while the names of the poor and the hunted went through her hands to their destruction—that seemed to her a baseness for which she could find no name. She had a vision of the little office, with its litter of papers and its smell of ink, and of herself grown old in that service, a party to a slow and secret betrayal, and she shrank from it as from a contagion.

For three days she hesitated, and in that time she said nothing. She watched Mr. Orme with a terrible attention, and she saw, or fancied she saw, a certain uneasiness in his manner, a furtiveness in his glance at the door, which confirmed her fears. On the third evening, as she was putting on her hat to go, he came out of the inner room and stood before her, and she thought that his face was paler than its wont.

“Miss Verrall,” he said, in his low, courteous voice, “I have been meaning to speak to you. You have been with us some time now, and I have been well satisfied—well satisfied.” He paused, and his fingers drummed upon the edge of the desk. “There may be—changes,” he went on, slowly. “I cannot say. But I should be glad to know that you would not—that you would be discreet.”

She looked at him, and for a moment the words she had prepared died upon her lips. There was something in his face—a weariness, a resignation, a sort of appeal—which disarmed her anger and left her only with a profound and sorrowful pity. She understood, in that instant, that he knew she knew, and that he was asking, not for himself, but for the journal, for the cause, for the poor fabric of faith and hope which he had served so long and so treacherously.

“I have always tried to do my duty, sir,” she said, at last, in a voice that sounded strange to her own ears.

He bowed his head, and she went out, and the door closed behind her with a soft, final click. She stood for a moment upon the dark landing, listening to the thud of the presses below, and then she went down the stairs into the fog, and the street swallowed her up, and the great city went on with its business, indifferent and immense, as though nothing had happened.
