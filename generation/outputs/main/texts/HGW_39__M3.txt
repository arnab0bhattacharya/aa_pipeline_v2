It was a little past eleven on a December morning when my uncle, Mr. Henry Bampton, having summoned me to his private room above the warehouse in Thames Street, announced his intention to stand for Parliament. He stood before the gas-fire, his hands deep in his pockets, his silk hat pushed back from his forehead, regarding me with that expression of aggressive benevolence he usually reserved for the photographers of the trade papers.

"I'm going into Parliament, Gordon," he said. "I'm going to stand for Milchester."

I was, I confess, a little startled. I had supposed him sufficiently occupied with the manufacture and advertisement of Bampton's Neuraline, that incomparable remedy for every disorder that had ever troubled the imagination of a hypochondriac. But he continued, with that air of weighing great matters which sat upon him rather better than one might have expected, that he had been sounded by the local association, that the sitting member was retiring, and that he felt it his duty—as he put it—to "take a hand in the steering of the ship."

"It's time we manufacturers had a voice," he said. "The lawyers and the landowners have had their inning. Look where they've brought us!"

I looked, indeed. My uncle had brought himself from a back-street druggist's shop in Birmingham to a house in Lancaster Gate, a motor-car of early and formidable pattern, and a domestic establishment that included a butler and a French chef, entirely through the sale of a liquid which consisted, as I had reason to know, chiefly of coloured water, dilute alcohol, and a trace of phosphate of iron. I had helped him to devise the advertisement campaigns that had made Neuraline a household word throughout England. I had written the testimonials. I had invented the "Open Letter to the Medical Profession" which had brought us so much angry correspondence and so much free publicity. And I had done these things with a continuous and secret reluctance which was, I think, the chief sentiment of my early manhood.

For I had been educated—at my uncle's expense, be it said—at a good school and a fair university, and had acquired there a habit of mind which made the large assertions of our advertisement copy read uncomfortably to me. I had a respect for fact, a disposition toward scientific accuracy, which my uncle did not share and could not understand. He regarded my scruples as a kind of amiable weakness, like short-sightedness, and continued to employ me because I was cheap, because I was a relative, and because I wrote advertisement copy with a fluency and conviction that he himself, for all his energy, could not quite equal. He thought me clever but soft. "Gordon's got the brains," he once said to a business acquaintance in my hearing, "but he hasn't got the——" he tapped his chest significantly—"the sort of thing that makes a man push."

There was truth in this. I had not his immense, driving, unreflective force. He believed in Neuraline—or at least he believed in selling it—with a sincerity that was quite untroubled by any acquaintance with its chemical properties. He believed in advertising as some men believe in prayer: not as a means of persuading the public, but as a daily exercise of the will upon the universe. And now he proposed to believe in Parliament, or rather in Parliament's capacity to provide him with a larger field for the exercise of that will.

He sent me down to Milchester the following week to "look the ground over," as he phrased it, and I went, carrying with me a certain shamefaced loyalty which I have never quite been able to reconcile with my judgment. Milchester was a Midland borough of the older sort—smoky, prosperous, and politically independent. It had sent a Radical manufacturer to Westminster in '85 and a Conservative lawyer in '92, and it appeared to be now in that mood of suspended judgment which the local press described as "deliberation." I found the town full of billboards already, displaying the portraits and professions of two rival candidates, and I arranged for three large hoardings on the main roads and a series of small cards in the shop windows, all bearing the simple legend: "BAMPTON FOR MILCHESTER. A BUSINESS MAN FOR A BUSINESS SEAT."

My uncle came down on the Tuesday, wearing a new overcoat and an expression of tremendous earnestness, and addressed a meeting of the Conservative committee at the Royal Hotel. He spoke for twenty minutes, and I thought it a remarkable performance. He had a way of saying things that were neither profound nor particularly relevant with such an air of robust common sense that his hearers were carried along by the manner rather than the matter. He talked about "efficiency," about "the commercial spirit," about "giving England a business government," and he used the word "push" twice in a context that made it sound almost dignified.

After the meeting, the local agent, a solicitor named Grinstead, took me aside and asked, with some diffidence, whether Mr. Bampton was "sound on the fiscal question." I said I believed he was, though I doubted whether he had ever thought about it. "He's sound on anything that promises to increase the sale of English goods," I said, and Grinstead seemed to find this satisfactory.

The opposition candidate was a young man of good family and uncertain opinions, a Mr. Faulkner, who had been a diplomatist and had retired upon a competence and a conviction that the country required his services. He was tall, fair, and beautifully dressed, and he spoke with a fluency that was the more admirable because it conveyed so little meaning. I heard him at a meeting in the Market Square, and I was struck by the contrast between his polished generalisations and my uncle's blunt, coarse, and energetic assertions. The crowd, I thought, preferred my uncle.

It was during the second week of the campaign that the Neuraline question was raised. A pamphlet appeared, circulated I have no doubt by the Faulkner committee, though it bore no imprint, setting out in some detail the composition of our remedy and the methods by which it was advertised. It quoted several of our more extravagant testimonials, including one from a clergyman in Worcestershire who had never, so far as I could discover, existed, and it contrasted these statements with the actual formula of the preparation, which had been obtained, I imagine, from a disgruntled former employee. The pamphlet was entitled "The Bogus Tonic: What Milchester Thinks of Its Candidate," and it made disagreeable reading.

I showed it to my uncle in his room at the Royal Hotel. He read it through slowly, his lips moving, and then laid it down on the table and looked at me with an expression of mild surprise.

"Well?" he said.

"It's damaging," I said. "The formula is correct. The testimonials——"

"The testimonials are advertisement copy," he said. "Everybody knows that."

"Not everybody. And the clergyman——"

"Which clergyman?"

"The Reverend A. J. Mortimer, of Evesham. He doesn't exist."

My uncle waved his hand. "That's a detail," he said. "The thing is, do they think I'm going to be frightened by a parcel of lies about a perfectly honest business? Neuraline has sold by the hundred thousand bottle. It's given satisfaction. I've got letters——"

"Real letters?"

"Real letters, from real people, saying they've been benefited. What more do they want?"

I did not say what I thought they might want. I had learned, in five years of partnership with my uncle, that there are arguments which it is useless to press. He had, in his own domain, a logic which was impervious to external criticism, a logic founded upon success and justified by results. He could not see that the sale of a patent medicine and the representation of a constituency in Parliament were different kinds of enterprise, requiring different kinds of integrity. To him, all human affairs were matters of energy and advertisement, and he proposed to conduct his political campaign exactly as he had conducted his business—with vigour, with confidence, and with a liberal expenditure of money.

And I, who was ashamed of the tonic and yet admired the man, continued to work for his election with a devotion that surprised myself. For there was something in his immense, vulgar, unhesitating competence that compelled one's respect, even while one's intelligence recoiled. He was wrong, and I knew he was wrong, and yet I could not bring myself to wish him defeated. He was, after all, the only great man I had ever known intimately, and his greatness, such as it was, had a quality of force that made the merely virtuous seem ineffectual by comparison.
