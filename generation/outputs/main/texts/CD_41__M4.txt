The police court at Clerkenwell was, upon that particular Monday morning, a very bazaar of misery and mischief, a roaring, reeking exchange where the small coin of human folly was traded for the heavy gold of the Law’s retribution. The air was thick with a compound of damp sawdust, fugitive tobacco, and the unwashed essence of a multitude, and the light that struggled through the grimed and cobwebbed windows fell in dusty, jaundiced shafts upon a scene of perpetual and squalid drama. Here a drunken woman, all shawls and shrieks, was being hauled away; there a sullen lad, whose only crime was to have been found in the company of a greater rogue, awaited his doom; and at the very bar of judgment, a trembling wisp of a girl was being admonished for the theft of a penny loaf, her tears making little channels through the grime upon her cheeks.

Presiding over this theatre of wretchedness was Mr. Ambrose Pinfold, a magistrate of long standing and of a conscience so exquisitely sensitive that it operated, in the general opinion, as a kind of chronic and painful disorder. He was a spare, upright man, with a countenance of pale severity, framed in scanty grey hair, and his every gesture was marked by a deliberation that spoke of a mind forever weighing, sifting, and balancing. To him, the law was not a mere collection of statutes, but a sacred and terrible trust; and he carried its burden with a gravity that would have been laughable, had it not been so profoundly sincere. He saw, in every case brought before him, not a mere malefactor, but a moral conundrum, a soul to be saved or damned by the precise measure of his justice.

The morning’s business had been of the usual depressing character—petty thefts, domestic brawls, and the sad, inevitable detritus of poverty—and Mr. Pinfold’s spirit was heavy within him. He had just dismissed a case of assault, the evidence being of so contradictory and muddled a nature that he had felt compelled to bind over both parties to keep the peace, when a stir at the back of the court announced a fresh arrival.

“Bring the prisoner forward,” said the clerk, in his dry, official voice.

A boy of perhaps twelve or thirteen years was led to the bar. He was a small, slender figure, clad in a jacket that had once been blue but was now a faded and patched nondescript, and his breeches were a miracle of ingenious mending. His face was white and pinched with hunger, but it was not the face of a hardened offender. There was a certain dogged, sorrowful dignity in the way he held his head, and his large, dark eyes, which seemed to hold a whole world of silent misery, were fixed not on the magistrate, but on the floor at his feet. In his hand, or rather clutched to his breast, was the evidence of his crime: a small, half-eaten loaf of household bread.

“What is the charge?” inquired Mr. Pinfold, his pen poised above his ledger.

“Stealing a loaf of bread, your worship,” replied the constable, a burly man with a face like a boiled ham. “From the shop of Mr. Grumble, the baker, in Roscommon Street. The boy was seen to snatch it and run.”

“Have you anything to say for yourself, boy?” asked the magistrate, his voice, though stern, not unkind.

The boy looked up, and as his eyes met those of Mr. Pinfold, a strange and terrible thing happened. The magistrate’s pen dropped from his fingers with a clatter. His pale face grew, if possible, a shade paler, and he half rose from his seat, staring at the prisoner as if he had seen a ghost.

For indeed, he had seen a ghost—the ghost of a memory, of a face he had known and loved in the days of his youth. The boy’s features, though sharpened by want and shadowed by fear, were unmistakably those of Thomas Pellow, his former coachman, a man of such open, honest countenance and cheerful spirit that Mr. Pinfold had trusted him with his life and his purse, and had parted from him with genuine regret when a change in his establishment had rendered his services no longer necessary.

“What is your name, boy?” the magistrate asked, and his voice, to his own ears, sounded strange and distant.

“Thomas Pellow, sir,” the boy mumbled, his voice barely audible. “Called Tom, for short.”

The name was a thunderclap in the quiet of the court. Mr. Pinfold felt a cold sweat break out upon his brow. His mind, that finely balanced instrument, was thrown into a chaos of conflicting duties. Here was the son of his old servant, the child of a man he had respected, brought low by the very law he, Ambrose Pinfold, was sworn to uphold. The law was clear: theft was theft, and the penalty must be paid. To show favour would be a gross violation of his sacred trust, a betrayal of his office. Yet, to condemn the boy, to send him to the treadmill or, worse, to the hulks, was to break faith with the memory of a good man and to add another stone to the already crushing weight of the world’s injustice.

He looked at the boy again. The child was shivering, and his knuckles were white where he clutched the bread. Mr. Pinfold saw, in his mind’s eye, the father, Thomas Pellow, sitting high on the box of his carriage, his face ruddy with the wind and his laugh a hearty peal that echoed down the years. He saw him, too, in his little cottage, with his good wife and this very child, then a mere babe in arms, a picture of humble, honest content.

“Pellow,” said Mr. Pinfold, and the name felt like a stone on his tongue. “Pellow. Have you a father?”

“Yes, your worship,” the boy replied, a flicker of hope lighting his eyes. “He is a coachman, sir. He is out of work at present. He is sick.”

The words struck the magistrate with the force of a physical blow. Sick. Out of work. The edifice of his righteous duty trembled to its foundations. This was no common thief, but the victim of that same cruel and arbitrary fortune which it was his daily lot to witness. He felt a great and terrible anger rise within him—not at the boy, but at the system, at the poverty, at the very nature of things.

He must be just. But what was justice? Was it the blind application of the statute, or was it the exercise of a higher, more humane equity? He looked at the loaf of bread, the cause of all this misery. It was not a luxury, but a necessity. It was not the object of a criminal’s greed, but the desperate prize of a starving child.

A sudden, audacious idea seized him. He would not acquit the boy; that would be to fly in the face of the evidence and to invite the censure of the press. He would not condemn him; that would be to violate his own conscience and to commit a moral crime against the memory of his friend. He would find a third way, a way that would satisfy the letter of the law while honouring its spirit.

He cleared his throat, and the court, which had been watching this silent struggle with bated breath, leaned forward.

“Thomas Pellow,” he said, his voice now steady and firm, “you are charged with the theft of a loaf of bread. The evidence against you is clear. You are, by the law of this land, guilty.”

A murmur ran through the court. The boy’s face fell, and the light of hope died in his eyes.

“But,” Mr. Pinfold continued, raising his hand for silence, “the law also recognises that circumstances may alter the nature of an offence. It is not my duty to punish the unfortunate, but to protect the innocent and to correct the guilty. This boy is not a criminal. He is a victim of want.”

He turned to the constable. “Constable, have you any knowledge of the boy’s family?”

“None, your worship,” the man replied, looking bewildered.

“I have,” said Mr. Pinfold, and a strange, unaccustomed warmth crept into his voice. “I know his father. He was once in my employ. He is an honest man, and a good one. That he should be reduced to this—to see his child brought before me for stealing a crust of bread—is a stain not upon him, but upon the society that has abandoned him.”

He paused, and the silence in the court was absolute. “The prisoner is discharged,” he said, “with a caution. And let this be a lesson to you, boy,” he added, turning to the trembling youth. “Let it be a lesson to you, not in the ways of crime, but in the ways of men. There are those in this world who will judge you by your misfortunes and not by your merits. It is for you to prove them wrong.”

He then did a thing that was utterly without precedent in the annals of the Clerkenwell police court. He put his hand into his own pocket, drew out a small, worn leather purse, and taking from it a shilling, laid it upon the desk before him.

“This,” he said, “is to pay for the loaf, and for the boy’s breakfast. See that he gets it, constable. And now, clear the court.”

The boy, dazed and uncomprehending, was led away. The court emptied, the next case was called, and the great machine of the law ground on. But Mr. Ambrose Pinfold sat for a long time in silence, staring at the shilling on his desk, a small, bright coin that seemed to him to bear the weight of a great and terrible truth. He had done his duty, he told himself, and he had done it well. But as he looked at the coin, he knew, in the secret chamber of his heart, that he had done something more. He had found a way to be both just and merciful, and in that moment, he felt a peace he had not known for years.
