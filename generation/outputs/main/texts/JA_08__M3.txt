The village of Birchwood, in the county of Surrey, though possessing no claims to fashion superior to a decent assembly room and a circulating library, found itself suddenly elevated in consequence, and considerably agitated in its quietude, by the arrival of Miss Clara Wentworth. Miss Wentworth, having lately inherited five thousand pounds from an uncle whose existence the neighbourhood had scarcely suspected, was become an object of the highest interest. She was twenty, tolerably handsome, and possessed a disposition naturally inclined to please; but she was wholly without experience in the management of independence, and therefore felt herself peculiarly fortunate in the proximity of a married sister.

Mrs. Fanny Audley, the wife of a respectable attorney in the adjoining market-town, had long since established herself as a woman of the world. She had taken a house in Birchwood for the summer, expressly to superintend her sister’s introduction into society, and she entered upon the duty with an alacrity that left no room for Clara to regret her own ignorance. "My dear Clara," said Mrs. Audley, on the very first evening of their arrival, "you are now to appear as a young woman of fortune. You must not be surprised if you find people exceedingly civil; it is the natural effect of five thousand pounds. But you must be upon your guard. You must not be too free, nor too reserved. You must smile when spoken to, but never laugh; and above all things, you must not be seen to take the lead in conversation with any gentleman of less than three hundred a year."

Clara, who had been accustomed to laugh freely and to converse with very little regard to incomes, received these instructions with a docility that her sister thought highly promising. She was told what gown to wear, what ribbon to select, and at what hour it was proper to show herself in the High Street. Mrs. Audley even went so far as to dictate the precise angle at which her parasol was to be carried, insisting that a forward inclination betrayed an unfurnished mind, while too great a backward tilt suggested vanity. Clara listened, assented, and adjusted her parasol accordingly.

The season at Birchwood advanced with all the mild festivity that the neighbourhood could command. There were evening parties, where cards were played and negus was drunk; there were morning visits, where the same observations on the weather were repeated by successive visitors with an exactness that might have gratified a philosopher; and there was the assembly, where Clara, standing up with Mr. Palmer, the curate, conducted herself with so much propriety that Mrs. Audley declared she had never seen a girl more naturally well-bred. Clara, indeed, performed her part with exemplary punctuality. She said the expected things to the expected people, and with such uniform good temper that she was universally pronounced a sweet girl. Mr. Palmer, who was understood to have some expectations from a distant relation, was encouraged to talk to her of sermons and turnpike roads, and Clara heard him with a patience which she scarcely knew whether to attribute to her sister’s precepts or to her own naturally compliant disposition. Mrs. Audley, meanwhile, looked on with the serene satisfaction of a general who sees his plans executed without a fault.

But there is a limit to the satisfaction derived from universal approval, particularly when it is accompanied by universal sameness. Clara, having been uniformly approved for six weeks, began to find herself uniformly weary. She had worn the gowns, carried the parasol, and smiled upon Mr. Palmer with such unvarying regularity that she sometimes wondered whether she might not be replaced by a suitably dressed automaton without any discernible loss to the society of Birchwood. She had been told what to think of every acquaintance, what to say at every visit, and what to feel upon every occasion; and she had thought, said, and felt exactly as she was bidden, with a meekness that would have done credit to a saint, had saints been known to tire of their own perfection. But Clara was not a saint. She was a young woman of five thousand pounds and a lively mind, and she was beginning to suspect that the two might be allowed to exist together without perpetual direction.

The crisis, if so slight a matter could be called a crisis, arrived on the morning of the Birchwood charity ball. Mrs. Audley had been engaged for a full hour in laying out Clara’s attire, and had at length decided upon a white muslin with a lilac ribbon, a choice which she pronounced at once elegant and unassuming. "It is precisely the thing," said she, surveying the arrangement with an air of settled judgement. "Lilac is becoming, and it says nothing. A young woman with a fortune should say nothing in her dress that she might afterward wish to unsay. You will wear the lilac, my dear, and I shall be perfectly easy."

Clara looked at the lilac ribbon, and a sudden spirit of rebellion, which had been gathering strength through six weeks of unintermitted compliance, rose within her. She thought of the white satin ribbon which her uncle had given her before his death, and which lay, untouched and unsanctioned, in the bottom of her trunk. It was excessively becoming; she knew this, though she had never been told it. It was also, she believed, precisely the sort of thing which Mrs. Audley would pronounce too conspicuous for a young woman in her situation. The knowledge was irresistible.

"I think," said Clara, with a composure that surprised herself, "that I shall wear the white satin."

Mrs. Audley looked up from the muslin with the startled air of one whose authority has been, for the first time, gently but firmly questioned. "The white satin, my dear? Surely not. It is too much. It is too decided. A young woman of fortune must not appear to be laying herself out for observation."

"But I wish to be observed," said Clara, with a modesty that would have disarmed severity, had severity been attainable in the circumstances. "I have been very little observed these six weeks, and I am tired of being overlooked. I believe I should like, for once, to be looked at."

Mrs. Audley, who had never expected to hear such a sentiment from her docile sister, could only stare. "You amaze me, Clara. I had thought you perfectly satisfied."

"I was perfectly obedient," replied Clara, "which is not, I begin to find, quite the same thing."

Mrs. Audley, recovering from her astonishment, entered upon a long and eloquent remonstrance, touching upon the duties of young women, the perils of singularity, and the certain loss of that reputation for steadiness which Clara had so laboriously acquired. Clara heard it all with a patience which she had learned from her sister’s own precepts, and when Mrs. Audley had concluded, she quietly fastened the white satin ribbon at her breast, and walked downstairs with an air of composure that defied objection.

At the ball, the effect of the satin was immediate and, by Mrs. Audley’s standards, alarming. Mr. Palmer, indeed, noticed nothing; but Mr. Frederick Ellsworth, who had lately come into possession of a small estate near Dorking, and who had hitherto regarded Clara with that polite indifference which a gentleman of taste reserves for young women who appear to have nothing to say for themselves, looked at her twice with evident surprise. He presently sought an introduction, and Clara, finding herself called upon to converse without the least instruction from her sister, discovered, with a pleasure she was half-ashamed of, that she had a great deal to say. She talked of books, of scenery, and of London, with a freedom that Mrs. Audley would undoubtedly have pronounced unbecoming, and Mr. Ellsworth listened with an attention that she could not but find flattering. He was a man of five-and-twenty, with a frank address and a pair of eyes that seemed to have formed their own opinions, and Clara, who had been accustomed to see the male sex only in the subdued condition prescribed by Mrs. Audley, found the difference exceedingly agreeable.

When the ball was over, and the sisters were returning in the carriage, Mrs. Audley, after a long and expressive silence, observed that she hoped Clara was satisfied.

"I am," said Clara, "perfectly."

"You were very much noticed," continued Mrs. Audley, in a tone which implied that this was the heaviest charge she could bring. "I do not know when I have seen a girl so much noticed. Mr. Ellsworth scarcely took his eyes from you the whole evening."

"I am glad of it," said Clara, "for I thought him very agreeable."

Mrs. Audley shook her head, but she said no more. She had the wisdom of a woman who knows when her influence is at an end; and Clara, leaning back in the carriage with the consciousness of having made one decision entirely upon her own responsibility, felt that independence, however imperfectly exercised, was infinitely preferable to the most judicious direction that could be bestowed by the most judicious of sisters.
