Mrs. Thorne was, at nine-and-thirty, possessed of that particular security which attends a small income and a settled reputation. Her jointure, the bequest of a husband whose chief distinction had been his excellent digestion and a disposition to agree with everyone, was sufficient to keep her and her three daughters in a state of genteel sufficiency, provided no one was ill, and no one developed a passion for expensive bonnets. Her neighbours at Little Haddington were, for the most part, agreed that she was a very proper woman, who had borne her widowhood with a commendable absence of fuss.

Her daughters, Sophia, Maria, and little Jane, were of a different opinion. They saw not a woman settled in her sufficiency, but a mother whose spirits required a more stimulating diet than parish news and the price of turnips. It was they, therefore, who received the arrival of Mr. Abernathy with the greatest enthusiasm. He was a merchant, his fortune made in the West Indies trade, and his presence in Hertfordshire was as startling as a pineapple at a harvest supper. He had taken Fernside, the property of a gentleman gone to the Continent, and his stated intention to settle in the neighbourhood was a matter of considerable speculation.

The speculation was soon resolved by his calling at Mrs. Thorne’s cottage. He was a man of perhaps fifty, with a countenance that seemed more accustomed to calculation than to cultivation, and a laugh so sudden and hearty it seemed to startle the very dust-motes in the air. He spoke of shipping and tariffs with a frankness that would have been vulgar in a gentleman, but which, coming from him, seemed merely a matter of honest business. He paid Mrs. Thorne no pretty compliments on her complexion, but he listened to her opinions on the management of a kitchen garden with an attention that was, in its way, far more flattering.

The neighbourhood, of course, was in an uproar. Mrs. Gable, the wife of the vicar, was the first to declare her astonishment. “A man in trade, my dear Mrs. Thorne!” she exclaimed, her voice a mixture of pity and reproach. “To think of you, connected to a family of unimpeachable standing for generations, entertaining such a person. It is a degradation.”

Mrs. Thorne, who had found Mr. Abernathy’s account of a mutiny in the Caribbean more diverting than any sermon Mrs. Gable’s husband had ever preached, replied with perfect calmness. “He is very well-bred, Mrs. Gable. And he has seen a great deal of the world.”

“Bred in a counting-house,” Mrs. Gable sniffed. “One can only imagine the society he has kept. It is a sad come-down for your girls.”

Her girls, when consulted, were of one mind. “A come-down?” cried Sophia, the eldest, her eyes bright with practicality. “Mamma, it is a step up! A jointure is all very well, but a fortune is a fortune. Mr. Abernathy can provide for us in a way Papa’s cousin never will.”

“And he is so diverting,” added Maria. “He told us a story about a monkey in Jamaica that was better than anything in the lending library.”

Mrs. Thorne did not tell her daughters that the monkey story was, indeed, the first thing that had made her laugh with genuine, unforced mirth in the three years since her husband’s death. She did not tell them that Mr. Abernathy’s directness was a relief after years of navigating the subtle, exhausting currents of genteel conversation. She merely said, “He is a very agreeable man.”

The courtship, if such it could be called, proceeded. Mr. Abernathy did not walk with her in the churchyard or write her verses. Instead, he arrived with a crate of oranges from a business associate, and stayed to help her daughters peel them, telling tales of the Spanish Main that left them wide-eyed. He noticed that her silver was tarnished and sent round a boy with a proper polishing cloth and a tin of excellent paste. He asked her opinion on the enclosure of the common lands, not as a matter of principle, but as one of practical consequence for the poor of the parish, and he was so thoroughly sensible in his views that she found herself quite silent with surprise.

The crisis, as such things are measured in Little Haddington, came at the assembly. Mr. Abernathy, in a coat of good but unfashionable cut, stood beside Mrs. Thorne. Mrs. Gable watched them like a hawk. When the vicar’s wife, in a voice of syrupy concern, asked Mr. Abernathy if he did not find the society of the county rather slow after the excitements of a commercial life, a hush fell over their little circle.

Mr. Abernathy smiled, a broad, uncomplicated smile. “Madam,” he said, his voice carrying easily, “I have spent thirty years calculating the risk of storms, pirates, and the fluctuating price of sugar. To spend an evening in a room where the greatest peril is a spilled cup of punch is, I assure you, the most welcome novelty I have ever known.”

There was a moment of stunned silence, and then Mrs. Thorne laughed. It was not a polite titter, but a clear, ringing sound of pure amusement. In that moment, she saw the whole situation with perfect clarity: Mrs. Gable’s wounded dignity, the county’s silent condemnation, her daughters’ hopeful ambition. And she saw Mr. Abernathy, who cared for none of it, but who had, with one plain sentence, cut through the suffocating web of propriety that had ensnared her for so long.

Later that evening, as he walked her home under a sky pricked with stars, he said, “I am afraid I have made a cake of myself with your neighbours.”

“On the contrary,” she replied, her arm resting lightly on his. “You have been the most sensible man in the room.”

“I am a plain man, Mrs. Thorne. I deal in facts and figures. I know the value of a good ship, a reliable crew, and a fair price. I do not know the value of a title, but I believe I know the value of a woman who can manage her affairs, raise her children well, and still find room in her day to laugh at a fool’s question.”

He paused, his grip on her arm tightening slightly. “I am not a gentleman. I can never make you a great lady. But I can offer you a comfortable home, a life without financial anxiety, and the company of a man who will never bore you. I find that my own happiness is greatly increased by your presence. I hope you might find the same.”

It was not the most romantic of proposals. It contained no talk of hearts or souls. But it was, Mrs. Thorne reflected, the most honest thing any man had ever said to her. Her neighbours would call it a degradation. Her daughters would call it a triumph. She thought only that she would not have to spend the next thirty years being sensible and secure and quietly, desperately, bored. She looked at his plain, good-humoured face in the moonlight and felt a lightness in her chest that was entirely new.

“Mr. Abernathy,” she said, “I believe I should like that very much.”
