It is a curious tragedy of modern England that a young man with an income of precisely four hundred pounds a year is expected to have a profession. Society extends its toleration to the idle, provided their idleness is clothed in bespoke wool and conducted in the proper postal districts; but it demands of them a narrative. Lord Arthur Merton had attempted the narrative of commerce and failed, chiefly because he possessed the fatal habit of paying his debts. He had tried the narrative of politics, but the House of Commons was far too badly ventilated for his complexion. He had even attempted the narrative of matrimony, but the heiress in question had, with that singular lack of taste common to those who possess money, fled to a Scottish earl.

Left with nothing but his immaculate wardrobe and a profound conviction of his own superiority, Lord Arthur decided to become a critic. He reasoned that criticism is the one vocation that demands absolutely no creative talent whatsoever. To write a poem, one must possess a soul; to paint a portrait, one must have eyes; but to review a book, one requires merely a pen and a memory for the misfortunes of others. It is the natural refuge of those who cannot create, and the unnatural delight of those who can.

His inaugural review appeared in the *Saturday Quill*, a weekly periodical read exclusively by those who wished to appear literate and those who genuinely were. It was a review of a new volume of verse by a poet who was, at that moment, the darling of the drawing rooms. The verses were of that pallid, trembling sort that speaks endlessly of lilies and spiritual yearning, and is entirely devoid of either. Lord Arthur dismantled it with the meticulous cruelty of a surgeon performing an autopsy upon a creature that had never truly been alive. He did not merely say the poems were poor; he proved, with the remorseless logic of an epigram, that they were an offence against the English language and a libel on the emotions of mankind.

The review caused an absolute sensation. By the following Tuesday, the poet had retired to a monastery in Devonshire, and Lord Arthur was famous. The cafes of Mayfair rang with his phrases. Young men in clubs repeated his paradoxes to one another as though they were counting rosary beads. Within a fortnight, it was universally acknowledged in London that the supreme mark of artistic achievement was to be savaged by Lord Arthur Merton.

To be ignored by him was social death; to be praised by him was, of course, quite unthinkable, as praise is the final refuge of the unimaginative. It was generally agreed among the cognoscenti that a man might survive a bad review from anyone else, but a brilliant review from Lord Arthur was a guarantee of immortality. He became the Pope of the salons, distributing excommunications with a graceful wave of his slender, pale hand.

The artists of London, being a race singularly devoid of dignity, immediately set about courting their own destruction. They wrote letters to him, begging him to notice their work. They left their manuscripts at his door, as the mediaeval peasant might leave a chicken at the castle of the local warlord, hoping only that the warlord might look upon it with wrath. Lord Arthur received them all with the same polished, urbanite composure. He sat in his drawing room in Curzon Street, a room decorated in a style of severe aesthetic perfection, surrounded by vases of black tulips and the faint, melancholy scent of hyacinths, and he destroyed reputations with the ease of a man tossing cards into a hat.

He found that he rather enjoyed it. There is a distinct, voluptuous pleasure in the exercise of a power that one has not earned. To take a painter who has spent twenty years labouring over the effects of sunlight on water, and to dismiss his entire output in a single sentence comparing his palette to a dish of stale porridge, is to experience a thrill that is, perhaps, not entirely moral but is undoubtedly exquisite.

It was into this atmosphere of delighted terror that a young painter named Cyril Vance entered. Vance was a romantic, a man who believed that art was a sacred calling and that the critic was merely a midwife to genius. He was possessed of a singular, almost divine talent for colour, and his canvases were alive with the rich, heavy hues of late autumn. He was also, regrettably, sincere.

He called upon Lord Arthur one afternoon in late November. The critic was draped across a sofa of deep violet silk, turning the pages of a manuscript with an expression of refined suffering.

“You must come and see my new picture,” said Vance, without preamble. He was a creature of impulse, entirely unversed in the polite hypocrisies of the drawing room. “It is my masterpiece. It is a portrait of Mrs. Asherton, in white and silver. It has taken me six months.”

“I am tremendously sorry to hear it,” murmured Lord Arthur, not looking up. “Six months is a very long time to spend with a single woman, unless one is married to her, and even then, it is generally considered excessive. However, I suppose I must see it. The *Quill* requires my monthly sacrifice. I shall look in tomorrow.”

The next afternoon, Lord Arthur stood before the canvas in Vance’s studio. It was, indeed, a magnificent thing. The white of the gown had the soft, luminous quality of moonlight on snow, and the silver seemed to hold the very chill of the metal. Mrs. Asherton looked out from the frame with the calm, slightly weary grace of a woman who has discovered the secret of life and found it to be entirely tedious.

Lord Arthur gazed at it for a long time. He felt a strange, unfamiliar stirring within him. It was not admiration, for admiration is a form of intellectual surrender, and he had long since surrendered to nothing but his own comfort. It was, rather, a sudden, sharp recognition of beauty, a beauty so absolute that it defied the scalpel of his wit. He realized, with a faint sense of panic, that he could not say a bad word about it. It was perfect.

He turned away, his mouth set in a thin, hard line. Vance was watching him, his eyes bright with the hope of the damned.

“Well?” cried the painter. “What do you think? Will it do?”

“It is a remarkable achievement,” said Lord Arthur, slowly, and the words seemed to stick in his throat like dry ashes. “I find I have nothing to say against it.”

Vance’s face fell. “Nothing?” he repeated, in a hollow whisper. “Nothing at all? You must find some flaw. You must tell me it is meretricious, or over-modelled, or that the drawing is weak. I cannot bear to be passed over in silence. If you do not insult me, I am ruined.”

Lord Arthur stared at him, a cold, cynical smile playing about the corners of his mouth. He saw the whole absurd, tragic comedy of the artistic temperament laid bare before him. The painter was begging for his own execution, pleading for the destruction of the only thing he had ever done that was truly good.

“My dear Cyril,” said Lord Arthur, at last, taking up his gloves and tapping them lightly against his palm, “you ask me to perform a miracle. I can make a reputation out of a piece of incompetence, but I cannot make a reputation out of a masterpiece. The public does not believe in excellence; it only believes in abuse. If I were to praise this picture, people would say I had lost my mind, or, worse, that you had paid me. If I ignore it, people will think you are of no consequence. If I insult it, they will flock to see it, for the English public has never been able to resist the spectacle of a man being thrown to the lions. But I am a critic, not a butcher. I deal in the slaughter of the mediocre, not the assassination of the divine.”

He paused, looking back at the portrait. The beautiful, calm face of Mrs. Asherton seemed to mock him with its serene, unassailable perfection.

“I shall write,” continued Lord Arthur, “that your picture is a triumph of the frame-maker’s art, and that the canvas has a certain noble blankness about it, which suggests the interior of a highly respectable coffin. It will be the most cruel thing I have ever written, and it will be entirely untrue. You will be famous by the end of the week.”

Vance’s eyes filled with tears of gratitude. “You are a true friend,” he said.

“I am a critic,” corrected Lord Arthur, moving toward the door. “The two are seldom synonymous. Remember, my dear boy, that in this world, the only thing worse than being talked about is being understood. You have been saved from understanding. I congratulate you.”

He stepped out into the grey, fog-shrouded street, pulling his cloak tightly about him. The fog was thick and yellow, creeping along the gutters and blurring the gas-lamps into halos of sickly gold. He walked slowly, his head bowed, a man who had just discovered the terrible, hollow price of his own power. He had reached the summit of his profession, and he found that the summit was a desert. He could destroy, but he could not create; he could wound, but he could not heal. He was the master of London’s conversation, but he had nothing left to say.

The irony of it struck him with a sudden, bitter force. He had set out to prove that criticism required no talent, and he had succeeded beyond his wildest dreams. He was a genius of denigration, a prince of the negative. And yet, standing there in the creeping, yellow fog, with the damp chill of the London evening settling into his bones, he realized that he had never felt so entirely, so hopelessly alone. He had spent his life sharpening his tongue into a rapier, and now, at the very moment of his triumph, he found that he had cut himself off from the only thing that made life worth living: the ability to be moved by beauty.

He smiled, a thin, tragic smile, and hailed a hansom cab. After all, he had a review to write.
