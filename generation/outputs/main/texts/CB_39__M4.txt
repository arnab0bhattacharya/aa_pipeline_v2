The parlour of the White Hart at Millbridge was a close and cheerless room, its windows small and its fire sullen, as though the smoke of so many anxious winters had soaked into the very mortar and would not be dislodged. Captain Markham had sat there an hour, and the landlord’s ale had grown flat in his cup, and the drum of his own thoughts had not ceased. He was a man of five-and-thirty, who had followed the colours since he was sixteen, and had learned to obey an order with the same steadiness with which he drew breath; yet there was in his present commission a savour he could not swallow, and it lay on his conscience like a stone.

The order was plain enough. One Ezra Thwaite, a weaver of the lower village, had spoken words at a meeting—words that the magistrate, Mr. Hollis, called seditious, and that the mill-owners called worse—and Captain Markham was to take a file of men, go up the lane at dusk, and bring him in. It was not the arrest that troubled him; he had arrested men before, and would again. It was the report that had come to him that morning, delivered with a smirk by the sergeant, that the weaver’s daughter, a quiet, pale girl of two-and-twenty, kept the Sunday school at the chapel in the croft, and that among her scholars was no less a person than Miss Lucy Markham, the captain’s own sister, who had come to Millbridge in the spring to keep house for him and to breathe the country air.

He had said nothing to the sergeant. He had merely nodded, and turned his face to the window, and watched the rain come down the valley in long grey spears. But the thing had worked in him all day. Lucy—his Lucy, who wrote him letters full of texts and little drawings of flowers, who had a way of looking at him with their mother’s eyes—sat at the feet of a rebel’s daughter, and learned her catechism from a tongue that had been busy with sedition. He did not know whether to be angry or ashamed. He only knew that the business must be done, and that he would do it himself, and that he would see the girl first.

The lane to the lower village was steep and miry, and the evening had closed in wet and wild, with a wind that came off the moor and rattled the shutters of the cottages. The file of men went ahead with their lanterns, and the light ran along the wet stones and made the puddles gleam like dull silver. At the door of the Thwaite cottage the captain halted his men, and bade them stand back, and knocked with his own hand.

The door was opened by the daughter. She had a candle in her hand, and the draught made it flare and gutter, so that her face was for a moment all shadow and then all light. She was not handsome, but she had a still, clear look, and her eyes were steady. She did not start when she saw the scarlet coat, nor the men behind it. She only set her lips, and said, “You are come for my father.”

“I am come for Ezra Thwaite,” said the captain, “on a warrant from the magistrate. Stand aside, if you please.”

“He is not here,” she said. “He is gone to Bradford, to see the solicitor. He will be back to-morrow, or the day after. You may search the house if you doubt me.”

The captain looked at her a moment. “I do not doubt you,” he said. “But I must do my duty. I will search the house.”

She stood aside, and he went in, and the men after him, and the little room was soon turned over—the loom, the table, the three chairs, the shelf of books, the poor ornaments of the hearth. There was nothing. The girl stood by the window with her candle, and watched them, and said never a word. When it was done, the captain came back to her.

“You are the schoolmistress,” he said, “at the chapel in the croft.”

“I am,” she said.

“My sister is one of your scholars.”

Her eyes changed a little—a faint, proud light came into them, and something that was almost a smile. “Miss Markham is a good child,” she said. “She learns quickly, and she is kind to the little ones. I am sorry you are come on such an errand, sir.”

“Sorry!” said the captain, and his voice was harder than he meant. “You are sorry, and yet you teach my sister sedition, and fill her head with notions that will ruin her peace and disgrace her name.”

The girl’s face did not flinch. “I teach her the Lord’s Prayer, and the Ten Commandments, and the parable of the good Samaritan,” she said. “I teach her to read, and to sew, and to be gentle. If that is sedition, sir, then the Bible is a seditious book, and you had better arrest the minister.”

“You know what your father has said,” he retorted. “You know the words he spoke at the meeting—words that set man against master, and make the poor a mob, and the rich a mark. Do not play the innocent with me.”

“My father is a poor man and a just one,” she said, and her voice was low but it did not tremble. “He has worked at the loom since he was seven years old, and he has never had a day’s sickness that was not earned by hunger. He spoke the truth at the meeting—the truth about the wages and the bread—and if the truth is sedition, then I am a seditious woman, and you may take me instead.”

The captain was silent. The wind rattled the casement, and the candle flared, and in the little room there was a great stillness. He looked at her, and he thought of Lucy, and of the letters she wrote, and of the way she had said, only last week, “The schoolmistress is so patient with me, brother; she says I am growing quite clever.” He thought of his own mother, who had been a poor woman before she was a soldier’s wife, and who had taught him his letters by the light of a farthing dip. And he thought of the warrant in his pocket, and the magistrate, and the mill-owners, and the whole hard machinery of the world that he had served so long.

“You are a brave girl,” he said at last, “and a foolish one. If your father is wise, he will not come back to-night. Tell him from me—Captain Markham, of the —th—that the warrant is out, and that he had better lie hid till the assizes, and then come in and answer for himself, and take his chance. I cannot help him; I would not if I could, for he has broken the law. But I will not have it said that I dragged a man from his loom for speaking the truth that every poor wretch in this valley knows.”

He turned to the door, and then he stopped, and looked back at her.

“You will not tell my sister what has passed,” he said. “She is young, and she is tender, and she does not understand these things.”

“I will not tell her,” said the girl. “She is a good child, and I would not give her pain. But, sir—when you see her, tell her that I shall expect her on Sunday, and that we are to learn the twenty-third psalm.”

The captain went out into the rain, and the men followed him, and the door was shut behind them. He walked down the lane with his head bent, and the wind and the wet beat on his face, and he was aware of a strange confusion in his breast—a fierce, impatient pain, and a kind of exultation that he could not name. He had done his duty, and he had not done it; he had served the law, and he had broken it. And in the midst of his trouble there rose before him the girl’s steady eyes, and her low, clear voice, and the candlelight on her pale face, and he knew that he should not forget them.

At the bottom of the lane he halted the men, and bade them go on to the inn, and he stood alone in the dark, with the rain on his shoulders, and looked up at the black brow of the moor. “The twenty-third psalm,” he said to himself. “The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want.” And he laughed, a short, hard laugh, and then he was ashamed of it, and he went on to the inn, and called for a fresh cup of ale, and sat down by the fire, and stared into the embers, and thought of the morrow.
