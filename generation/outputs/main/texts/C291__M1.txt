The air in the upstairs corridor of Blackwood Manor was thin and cold, smelling of beeswax and the perpetual damp of the Derbyshire hills. Eleanor Vance kept her hand pressed flat against the cool plaster of the wall, a silent, steadying gesture. She had been returning from the schoolroom, her mind on the Roman conquests she had just been explaining to the elder girl, Clara, when the voices from the drawing-room door, left ajar, had snagged her. They were the voices of her employers, Mr. and Mrs. Blackwood, and her name was in them.

“A governess must be a fixture, my dear,” Mr. Blackwood was saying, his tone the same one he used when discussing the purchase of a new carriage or the repair of the north pasture. “Something functional, to be acquired and placed. One does not form attachments to the chaise longue.”

Mrs. Blackwood’s reply was a soft, tinkling laugh. “Oh, Arthur, you are so droll. But you are right, of course. Miss Vance is most efficient. The children are well-taught and, more importantly, well-behaved. She has her place.”

Eleanor felt the world tilt slightly. A fixture. The chaise longue. The words did not sting so much as they anesthetize, freezing her into the very object they described. For two years, she had poured the entirety of her modest intellect and vast patience into these children. She had mended scraped knees, celebrated lost teeth, and wrestled with the intricacies of Latin declensions. She had believed herself to be, if not part of the family, then a respected and integral component of its running. She was not a person to them; she was a well-oiled cog in the domestic machine.

She did not wait to hear more. She moved on down the corridor, her footsteps making no sound on the worn runner. Her small room at the top of the servants’ stairs was her only sanctuary. Inside, the single window looked out over the stark, skeletal trees of the parkland. On her bedside table lay a letter from her sister in London, an invitation to come and stay, to perhaps find work as a seamstress. It was a prospect of poverty and hardship, but it was a prospect where she would be a woman, not a piece of furniture. All evening, as she read to the children and brushed Clara’s long hair, the words echoed in her mind. *A fixture. Functional. Her place.*

Later that night, a sound cut through the thick silence of the house. It was a cry, thin and sharp, followed by a frantic, muffled calling of her name. “Miss Vance! Miss Vance, please!”

Eleanor was out of bed and pulling on her dressing gown before the second call had faded. It was the nursemaid, young Meg, her face a white oval of terror in the candlelight. “It’s Master Thomas, miss. He’s burning up.”

Eleanor followed her at a run to the nursery. The little boy, only four years old, was thrashing in his cot, his small face flushed a terrible, mottled red. His breathing was a dreadful, wet rasp, and when Eleanor laid a hand on his forehead, the heat of it was shocking, a dry, fierce fire. This was no simple fever. She had seen this before, in a village in Kent, with a child who had not survived the week.

“Send for Dr. Albright,” she commanded, her voice calm and cutting through Meg’s panicked sobbing. “And fetch cool water and clean cloths. Now.”

She worked with a grim, focused purpose. She stripped the heavy quilts from the boy, leaving only a thin sheet. She wrung out cloths in the cool water and laid them on his brow, his chest, his tiny wrists. She knew the fever had to be broken, but it had to be done gently, with patience. The doctor, when he finally arrived, was a portly man with an air of impatience. He prescribed a poultice and a bitter tonic and said the crisis would come before dawn. After he left, Mrs. Blackwood appeared at the door of the nursery, a cashmere shawl wrapped around her, her face pale.

“How is he, Miss Vance?” she whispered, her eyes fixed on her son.

“He is very ill, madam. The fever is high.”

“Is there… is there anything more to be done?”

“Only to keep him cool and to wait,” Eleanor said, not looking up from the cloth she was wringing out. “I will need to remain with him. I have nursed a child through this before.”

Mrs. Blackwood looked at her, a flicker of something—uncertainty, perhaps even hope—in her eyes. “Of course. Whatever is necessary.” She lingered for a moment longer before retreating, leaving Eleanor alone in the candlelit room with the laboured breathing of the child.

The hours bled into one another. The house was silent save for the clock in the hall marking the slow, inexorable passage of time and the terrible, rattling breaths from the cot. Eleanor worked ceaselessly, changing the cloths, coaxing sips of water between Thomas’s cracked lips, whispering to him in the darkness. She spoke of sunshine and green fields, of the small black kitten in the stables he loved so much, her voice a steady anchor in the storm of his fever. This was not the work of a governess. This was the work of a nurse, a protector. This was the work of a human being, bound to another by a thread of pure, unadorned duty. The words from the drawing-room returned to her, but they were different now. *A fixture.* A fixture could be removed. A fixture had no will. But this child, this small, helpless life, was in her hands. If she left, if she packed her bag and fled to London in the morning, she would be abandoning him. Her self-respect felt like a small, cold stone in her chest, but the warmth of the child’s skin under her palm was a living, burning thing.

Around four in the morning, the change came. The frantic thrashing subsided. The terrible heat on his forehead began to recede, replaced by a damp, healthy sweat. His breathing, though still weak, lost its awful rasp and deepened into the rhythm of true sleep. It was over. The crisis had passed.

Eleanor slumped back in the chair, her body aching with exhaustion. The first grey light of dawn was seeping through the window, illuminating the dust motes dancing in the air. She watched the small, quiet rise and fall of Thomas’s chest. He was safe.

An hour later, Mr. and Mrs. Blackwood came to the nursery. They looked haggard and anxious. They rushed to the cot, and Mrs. Blackwood stroked her son’s damp hair with a trembling hand.

“He is out of danger,” Eleanor said, her voice hoarse. “The fever has broken.”

The relief on their faces was profound. Mr. Blackwood turned to her, his expression one of immense gratitude. “Miss Vance. We cannot thank you enough. You have saved our boy. You are a treasure. A true treasure to this household.”

The word, meant as the highest praise, landed with the force of a physical blow. A treasure. Something to be possessed, to be locked in a cabinet, to be valued for its utility and beauty. Not a person. Not a woman who had sat through the long, dark night, wrestling with her own demons while she fought for his child’s life. She looked from their grateful, oblivious faces to the sleeping child. She could leave. The letter was still on her table. She could walk out of this house and never look back, her dignity intact. But the child’s hand, small and trusting, was curled on the blanket. He would wake up, and he would look for her.

She gave a small, formal curtsy. “I am glad to have been of service, sir. Madam.” She picked up the basin of now-tepid water. “I will go and fetch fresh water and a broth for when he wakes.”

As she walked out of the nursery and down the corridor, she did not feel like a treasure, nor did she feel like a fixture. She felt the weight of her choice settle upon her, not as a burden, but as a cloak. She would stay. Not for them, and not for the wage. She would stay for the boy in the cot, for the duty that was hers alone. Her place, she had discovered, was not the one they had assigned her. It was the one she had claimed for herself, in the quiet, desperate hours of the night. It was a place of her own making.
