The gas-lamps of the Orpheus Theatre were still sputtering in their brackets when we entered the narrow dressing-room behind the stage. The smell of greasepaint, damp sawdust, and stale tobacco hung heavy in the air. At the centre of the room, sunk into a wicker chair with his face buried in his hands, sat the conjuror, Monsieur Valerius. His assistant, a pale-faced girl in a spangled dress, stood by the looking-glass, nervously picking at the ends of her frayed cuffs.

"You will forgive the intrusion, Monsieur Valerius," said my companion, drawing off his damp gloves with the deliberate care of a man who wishes to impress the importance of a moment upon another. "We have come from the Duchess of Cranborne, and our business is of the most pressing. You are accused of substituting a paste copy for her diamond bracelet during your performance last evening. I am here to find the truth of the matter, for I confess your story struck me as singularly honest."

Valerius looked up, his dark eyes haggard. "I am a ruined man, sir," said he, in a voice that trembled with the passionate restraint of a foreigner. "I swear before Heaven that the bracelet I returned to her Grace was the very one she placed upon the tray. I felt the weight of it. It was cold, heavy, and rough-set. The paste trinket the police discovered in my possession is one I use for the toss, nothing more. It was in my box all evening."

"The police," remarked my companion, "seldom look beyond the immediate and the obvious. Now, Monsieur Valerius, I must ask you to recount the trick precisely. Leave out nothing, however trivial. The manner in which the bracelet was passed, the words spoken, the movements of your hands, and above all, the movements of your assistant."

Valerius gestured weakly toward the girl. "It is the Vanishing Bijou. You understand the principle. I borrow a piece of jewellery from the audience. Her Grace was good enough to offer her bracelet. I placed it upon a brass tray, covered it with a velvet cloth, and carried it to a small cabinet at the side of the stage. Mademoiselle Claire was within the cabinet to receive it. I struck the cabinet with my wand. The audience saw the bracelet vanish. I drew back the cloth to reveal the empty tray. Then, Mademoiselle Claire produced the bracelet from a silk bag, having supposedly passed it through the floorboards, and returned it to me. I took it to her Grace."

"And you swear the bracelet Mademoiselle Claire handed you was the original?"

"I swear it. The settings of the Cranborne diamonds are unmistakable. They are old-fashioned, heavy, and set in dark gold."

My companion turned his sharp, gray eyes upon the girl. She was a slight creature, with a pale, intelligent face and a singularly nervous manner. Her hands, I noticed, were red and chapped, as though from rough work, and she kept the left hand hidden behind her back.

"Your name is Claire, I understand?" said my companion, with a gentleness that he reserved for frightened women and anxious clients.

"Claire Marsh, sir," she replied, in a low, clear voice.

"Tell me, Claire, did you at any time leave the cabinet during the trick?"

"No, sir."

"And when you received the tray from Monsieur Valerius, what did you do?"

"I removed the bracelet from the tray, placed it in the silk bag, and passed the bag through a slot in the back of the cabinet to Monsieur Valerius, who was waiting behind the canvas screen."

"You did not yourself leave the cabinet?"

"No, sir."

My companion drew a small magnifying lens from his pocket and examined the girl's right hand, which she had extended with a frightened air. He looked at the fingers, the palm, and the wrist, with the same minute attention that he would have given to a piece of evidence at a murder trial. He then asked to see the left hand. The girl hesitated, and with a visible effort brought it forward. The hand was not chapped like the other, but bore a small, dark stain upon the thumb.

"Ha!" said my companion, with a quick, suppressed exclamation. "Pray, how did you come by that stain?"

"It is nothing, sir. A burn from the spirit-lamp. We use them in the illusions."

"Spirits of wine do not leave a brown stain, my girl. That is the mark of silver nitrate, used by photographers in developing their plates. Come, we waste time. Where is the bracelet?"

The girl's eyes filled with sudden terror. She glanced toward the corner of the room, where a small, black travelling-trunk stood beneath a heap of theatrical draperies. My companion followed her glance. In three strides he was at the trunk. He tried the lid. It was locked.

"The key, if you please," said he.

The girl, trembling violently, drew a small key from her pocket and handed it to him. He opened the trunk, and drawing out a folded piece of newspaper, disclosed the missing bracelet, with its heavy, dark-gold setting and its glittering array of diamonds. He examined it closely, turning it over in his hand, and then, with a sudden exclamation, held it up to the gaslight.

"Look here, Doctor," said he, turning to me. "You will observe that the clasp of this bracelet has been recently tampered with. The gold has been filed away, and a small hole has been drilled through the setting. This is not the work of a thief, but of an investigator. Someone has been examining the construction of the clasp."

He turned to the girl, who had sunk into a chair. "Now, Claire," said he, in a sterner voice, "you will tell me the truth, and you will tell it quickly. You have a brother who is a photographer, I presume?"

The girl burst into tears. "It was Tom, sir. He is a photographer in Whitechapel. He is in debt, and he has been threatened. He said he only wanted to borrow the bracelet for a day, to take an impression of the clasp for a jeweller who is making a copy. He said no harm would be done, and that he would return it before morning. He made me do it, sir. He said he would tell the police about my past if I refused."

"And so, when you received the bracelet in the cabinet, you concealed it and passed out the paste copy, which you had hidden in the silk bag?"

"Yes, sir. I gave the real bracelet to Tom, who was waiting at the stage door. But when he saw the police were at the theatre, he lost heart and gave it back to me this morning. I was afraid to give it to Monsieur Valerius, for I knew I should be accused."

My companion handed the bracelet to me. "You see, Doctor," said he, "the case is clear. The substitution was made by the assistant, not by the conjuror. The paste copy, which the police found in Valerius's box, was part of the trick, and was intended to be used in the place of the real bracelet. The assistant, acting under the coercion of her brother, made the substitution at the critical moment. The brother's intention was to have a copy made, and to return the original before the theft was discovered. The timely arrival of the police, alerted by the Duchess's servants, threw his plans into confusion. I think we may inform the Duchess that her property is recovered, and that Monsieur Valerius is innocent of the charge against him."

He turned to the conjuror. "Monsieur Valerius, I congratulate you. You will, perhaps, be more careful in your choice of assistants in future. As for the girl, I shall speak to the Duchess, and I have no doubt that her Grace will be merciful. Good evening."

We passed out into the drizzling rain of the East End, and my companion hailed a hansom. "A singular instance," said he, as we rattled westward, "of the manner in which the secrets of the stage are often betrayed by those who are entrusted with them. The trick was clever, but the human element was the weak point. It is always so. The mechanism of an illusion may be perfect, but the hands that operate it are fallible. You noticed the stain of silver nitrate, of course?"

"I confess I did not."

"Ah, my dear Doctor, you must learn to observe. The stain was the key to the entire mystery. It told me that the girl was in communication with a photographer, and that she had been handling photographic chemicals. A photographer in Whitechapel, in debt, and with a knowledge of jewellery, was a very natural suspect. The rest was mere deduction."
