The intelligence reached Miss Anne Ellingham on a Tuesday morning, and in a manner so little prepared for, that she had quitted the breakfast-room with her composure entirely unbroken, and was halfway up the stairs before she understood that her life had been altered. It was Mrs. Rushworth who told her — Mrs. Rushworth, the housekeeper at Delaford Place, who had it from the butler, who had it from the groom, who had it from the very inn at Lewes — that Mr. Charles Ashby had lost eight hundred pounds at whist on the Friday night, and was to sail for the West Indies before the month was out. The news was delivered with that comfortable solemnity which servants reserve for the misfortunes of those above them, and Anne had received it with a smile and a remark upon the weather, and had gone upstairs to sit down very quietly upon the window-seat of her own chamber and look out at the park without seeing a single tree of it.

She had liked Charles Ashby, she was obliged to confess to herself, a great deal more than she had ever permitted herself to say. There had been no engagement, no understanding, hardly a word that could be produced in evidence; only four years of neighbourhood, of riding parties and harvest suppers and evenings at the pianoforte, in which he had sought her out with a constancy that might have meant much or nothing, and she had received his attentions with a composure that had cost her a great deal more than it appeared to. He was the younger son of Sir John Ashby of Stanmer Court, with a lieutenant's commission behind him and nothing before him; and she was the daughter of a country attorney's widow, with two hundred a year and a tolerable talent for drawing. It had been a hopeless sort of attachment from the first, the kind of attachment which sensible people are careful not to encourage, and Anne had been very sensible indeed — so sensible that she had never once allowed herself to name it, even in the privacy of her own thoughts, until this Tuesday morning, when the naming of it could do no good whatever.

Her friend came to her before noon. Miss Ashby — Harriet — was the elder sister, married these three years to Mr. Rushworth of Delaford, and it was to her house that Anne had come for the Michaelmas fortnight, as she came every year, to be happy in a quiet way among people who were kind to her. Harriet entered without knocking, her face disordered with weeping, and sat down upon the bed, and took Anne's hands in both of hers, and said a great many things in a very little time, of which the substance was this: that Charles was ruined, that the money was not his to lose, that Sir John would pay it and never forgive him, that the creditors were already writing letters, and that Charles was to go out in the *Ariadne* to seek his fortune in a climate that killed one man in three.

"He must go," said Harriet, with a vehemence that was half anger and half despair. "He must go, Anne, and he must go with nothing said to keep him. If he stays he will be in a worse case than ever, for my father will not pay twice, and there is no profession for him here but the one he has disgraced himself in. I have told him so myself. I have been very hard with him, and I shall be harder yet if it is required of me." She stopped, and looked at Anne with a significance that was not to be mistaken. "You know what I mean. You know very well what I mean. If you were to speak to him — if you were to give him the least encouragement — he would not go. He would stay and be ruined, and I should lose my brother twice over. Anne, I beg you will not."

Anne sat very still, and considered how strange it was that a friend should ask such a thing, and how much stranger that she should have foreseen it. "You need not have asked," said she, at last, with a smile that was not quite steady. "I have never given him any encouragement, as you call it, and I am not likely to begin now that it would be so very ill-timed."

Harriet kissed her, and cried a little more, and pronounced her the best and bravest of women; and Anne received these commendations with a composure that did her credit, and thought privately that she had never in her life been so near to breaking down.

The farewell dinner was fixed for the Thursday, and it was a very handsome dinner, and very long. Sir John Ashby presided with the air of a man who has paid eight hundred pounds and means to be cheerful in spite of it; Lady Ashby talked of the voyage and the climate and the excellent prospects of a young man of spirit in the Indies, in a voice that occasionally faltered and then recovered itself with great determination; Harriet was all affectionate vivacity; and Charles Ashby, at the other end of the table, was gay with a gaiety that had a little too much effort in it, and drank rather more wine than was his custom, and looked at Anne across the candles with an expression she took care not to see.

She was placed, by some malice of arrangement, next to Mr. Hollis, the clergyman of the parish, a widower of fifty with a great deal to say upon the subject of turnips; and she listened to him with a attention that was universally admired, and answered him at the proper intervals, and ate very little, and thought of nothing but the man at the other end of the table, who was laughing too loud at a joke of his brother-in-law's, and who would be gone in a fortnight, and whom she would never see again.

When the ladies withdrew, Harriet found occasion to press her hand, and to whisper, "You are doing nobly"; and Anne smiled and said that she hoped she knew her duty; and then she sat down at the pianoforte, because she was asked to, and played a sonata of Pleyel's with a steadiness that astonished herself. The gentlemen came in before it was over, and Charles Ashby came and stood at the end of the instrument, as he had stood a hundred times before, and turned the pages for her, and said, when the last note had died away, and the room had fallen into that pleasant murmur which follows music, "You play that better than anyone I know, Miss Ellingham. I shall think of it — out there."

"Shall you?" said Anne, lightly. "I hope you will have better things to think of. You will have a great deal to do, I daresay, and a great deal to see, and you will forget all about Sussex and the pianoforte."

"I shall not forget," said he, and there was a something in his voice that made her heart beat very fast. "Miss Ellingham — Anne — I have been a fool, and I am going away, and I may never come back; and I cannot go without telling you that if things had been different — if I had been a better man, or a richer one —"

"Mr. Ashby," said Anne, "you must not." She had turned quite pale, but her voice was steady, and she looked at him with a kindness that was worse than any coldness. "You must not say anything that we should both be sorry for. You are going away to make your fortune, and I hope with all my heart that you will make it; and when you come back — if you come back — you will find us all just where you left us, and very glad to see you." She held out her hand. "Goodbye, Mr. Ashby. I wish you a very good voyage, and a very prosperous one."

He took her hand, and held it a moment longer than was quite proper, and looked as if he would have said more; but Harriet was at his elbow, and Sir John was calling for a toast, and the moment passed. He bowed, and said, "Goodbye, Miss Ellingham," and went to the other side of the room.

Anne sat down again at the pianoforte, and played a country dance, and was very merry, and was thought by everybody to be in excellent spirits. And when she went up to bed, she sat for a long time at the window, and looked out at the dark park, and permitted herself to cry, since no one was there to see; and then she wiped her eyes, and said her prayers, and composed herself to sleep, as a woman must who has done her duty and means to go on doing it.
