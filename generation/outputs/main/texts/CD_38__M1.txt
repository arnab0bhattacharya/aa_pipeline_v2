It was a quarter to six upon a foggy November evening, in the year of our Lord eighteen hundred and forty-two, when Mr. Ephraim Scrimp sat in his counting-house upon Saffron Hill and counted the days of other men’s lives as if they were so many shillings to be weighed, found wanting, and cast into the abyss. The counting-house of Mr. Scrimp—for he was the sole proprietor, clerk, and general tormentor of the establishment known as Scrimp and Scrimp, Money Lent upon Security—was a narrow, weasel-faced den, squeezed between a chandler’s shop that smelled perpetually of tallow and despair, and a ragged warehouse that exhaled the ghosts of a thousand forgotten garments. The gas-lamp without fought a losing battle with the encroaching gloom, its sickly yellow light falling upon the rain-slicked cobbles and the hurrying, hunched figures of the poor, who scurried past the window as if the very glass might infect them with the poverty it observed.

Inside, the world was reduced to the scratching of Mr. Scrimp’s pen and the rustle of his ledgers—great, leather-bound monsters with clasps like the jaws of hungry beasts. Mr. Scrimp himself was a creature seemingly fashioned from the very materials of his trade: his skin was the colour of old parchment, his fingers were long and inky, and his eyes, deep-set beneath brows of iron-grey, were two small, hard coins that reflected nothing but the prospect of interest. He was a man who believed that every human sorrow could be expressed as a column of figures, and that the ultimate solution to all earthly woes was to make the columns balance.

It was at this precise moment, as the church bell of St. Andrew’s tolled a funereal six, that the outer door creaked open and a blast of damp, fetid air entered, followed by the man it seemed to have been blowing along. This was Mr. Millet, the bailiff, a man whose cheerful, ruddy face was a constant affront to the melancholy of his profession. He was built like a barrel and wore a greatcoat that had seen service in several more prosperous decades.

‘A cold wind, Mr. Scrimp, a cold wind that bites to the bone,’ he announced, rubbing his hands together with a sound like two stones being warmed for a purpose.

‘The wind does not concern me, Mr. Millet,’ replied Scrimp, without looking up. ‘The rent of the family at Number Seven, Bristle’s Court, concerns me. The last quarter is owing. The notice has expired. The law is a hammer, and it must fall.’

‘Ah, Number Seven,’ said Millet, his cheerfulness dimming by a crucial degree. ‘The Groggans. A sad case, sir. The father, a shoemaker, has the consumption. The mother takes in what washing she can, which is precious little. There are five children, and the eldest is but a girl of fourteen.’

‘The particulars of their misery are their own, Mr. Millet,’ Scrimp said, his voice as dry as a September leaf. ‘The particulars of their debt are mine. I have furnished you with the warrant. You will furnish me with the possession of the premises. The sum of four pounds, seven shillings, and sixpence is to be recovered if possible; if not, the goods are to be seized and the persons ejected. Is this understood?’

Millet sighed, a great, gusty exhalation that seemed to carry the weight of all the evictions he had ever performed. ‘It is understood, sir. But I must say, the sight of those children…’

‘You are not engaged to say, Mr. Millet,’ Scrimp cut in, his pen scratching a final, decisive period upon the page before him. ‘You are engaged to act. Go.’

And Millet, being a man who, for all his sentimentality, knew where his own bread and butter came from, went.

The staircase of Number Seven, Bristle’s Court, was a spiral of filth and sorrow. It was a place where the very banisters seemed to weep a greasy condensation, and where each step groaned under the foot with the protest of long-endured hardship. The air was thick with the smell of boiled cabbage, damp wool, and the faint, metallic odour of sickness. Millet, a heavy man, made the ascent with a ponderous tread, each footfall an announcement of the doom he carried in his pocket. He was preparing the words of his office—the formal, soulless phrases of the law—when a small figure detached itself from the shadows of the third-floor landing and stood before him.

It was a girl, thin and pale, with eyes too large for her face and a worn woollen shawl drawn tightly about her shoulders. Her hair was neatly braided, a small island of order in the surrounding chaos. She did not speak, but simply looked at him, and in that look was a world of knowledge, a precocious and terrible understanding of why he was there.

‘Well, miss,’ Millet began, his voice losing its official boom. ‘I am looking for the rooms of Groggan.’

‘I know why you are come, sir,’ said the girl. Her voice was quiet, but clear, without tremor or plea. ‘My father is sleeping. He is very ill.’

‘I am sorry for it,’ Millet said, and he was. ‘But I have my orders, you see. From the gentleman in the office. The rent, you understand.’

The girl nodded slowly. She reached into a small pocket in her dress, her movements deliberate and grave. When her hand emerged, it held a single object, which she held out to him upon her palm. It was a sixpence. Not a bright, new sixpence, but a worn, dark, and infinitely precious coin, its edges softened by the touch of many hands, its sovereign’s head rubbed to a ghostly impression.

‘This is all we have,’ she said. ‘It was for a little milk for my father in the morning. But if you will take it, and tell the gentleman… tell him we are good people, and we will pay the rest when we can. Tell him my mother works, and I will work too. Tell him… tell him please, not to put us out into the cold.’

Millet stared at the coin, then at the girl. He saw not a debtor, but a daughter. He saw not a case of arrears, but a sacrifice. The sixpence on her small, pale hand seemed to him the heaviest, most valuable piece of silver in all of England. It was not money; it was a plea, a prayer, a hope, all struck into a single, humble disc of metal. He felt a great and sudden lump in his throat, and his official business turned to ashes in his mouth.

‘There now, there now, my dear,’ he said, his voice thick. He gently closed her fingers over the coin. ‘You keep that for the milk. You hear me? You keep it.’

He turned and lumbered back down the stairs, leaving the girl standing in the gloom, her hand still outstretched, her face a mask of bewildered hope.

A few minutes later, the door of the counting-house burst open again, and Mr. Millet stood there, breathing heavily, his face flushed with more than the exertion of his climb. Mr. Scrimp looked up, his iron-grey brows contracting with irritation.

‘Well?’ he snapped. ‘Is it done?’

Millet shook his head. He walked to the great mahogany desk, reached into his waistcoat pocket, and placed a single sixpence upon the green baize. It lay there between them, a tiny, dark circle in the vast expanse of the ledger.

‘What is the meaning of this farce?’ Scrimp demanded, his voice rising to a querulous pitch. ‘Is this a portion of the sum? Where is the rest? Where is the warrant?’

‘The warrant is fulfilled, sir,’ said Millet, his voice low but firm. ‘The eviction is carried out. That family is put out. I saw it with my own eyes. The father, coughing his heart into a rag, the mother weeping, the little ones crying in the street. It is done. And that, sir, is the last coin they possessed in this world. The girl gave it to me to save them. I have brought it to you. It is all the payment you will ever receive for that particular misery.’

Scrimp stared from the bailiff’s grim face to the coin. His own face, for the first time in living memory, seemed to lose its rigid structure. The parchment skin slackened; the coin-like eyes widened, not with anger, but with a kind of horror. He saw the figures in his ledger—the neat columns of pounds, shillings, and pence—waver and dissolve, and in their place he saw the weeping mother, the coughing father, and the girl with the large, solemn eyes. He saw the sixpence not as a sum, but as a story. A story he had authored, with all the cold precision of a man who believed in nothing but the balance of his books.

He opened his mouth to speak, to rage, to dismiss the man before him, but no words came. The scratching of his pen had stopped. The only sound in the room was the frantic, unfamiliar beating of his own heart, a debt he suddenly realised he had incurred, and for which he had no means of payment. He sank back in his chair, a small, withered man in a great, dark room, and stared at the sixpence on his desk, which lay upon the ledger like a single, fallen tear.
