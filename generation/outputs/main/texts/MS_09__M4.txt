The rain had fallen without intermission for three days upon the town of Ashby-de-la-Wold, and the pavements of the market square, where the assizes were held, were foul with mud and the trampling of a sullen crowd. I had come hither, not as a spectator, but as a prisoner; and as the coach that bore me laboured over the last long hill, I looked upon the grey tower of the shire hall and felt that the world had ended for me before it had well begun. My name is Edmund Ashworth; I was then two-and-twenty years of age, and I had been reared in the belief that a good name was the inheritance of my house, and that the honour of the Ashworths was as stainless as the snow upon the hills of my native valley. How bitter was the knowledge that I had come to this place to answer for a crime which my soul abhorred, and to stand before the judgment of strangers as a thief and a forger!

The accusation was this: that I had counterfeited the signature of a wealthy merchant of the town, one Mr. Hollis, upon a bond for a considerable sum, and had sought to convert the same to my own use. I will not weary you with the particulars of the evidence; it is enough that it was woven with a cunning so diabolical that, had I not known my own innocence, I should have believed myself guilty. The hand that traced the forgery resembled mine so closely that even my own mother might have been deceived; and there were circumstances of time and place which gave to the whole a colouring of truth that I could not dispel. I stood alone in that crowded court, friendless and bewildered, while the counsel for the prosecution, with a cold and relentless eloquence, painted me as a monster of ingratitude and vice. My father was not there. He had remained at our house in the country, pleading indisposition; and I, who knew the secret of his absence, felt the iron enter into my soul.

For it was my father, Sir Roger Ashworth, who had committed the deed. I had discovered it by accident, a few days before my arrest, when, seeking him in his study to speak of some matter of business, I found him in a swoon upon the floor, and beside him the half-consumed bond, and a pen whose ink was yet wet upon the paper. When he recovered, he clung to me with a frantic entreaty that I would conceal what I had seen; he wept, he implored, he spoke of the disgrace that would overwhelm our ancient name, of my mother's fragile health, of the ruin that would fall upon us all. And I, who had been bred to reverence him as the pattern of honour, and who loved him with a devotion that knew no bounds, consented to be silent. I did not then foresee the consequence. I did not dream that suspicion would fall upon me, nor that my father, to save himself, would suffer me to be dragged to a prison.

The trial lasted two days. On the morning of the second, as I was brought into court, I saw my father seated in a remote corner of the gallery, wrapped in a cloak, his hat drawn low over his brow. He did not look at me. I felt my heart swell with a tumult of anguish and indignation; I could have cried aloud to him to speak, to save me, to acknowledge the truth; but my lips were sealed by the promise I had given. The jury retired, and for an hour I sat in a stupor of despair, hearing nothing but the beating of my own heart. When they returned, the foreman pronounced the word “Acquittal”; but the verdict was received in silence, and I saw upon the faces of the crowd that look which is worse than condemnation—the cold, suspicious glance that says, “He has escaped by a quibble, but we know him for a villain.”

I left the court a free man, but a ruined one. The news of my acquittal was published in the journals, but with it went whispers and insinuations that poisoned every ear. My old friends passed me in the street with averted eyes; the tradesmen of the town refused my custom; and when I returned to my native village, I found that my name was become a byword and a jest. My mother, indeed, received me with tears and embraces, but even she looked at me with a questioning sadness, as if she sought in my countenance the confirmation of a dreadful doubt. And my father—my father met me with a cold and constrained civility, as though I were a stranger whose presence was an embarrassment. He never spoke to me of the trial; he never alluded to the secret that lay between us; but I saw that he avoided my eye, and that his hand trembled when he took mine. The consciousness of his guilt had wrought upon him a terrible change; his health declined, his spirits sank, and he became moody and silent, given to long solitary walks and to the reading of books that spoke of death and judgment.

Thus passed some months. I bore my shame in silence, striving to console myself with the reflection that I had done my duty, and that the approbation of my own conscience was a sufficient reward. But the burden grew daily heavier. I was young; I had ambition, and a desire to be useful in the world; and I found every path to honourable distinction closed against me. I thought of leaving England, of seeking in a foreign land the obscurity that was denied me at home; but the idea of abandoning my mother in her declining years, and of leaving my father to the stings of his own remorse, held me back. I was torn by a thousand conflicting emotions; and often, in the solitude of my chamber, I wept like a child, and prayed that I might die.

At length a crisis came. My father was seized with a dangerous illness, and it became evident that his end was near. As I watched by his bedside through the long watches of the night, I saw upon his countenance the traces of a suffering that was not of the body. He would start from his sleep with a cry of terror, and clutch my arm, and whisper words that I could not understand. One evening, when the room was darkened and the physician had departed, he called me to him, and, with a voice broken by weakness, he said: “Edmund, I cannot die with this lie upon my soul. I have wronged you, my son; I have been a coward and a traitor. The forgery was mine. I would have confessed it at the trial, but the fear of shame withheld me; and now—now I am going to my account, and I dare not meet my God with this sin unacknowledged. Take this paper; it is a full confession, signed by my hand. Publish it to the world, and let the shame fall where it is due. I ask not for your forgiveness; I ask only for justice.”

I took the paper, and for a moment I was silent. The temptation was strong. To clear my name, to stand before the world an innocent and injured man, to see the flush of shame upon the faces of those who had condemned me—this was a triumph that my wounded spirit craved with an intensity that I cannot describe. But as I looked upon my father’s pallid face, and saw the anguish that was depicted there, a nobler feeling prevailed. I remembered his lifelong kindness, his affection, his pride in me; I remembered the promise I had made him, and the sacredness of that bond which no earthly consideration could dissolve. I knelt beside him, and, pressing the paper to my lips, I said: “Father, I will not expose you. The world may think me guilty, but I shall know that I am innocent; and the approval of my own heart, and of Heaven, is dearer to me than the applause of men. Let the secret remain with us; and may God forgive you, as I do.”

He gazed at me with a look of wonder and gratitude that I shall never forget; he strove to speak, but his voice failed him; and in a few hours he was at peace. I buried him in the village churchyard, and the inscription upon his tomb records only his virtues. The world still believes me guilty; but I have learned to bear the burden, and to find in the love of my mother, and in the consciousness of duty performed, a consolation that the world cannot give. I have taken up my abode in a distant part of the country, where my history is unknown; and here, in the cultivation of my garden and the study of the great poets, I endeavour to forget the past, and to prepare myself for that future where the secrets of all hearts shall be revealed, and where the innocent shall stand confessed before the throne of eternal justice.
