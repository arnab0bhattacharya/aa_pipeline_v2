The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which was unfortunate, for Tuesday was the day on which Mrs. Ellington received the vicar’s wife and such of the neighbourhood as felt themselves entitled to call, and a letter of consequence was not a thing to be enjoyed in company. It lay upon the hall table, a single sheet of thick, foreign paper, its seal a great blot of crimson wax stamped with an armorial device no one in the family could recollect, and its direction written in a hand so bold and so unfamiliar that the housekeeper, who had brought it in, confessed herself at a loss to know whether it came from a gentleman or a madman.

“From your brother, my dear,” said Mr. Ellington, turning it over and over in his hands as though its reverse might offer some explanation of its front. “From Frederick. After all these years.”

Mrs. Ellington, who had been pouring tea, set down the pot with a steadiness that betrayed a considerable effort. “From Frederick,” she repeated. “Good heavens. Is he alive, then?”

“He appears to be not only alive but prosperous,” replied her husband, breaking the seal with a deliberation that maddened his daughters. “He writes from Bristol. He has been in the West Indies, it seems, and has lately returned to England with a fortune — he does not specify the amount, but the tone is not that of a man who has been disappointed — and he proposes to pay us a visit before the month is out.”

The effect of this intelligence upon the assembled family was various, and might have furnished matter for a philosopher. Miss Ellington, the eldest, turned pale and said it was very unexpected; Miss Harriet Ellington, the second, turned pink and said it was very interesting; Miss Charlotte, the third, said nothing at all, but looked at her mother; and the two youngest, who had been disputing over a piece of music at the window, abandoned their quarrel entirely and demanded to know what an uncle was.

“You have one, it appears,” said Mr. Ellington drily. “Though I confess I had almost forgotten it myself.”

The history of the estrangement was, in truth, a matter of some obscurity even to those most intimately concerned. It was generally understood that Frederick Ellington had quarrelled with his father — over what, no one could now say with any certainty. Mrs. Ellington maintained that it had been a question of money; Mr. Ellington was inclined to think it had been a question of temper; and the family solicitor, who might have known, was dead. What was certain was that Frederick had left England some five-and-twenty years before, under a cloud that had never entirely dispersed, and that his name had not been spoken in the house since the old squire’s funeral, when it had been discovered that the younger son was not to inherit so much as a mourning ring.

“He will want something,” said Mrs. Ellington, with the decision of a woman who has raised five daughters and knows the world. “A man does not return from the Indies after five-and-twenty years for the pleasure of seeing his brother’s face.”

“Perhaps he wants nothing but to be forgiven,” said Harriet, who was of a romantic turn.

“Perhaps he wants to be introduced to his nieces,” said Charlotte, “and to see what we are like.”

“Perhaps he wants to cut us all off with a shilling,” said the youngest, whose name was Sophia, and who had a taste for the dramatic. “Or perhaps he wants to leave us all his money, and we shall be rich, and go to London, and have a house in town.”

“That is the least likely of all,” said Miss Ellington, who had a sober mind and a tender conscience. “A man who has been wronged does not generally return to heap benefits on the wrongdoers.”

“But we did not wrong him,” objected Sophia. “We were not even born.”

“That is very true,” said her father, folding the letter with great care and putting it into his pocket. “And yet I fancy he will not distinguish very nicely between us. Families are not just, my dear. They visit the sins of the fathers upon the children, and very often the virtues too, which is sometimes worse.”

The weeks that followed were passed by the ladies of Ellington Hall in a state of agitation that would have been ridiculous had it not been so very natural. The house was cleaned from top to bottom; the best bedchamber was aired and rehung; the shrubbery was pruned; and Mrs. Ellington, after much deliberation, ordered a new dinner service, observing that if her brother-in-law was indeed rich, it would not do to be shabby, and if he was not, it would not do to seem to expect that he was.

The theories of the young ladies as to their uncle’s character and intentions underwent a hundred revisions. Harriet, who read novels, pictured him a gentleman of melancholy aspect, with a dark complexion and a secret sorrow, who would be softened by the sight of his brother’s happy domestic circle and would settle a thousand a year upon each of his nieces upon the spot. Charlotte, who was shrewder, suspected that he would prove to be a man of business, precise and cold, who would inquire into the state of the estate and find it wanting, and would go away again without having promised anything at all. Sophia, who was fifteen, was chiefly concerned with what he would bring them, and had formed a list of desirable presents that included a shawl, a parrot, and a gold watch.

“And what do you think, Maria?” said Mrs. Ellington to her eldest, who had said little on the subject. “You have not given us your opinion.”

Miss Ellington was silent for a moment. “I think,” she said at last, “that we know nothing of him at all, and that we had better not decide what to feel until we have seen him. He may be very disagreeable, or very agreeable, or neither. But he is my father’s brother, and he is coming to our house as a guest, and I hope we shall all behave to him as we ought.”

“That is very proper,” said Mrs. Ellington, with a sigh. “But I wish, my dear, that you would not always be so very proper. It is not natural in a girl of your age.”

“I am sorry for it,” said Maria, “but I cannot help it. Somebody in a family must be proper, or the world would not go on at all.”

The day appointed for the visit came at last, and with it a carriage that was neither so fine as Harriet had hoped nor so plain as Charlotte had feared. From it descended a gentleman of middle height and middle age, with a brown face and a brisk manner, who looked about him at the house and the lawn and the five young women drawn up upon the steps with an expression that was not easily to be read. He bowed to his sister-in-law, shook his brother’s hand with a grip that made that gentleman wince, and then, standing back, surveyed the row of his nieces in silence.

“Well,” said he at last, “which of you is which? I cannot be expected to know. You all look alike to me, and none of you looks in the least like your father, for which I am sure you must be very thankful.”

There was a little gasp from Harriet, a stifled laugh from Sophia, and a general sensation that the visit had begun in a manner that no one had foreseen. Mr. Ellington, recovering himself, began the introductions; but his brother waved them aside.

“Never mind the names,” said he. “I shall learn them in time, or I shall not, and it will make very little difference. What I want to know is this — which of you has the management of the household? For I have been twenty-five years without a proper English dinner, and I mean to have one now, if it is to be had in this part of the country.”

Mrs. Ellington, who had prepared a speech of welcome, found herself obliged to abandon it. “I have the management, sir,” said she, with dignity. “And I hope you will find that we do not starve in Somerset.”

“I am very glad to hear it,” said Frederick Ellington. “For I have eaten things in the Indies that would make a Christian weep, and I have come home to be comfortable.” He looked at her with a sudden, unexpected twinkle. “And I have come home,” he added, more quietly, “to see what sort of a family my brother has made for himself. I find it larger than I expected. I hope it is as happy as it looks.”

It was not, perhaps, the most elegant beginning to a reconciliation; but it was a beginning, and the five Miss Ellingtons, who had expected they knew not what, found themselves looking at their uncle with a new and lively curiosity. The visit, it was clear, would not be dull.
