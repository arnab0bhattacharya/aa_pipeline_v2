The light upon the water was not the light of England; it was a substance, thick and golden, in which the boat seemed to move with a ponderous, dreamlike grace. Eleanor watched the mud banks slide past, the palms standing in attitudes of ancient, resigned exhaustion, and felt that the very air, which smelled of dust and damp and something sweetly rotten, was entering her not merely through her lungs but through the pores of her skin, changing her. Beside her, her father, Mr. Ashworth, adjusted the brim of his Panama hat and spoke of the price of cotton, his voice a familiar, solid thing in this fluid, shimmering world, a stone in a river of honey. But even his voice seemed to come to her from a great distance, as if the heat had thinned the air between them, and what she heard was not his words but the drone of a fly, the creak of the tiller, the slow, rhythmic dip of the oars.

She saw him then, on the shore, a figure of dust and concentration, standing before the black mouth of a tomb cut into the limestone cliff. Julian. His name was Julian. He did not look up as their dahabeeya drifted past; he was bent over a plan spread upon a crate, his pencil moving with a swift, certain gesture. And in that single, absorbed figure, Eleanor felt a sudden, sharp pang of recognition, of a world so wholly contained within itself that it seemed to cast a shadow even in this relentless sun. Later, on the shore, her father’s booming pleasantries seeming to startle the very air, she learned of his work. He spoke of strata, of foundations, of the careful, patient unpicking of centuries. His hands, which were brown and not quite clean, moved as he talked, shaping the air, describing the slow revelation of a painted floor, the ghost of a king’s profile. She watched his hands and felt the same fascination she had felt for the ancient, unchanging river.

The days acquired a new rhythm. They were measured not by the ship’s bell or her father’s complaints about the food, but by the slow uncovering of the past. She would walk to the excavation site in the cool of the morning, the dust rising around her ankles, and sit upon a sun-warmed rock, watching him and his men. The work was quiet, punctuated by the scrape of a trowel, a murmured word in Arabic, the soft thud of earth in a basket. Julian would show her a fragment of blue faience, a bead of lapis, a splinter of bone, and these small, hard things, rescued from the dark, seemed to hold more life than the living palms and the flowing water. They were not dead; they were sleeping. She felt she understood this. And when he looked at her, his eyes, narrowed against the glare, were not like her father’s, seeking to define and place her, but were simply seeing her, as he saw the strata, as a thing of layers and light.

But the light was changing. The golden quality of the afternoon was hardening, turning a fierce, white glare that bleached the colour from the world and left only shadow and heat. Her father had begun to speak of England. He spoke of damp lawns, of the scent of wet earth, of her cousin, Arthur, who was expecting them. He said it all with a kind of determined cheerfulness, as if naming these things could conjure them into existence on the banks of the Nile. “A sensible match, Eleanor,” he said one evening, as the sun bled across the water. “A man with a position. Not digging in the dirt for dead men’s trinkets.” The words fell between them, heavy and flat. Eleanor looked at her hands, resting in her lap. She thought of the lapis bead, cool and impossibly blue in her palm.

The journey north began. Luxor receded, a smudge of green and stone against the ochre cliffs. The silence on the boat was different now. It was no longer the peaceful silence of shared observation, but a thin, stretched thing, filled with her father’s determined jollity and her own unspoken thoughts. She stood at the rail, watching the wake the boat carved in the water, a white, churning line that was the only proof of their passage. The tomb, Julian, the quiet industry of the excavation—they were falling behind, becoming part of the vast, indifferent landscape. She felt a strange lightness, as if a weight had been lifted from her, and yet a profound emptiness, too. She had not said goodbye. There had been no need; their farewells were written in the dust, in the unspoken understanding that their worlds were as far apart as the sun and the deep, dark earth. She watched the sun set, a final, furious blaze of orange and purple that was swallowed by the swift, Egyptian dark, and felt the boat move on, carrying her back into a life she knew, but which now seemed as remote and fantastical as a painted wall in a tomb.
