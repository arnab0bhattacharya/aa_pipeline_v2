My dismissal from Marchmont House was effected with a promptitude that I can now regard as almost comical. The headmaster, a florid clergyman of the old school, had summoned me to his study on a Tuesday morning and informed me, with every circumstance of Christian sorrow, that my little talk to the Upper Fourth upon the descent of the horse had been reported to him by a parent of considerable influence in the county. He spoke of “dangerous speculation,” of “the tender years of the boys,” and of the necessity of “guarding the young against the insidious encroachments of a false science.” I replied—with more heat, I fear, than dignity—that the evidence for the evolution of the Equidae was as solid as the evidence for the Norman Conquest, and that a boy who could not be trusted with the one could scarcely be trusted with the other. This, I perceived at once, was a mistake. He looked at me with the pained severity of a man who has been cheated at cards by a friend, and by Friday I was upon the pavement with a cheque for a quarter’s salary and a character that described me as “conscientious but injudicious.”

For some weeks I existed in a state of genteel shabbiness in a lodging-house at Clapham, watching my small capital dwindle and considering the various ways in which a man with a first in biology and a fatal gift for candour might keep body and soul together. It was my old friend Travers, of the cramming establishment in Brixton, who rescued me. He wanted an assistant to drill dull young men in the elements of mathematics and the rudiments of physical science, and he offered me a salary that would have made a crossing-sweeper laugh, but which had the sovereign merit of being regular. I accepted it with the alacrity of a drowning man clutching a straw, and took up my quarters in two rooms above a baker’s shop in a neighbourhood of trim villas and damp laurels, where the prevailing odours were those of yeast and coal-smoke, and the prevailing sounds the rattle of the tram and the practice of the pianoforte.

Travers’s establishment was a large, gaunt house of grey brick, standing in a square of gravel that had once been a garden. The boys were of a type with which I was to become painfully familiar: heavy, good-natured, entirely uncurious young men, destined for Sandhurst or Woolwich, who regarded the binomial theorem as a kind of malignant conjuring trick and the laws of motion as an elaborate personal affront. My task was to force into their reluctant heads sufficient mathematics to carry them through the entrance examinations, and to maintain, in the interstices, a pretence of general scientific instruction. I confess I found the work dispiriting. I had come from the brisk intellectual atmosphere of a great public school, where a boy might at least be flogged into a semblance of attention; here I faced a solid phalanx of apathy, a wall of beef-fed stupidity against which the waves of my enthusiasm beat in vain.

It was in the third week of the autumn term that I first noticed the young woman in the passage outside my classroom. I had been holding forth, with more vigour than success, upon the conservation of energy, illustrating my remarks with the aid of a pendulum and a leaden weight, when the door—which I had left ajar for the sake of air—was pushed a little wider, and a face appeared in the aperture. It was a singularly intelligent face, pale, with dark eyes that seemed to take in the whole room at a glance, and it was gone again before I could do more than register its presence. I supposed it to be one of the servants, or perhaps the sister of some boy who had come to fetch him, and thought no more of it.

But she came again, on the following Thursday, and this time she remained. I had launched into an account of the nebular hypothesis—a subject upon which I was apt to become eloquent—when I became aware that the door had opened and that the young woman had slipped quietly into the back of the room, where she stood with her hands clasped before her, listening with an attention that was almost disconcerting. My boys, I need hardly say, noticed nothing; they were engaged in the absorbing occupation of drawing caricatures of me upon their blotting-paper. I continued my discourse, but I confess my voice betrayed a certain unsteadiness. When the hour struck and the boys clattered out, she remained for a moment, and then came forward to the desk.

“You are Mr. Haywood,” she said. It was not a question. “I am Edith Marchant. My brother is in your class—the fat one, Henry. He tells me you are the only master here who teaches anything.”

I murmured something deprecatory.

“I have read your book,” she went on, with a directness that took my breath away. “The one on the geological record. I found it at the circulating library in the High Street. It is the only sensible thing on the subject I have ever read.”

I was, I need hardly say, astonished. My book—a slim volume, published at my own expense, which had been received by the scientific press with a silence that was almost contemptuous—had found, it appeared, at least one reader. And that reader a young woman in a Brixton villa, the sister of one of my own dullards. I looked at her more attentively. She was perhaps three-and-twenty, plainly but neatly dressed, with a face that owed nothing to conventional prettiness but everything to animation and intelligence.

“You are interested in science?” I asked, fatuously.

“I am interested in everything that is true,” she replied. “Which is why I come here. Henry’s fees are paid, and I have as much right to listen as he has to sit there and dream. I hope you don’t object.”

I assured her that I did not object in the least.

From that day she became a regular attendant at my lectures. She would appear at the door just as I was beginning, slip into a seat at the back, and listen with an intensity that was in striking contrast to the bovine somnolence of my legitimate pupils. Sometimes, when the boys had gone, she would stay behind to question me—shrewd, searching questions, the questions of a mind that had been starved and was now determined to make up for lost time. She had read, it appeared, everything: Darwin and Huxley, of course, but also Lyell and Lubbock, and even that dangerous Frenchman, Lamarck, whose name was scarcely to be mentioned in polite society. She had opinions of her own, too, and did not hesitate to express them. I found myself looking forward to our Thursday afternoons with an eagerness that I did not at first care to examine.

The situation, of course, could not last. One afternoon in November, as we were deep in a discussion of the antiquity of man—a subject upon which she held views that would have caused the headmaster of Marchmont House to reach for his smelling-salts—the door opened and Travers himself appeared. He stood in the doorway for a moment, taking in the scene: the empty benches, the young woman seated at the back, the blackboard covered with my hasty diagrams. His face was a study in conflicting emotions.

“Haywood,” he said at last, “a word with you, if you please.”

I followed him into the corridor. He closed the door behind us with a deliberation that I found ominous.

“I have had a letter,” he said, “from Mrs. Marchant. She is concerned—very naturally concerned—about her daughter’s attendance at these—these informal lectures. She seems to think it—imprudent.”

“Miss Marchant is a person of remarkable intelligence,” I said. “She has as much right to an education as her brother.”

“That may be,” said Travers, who was a kindly man but not a bold one. “But the fact remains that she is a young woman of marriageable age, and you are a young man, and there is talk. I must ask you to discourage her visits.”

I could see the justice of his position, and I said so. But I did not, I fear, act upon it with the promptitude he desired. The following Thursday, when she appeared as usual, I told her—with what I flattered myself was a tone of firmness—that her visits must cease. She listened in silence, her dark eyes fixed upon my face.

“I see,” she said at last. “And do you propose to continue teaching here, Mr. Haywood, until you are old and grey, drilling these—these young buffaloes in the binomial theorem?”

I had no answer to that.

“I have been thinking,” she went on, “about what you said last week—about the waste of intellect, the waste of curiosity. It seems to me that the waste is not all on the side of the boys. You have a mind, and you are burying it in a Brixton classroom.”

She rose and held out her hand.

“I shall not come again,” she said. “But I hope you will not stop thinking. That would be a greater waste than any.”

She went out, and I stood for a long time at the window, watching the grey street and the dripping laurels, and thinking—as I had not thought for years—about the future. The gas-lamps were being lit, one by one, by a man with a ladder, and the trams went rattling by, and somewhere a piano was being practised with a dogged persistence that spoke of a parent’s ambition and a child’s despair. And I thought of the vast, slow, inexorable processes of nature, of the ages that had gone to the making of a horse’s hoof or a man’s hand, and of the small, absurd, infinitely pathetic business of a young woman with a hungry mind in a London suburb, and of a science master who had been too great a coward to tell her so.

I did not see her again. But I have not forgotten.
