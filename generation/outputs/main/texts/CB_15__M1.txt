The valley of Blackstone, when I first took hold of it, lay under a sky the colour of a bruised plum, threatening a storm that would not break. From the high windows of Thornleigh Manor I could see the mills down by the river, their chimneys vomiting black smoke that smudged the very hills. It was my inheritance, this smoke and these hills, this house and these people; and I was determined that it should be mine in more than name.

My uncle, Mr. Fairfax, did not see it so. “Ellery, child,” he said, his voice carrying the soft, insufferable weight of paternal concern, “this is not a woman’s work. The ledgers, the leases, the very coal-dust of the place—it is a man’s world. You are to be its ornament, not its manager.”

He spoke to me thus in the library, a room he kept as dark and stuffy as a tomb, the air thick with the scent of old leather and his own stagnant will. Outside, the wind was beginning to howl round the gables. I felt a surge of something hot and fierce rise in my breast. “The ornament may also be the architect, Uncle. I have read my father’s papers. I see how the rents have been raised while the wages have been cut. I see the cottages at Cragg’s End with their leaking roofs. An ornament does not see these things; an owner must.”

He stared at me, his face a mask of pained astonishment. “You are talking of injustice where there is only industry. Of hardship where there is only necessary discipline. Your father, my brother, was a man of sense. He knew that sentiment is a luxury a mill owner cannot afford.”

“And I,” I returned, my voice hard and clear as struck flint, “am a woman of sense who cannot afford the luxury of ignorance.” I left him then, the wind catching the door and slamming it behind me with a violence that echoed my own heart.

The next day I rode out to the tenanted farms on the estate. The scandal preceded me. As I passed through the village, women at their doorways drew their shawls tighter, their eyes following me with a mixture of fear and curiosity. I heard the word ‘madness’ whispered on the wind. A woman, managing her own affairs, was a kind of monstrosity, a disruption in the natural order as sure as a river flowing uphill.

I found the tenant, a man named Harker, in his field, his back bent over a broken plough. He was old, his face a roadmap of hardship. When I dismounted and spoke to him of the repairs to his cottage, he could not meet my eye. “It is not for me to say, Miss Ellery,” he mumbled, twisting his cap in his hands. “Mr. Fairfax, he manages all that.”

“Mr. Fairfax is not the owner,” I said, my patience fraying. “I am. And I say the roof shall be mended.” He only shook his head, a deep, abiding terror in his eyes, as if my very presence was a calamity. I saw then that my uncle’s obstruction was not merely in the ledgers; it was in the minds of the people, a tyranny of expectation.

It was the parson, Mr. Davies, who first treated me not as a madwoman, but as a combatant worthy of his wit. I met him on the high moor road, the wind tearing at my bonnet, the sky now a sheet of driving grey. He was a small, wiry man with eyes that sparkled with an unholy amusement.

“Miss Ellery!” he called out, his voice cutting through the gale. “I hear you are waging war upon the established order! A new Amazon, come to liberate the serfs of Blackstone.”

“I am merely tending to my own property, Mr. Davies,” I replied, reining in my horse. “Is that so revolutionary?”

“In this valley? My dear lady, it is more revolutionary than the French guillotine. You are upsetting the men, who feel their dominion threatened, and the women, who feel their security threatened. You are, in short, a walking, talking scandal.” He smiled, a quick, sharp flash. “And I confess, it is the most interesting thing to happen here since the great flood of ‘98. Do be careful, though. The world prefers its women to be beautiful statues, not active volcanoes.”

His words, though meant to amuse, struck me with the force of a prophecy. That evening, a letter arrived from my uncle, informing me he had taken the liberty of signing a new five-year lease with a Mr. Thornton, a manufacturer from the next valley, for the use of the lower water-wheel. The terms, he noted, were ‘most advantageous.’ I knew at once it was another chain, another man placed between me and my own estate. The injustice of it was a physical pain, a burning in my throat.

I did not wait for the morning. Taking a lamp, I strode through the howling dark to my uncle’s part of the house. I found him in his study, a glass of port in his hand, looking supremely satisfied. I laid the letter on the table before him. The lamp flame threw our shadows, monstrous and wavering, upon the walls.

“You will retract this, Uncle,” I said. My voice did not shake.

He looked at me with a kind of weary condescension. “Ellery, you are overwrought. This is for the best. You are not fit for these negotiations.”

“Not fit?” The words were a spark to the tinder of my soul. “I am more fit than you, who use my name to bind my hands. You speak of my father’s sense, but you betray his trust at every turn. You call my concern sentiment, and my will madness. But I tell you what is madness: it is a man’s belief that a woman’s mind is a plaything, that her property is his to dispose of, that her independence is a disease to be cured! It is you who are mad, mad with the lust for power, and I will not be your victim!”

I stood there, my chest heaving, the storm outside seeming but a whisper compared to the tempest in my own heart. He stared at me, his face pale in the lamplight, and for the first time, I saw not condescension in his eyes, but a flicker of something else—fear. He saw not his niece, but a force he could not control. He saw the volcano.

He did not answer. He simply crumpled the letter in his fist. The battle was not over, I knew. But as I turned and walked back into the storm, the wind on my face felt like a baptism. They could call me mad, they could call me a scandal. But I was awake. And the estate, my estate, would be mine. The smoke from the mills, for the first time, did not look like a stain upon the sky, but a sign of life, of labour, of a world I was now determined to command.
