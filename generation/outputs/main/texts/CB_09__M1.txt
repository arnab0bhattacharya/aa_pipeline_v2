The letter, when it came, was as flat and unadorned as the grey stone of the courtyard below. It bore the franking of London, and the stern, impersonal seal of the banking house of Messrs. Harkness & Croft. Miss Elinor Vance held it for a moment in the chill of the autumn morning, her breath misting in the cloistered air of the Pensionnat de Sainte-Claire. She knew, with a premonition as sharp and cold as the air itself, that it contained no good news. She slipped the letter into her pocket, to be read in the solitude of her small chamber, and turned to the sound of girls’ voices, a sound like the chattering of starlings before a storm.

All that day she taught. She drilled the fractious daughters of the city’s merchants in the intricacies of English grammar, and led the more docile pupils through the sonorous, melancholy verses of Lord Byron. Her voice remained level, her hand at the blackboard steady. She corrected pronunciation with a patient firmness, and praised a well-turned translation with a smile that did not reach her eyes. Inside her, a great stillness had fallen, the terrible quiet at the heart of a whirlwind. The words of the letter were etched upon her mind as if with a hot iron: *regret to inform… unforeseen liabilities… total loss of assets… deepest sympathy…*. The sum—her entire savings, the legacy of a dead aunt, the fruit of five years of pinching and careful economy—was gone. Swallowed by a maw of silent, respectable greed in a city she could no longer afford to call home.

When the last lesson was over, and the girls had scattered to their play in the walled garden, Elinor ascended the narrow, winding stair to her room. It was a cell, furnished with a bed, a washstand, and a single chair. The window looked out not upon the street, but upon a high brick wall, from which a single, stubborn vine grew. She sat upon the bed and read the letter again, though she knew its contents by heart. Then she folded it, placed it in her small writing-desk, and locked it away, as if she could imprison the disaster along with the paper.

Pride was a formidable fortress, and Elinor Vance was its sole and unyielding defender. She would not speak of this. To whom could she speak? To Madame Dubois, the directress, a woman whose kindness was a thin veneer over a core of flinty practicality? To see pity in her eyes, or, worse, a calculating assessment of Miss Vance’s future utility? The thought was a humiliation she could not endure. She would not be an object of charity. She was a gentlewoman, however poor, and she would live and die with her dignity intact.

That evening, she took out a sheet of foolscap and her inkstand. By the light of a single tallow candle, whose flame sputtered and threw leaping shadows on the wall, she began to calculate. The figures were stark, merciless. Her salary, which had seemed a pittance, was now a lifeline. Her rent to the school, her meals, the small sums for soap, candles, and mending. She subtracted and added, her brow furrowed, her lips pressed into a thin, bloodless line. The candle burned lower, wax dripping like slow tears onto the wooden table. At last, she arrived at a number. Four months. She could survive for four months. After that, there would be nothing. She would be destitute, a stranger in a foreign land, with no recourse but to throw herself upon the mercy of the one man whose mercy she most dreaded to ask: Monsieur Leclerc, the director.

Monsieur Leclerc was a man of immense, silent presence. He moved through the school like a shadow, his eyes missing nothing. He was not unkind, but he was exacting, and he valued order and self-sufficiency above all else. To approach him, to lay her poverty and her failure at his feet and beg for an advance upon her salary—it would be like stripping herself bare. It would be an admission that she had failed to manage her own affairs, that she was a dependent, a burden. Her soul revolted against it. Better to walk out into the cold, unknown streets with nothing but the clothes on her back. Better to starve.

A sudden gust of wind rattled the windowpane, and a spatter of rain struck the glass. The storm that had been threatening all day was upon them. Elinor rose and looked out at the sliver of sky visible above the wall. It was the colour of a bruise, churning and violent. She thought of the sea, of the vast, indifferent ocean that separated her from England, from all that was familiar. She felt utterly alone, a single, upright reed in a gathering tempest. But as she watched the rain lash the stubborn vine against the brickwork, a strange resolve hardened within her. She would not break. She would teach her lessons with the same care. She would mend her stockings until they were more thread than cloth. She would eat less. She would endure. For four months, she would live as if she were made of iron and stone. And on the last day of the fourth month, if no miracle had intervened, she would walk into Monsieur Leclerc’s office, and she would speak the words that must be spoken. But until that day, not a soul would know. This was her trial, and she would meet it alone. The rain fell harder, washing the courtyard stones clean, and in the heart of Elinor Vance, a fierce, cold fire was lit.
