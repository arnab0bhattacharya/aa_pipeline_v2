The pension stood at the upper end of the town, where the streets, having climbed with a certain breathless insistence from the lake-side, faltered at last into a quiet terrace and a few trees that seemed to have grown weary of the ascent. It was a tall, narrow house of a faded yellow, with green shutters that were closed against the afternoon sun, and from the window of her room on the third floor the Countess Alexandrovna Sergeievna Varvinsky could see, beyond the chestnut-trees of the terrace, a portion of the lake lying like a plate of dull silver under the haze, and the mountains beyond it, remote and pale, as though they belonged to another world than this one of cheap boarding-houses and indigent respectability. She had been a widow these three years—or, to speak with the precision that her situation demanded, she had been a wife who had buried her husband; for the distinction, she had come to feel, was not without its importance—and she lived here in a retirement that was less a choice than a necessity, upon an income that had shrunk with every year of her exile until it had become a thing of shreds and patches, eked out by the sale of a brooch, a ring, a piece of old lace, and by the occasional generosity of a cousin in Petersburg who was himself not entirely secure.

It was in this condition of things, upon an afternoon in late September, when the light had begun to take on that melancholy softness which is the peculiar gift of the Swiss autumn, that the servant brought her a card, and she read upon it the name of Monsieur Étienne Delacroix, and beneath it, in smaller letters, the words *ancien secrétaire*. She sat quite still for a moment, the card between her fingers, and her face, which was still handsome in a worn, distinguished fashion, betrayed nothing beyond a slight contraction of the brows. Then she said, in French, that the gentleman might come up.

He entered with the air of a man who has rehearsed his entrance and is not entirely satisfied with the performance. He was of middle height, thin, with a sallow complexion and dark eyes that seemed to have receded a little into his head, giving him a look of sleepless vigilance. His clothes were decent but shabby, and he carried his hat in his hand as though it were an object of suspicion. He bowed, and she inclined her head, and for a moment they regarded each other across the little table that held the lamp and a few books from the circulating library.

"You have changed," she said at last, in a tone that was not unkind.

"Madame," he replied, "we have all changed. The world has changed. It is the fashion to change."

"Sit down, Monsieur Delacroix. You will take some tea?"

He refused the tea with a gesture that was almost abrupt, and then, as though recollecting himself, he sat down on the edge of the chair she indicated, and placed his hat carefully upon the floor beside him. There was a silence, during which she heard the distant tinkle of a tram-car and the voices of children in the terrace below.

"I have come," he began, and then stopped, and passed his hand over his forehead. "I have come, madame, upon a matter which I fear will appear to you—will appear—" He hesitated, and she saw that his hand was trembling.

"You have come to ask me for something," she said. "That is evident. You were my husband's secretary for five years. You were with him when he died. You know that I have very little, and that what I have is not easily parted with. But tell me what it is."

He looked up at her then, and for the first time there was something in his eyes that was not entirely the look of a suppliant.

"I have joined the Cause," he said.

"The Cause?"

"The revolution. The party. I have been three months in Geneva, and from there I go—elsewhere. I am not at liberty to say. But I have come to you because—because I remembered that you were once—that you had the reputation—" He broke off, and then went on with a kind of dogged resolution. "We need money, madame. Not for myself—I have learned to live on very little—but for the work. There are men who are waiting, who must be fed, who must be armed, who must be hidden. I have no right to ask you. I know what you think of us. But I have come."

She had listened without moving, her hands folded in her lap, and now she said, very quietly:

"You are mistaken, Monsieur Delacroix. I have no sympathy with your cause. I have never had. I am not one of those women who amuse themselves with revolutions. My husband—" She paused, and a faint colour came into her cheeks. "My husband was a man of order. He served the Emperor. He died in the service of the Emperor. I do not say that he was always wise. I do not say that the world was well ordered. But I know what revolution means. I have seen it. I have seen the house of my father burned, and my brother shot in the garden, and my mother—" She stopped again, and this time it was she who looked away. "I have seen enough."

"I know," he said. "I know. And yet—"

"And yet you come to me."

"I come to you because you are the only person in the world who has ever been kind to me. Because when I was a young man, ignorant, awkward, ashamed of my poverty, you spoke to me as though I were a human being. You gave me books. You told me—" He made a gesture of helplessness. "It is not for the Cause that I come, madame. It is for myself. I am ashamed. But I am also—I am also desperate."

She rose from her chair and went to the window, and stood with her back to him, looking out at the lake. The haze had thickened, and the mountains had dissolved into it, and there was nothing but a grey immensity, without form or feature. She thought of her husband, of his heavy, handsome face, of his voice, which had been loud and confident and had never doubted itself. She thought of the house in Petersburg, of the servants, of the winter evenings when the samovar had hissed upon the table and the guests had talked of nothing. She thought of the letter that had come to her in Paris, three years ago, telling her that he was dead, and that there was nothing left, and that she must not return. And she thought of this man, this thin, shabby, trembling man, who had been young once, and had believed in something, and had sat at the foot of the table, silent, while the great ones talked.

She turned back into the room.

"How much?" she said.

He named a sum. It was not a large sum, as such sums go, but it was very nearly all that she had.

She went to a little writing-desk in the corner, and unlocked it, and took from a drawer a roll of notes and some gold. She counted them out upon the table, and then she stood looking at them, and at him, and there was in her face an expression that he could not read—something that was not contempt, and not pity, and not quite resignation, but a little of all three, and something else besides, that was like the ghost of an old tenderness.

"Take it," she said. "I do not give it to the Cause. I give it to you. And now go. And do not come back."

He rose and took the money, and put it into his pocket, and then he stood before her, holding his hat, and it seemed that he would have spoken, but no words came. He bowed very low, and she inclined her head, and he went out, and she heard his footsteps descending the stairs, slowly, as though he were very tired.

She stood for a long time by the window, watching the grey haze, and the lights beginning to twinkle along the lake-side, and the mountains that were no longer there. And she thought that she had done a foolish thing, and perhaps a wicked thing, and that she would never see him again, and that it did not matter very much, one way or the other, in a world that was so dark and so confused and so full of the sound of distant, inexplicable things. Then she rang for the servant, and asked that her tea should be brought up, and sat down to wait for it, with her hands folded in her lap, and her eyes fixed upon the door.
