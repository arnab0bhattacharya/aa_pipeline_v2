# The Red Sea

There are moments in a man's life when the whole edifice of his careful morality—that structure he has built, beam by beam, over years of quiet, unremarkable conduct—trembles before him like a house in a gale, and he perceives, with a clarity that is almost blinding, how thin is the partition between the order he has sworn to uphold and the chaos he has been appointed to hold at bay. I speak of a particular night. The Red Sea, that narrow furnace of water between the deserts, lay around us like molten copper under a sky so thick with stars it seemed the heavens themselves had been crushed and scattered. There was no wind. The heat came off the water in visible waves, and the steamer *Meridian*—an iron hull, six hundred tons, carrying a cargo of machinery parts and cotton bales to a port whose name no longer signifies—pushed through that stifling sea with the laborious certainty of a thing that knows its duty and performs it without pleasure. I had been three years her captain. I was thirty-four.

It was the fourth hour of the middle watch, and I had come on deck not from anxiety—I had no cause for anxiety then—but because the heat below had become a species of torment, a physical oppression that pressed upon the chest and turned the act of breathing into an act of will. I found the second mate at the rail, a young man called Hessler, and he was staring aft with an expression I did not at first comprehend. It was the expression of a man who has witnessed something he cannot reconcile with the order of things as he understands them, and who is therefore in the process of revising, silently and with some distress, his understanding of that order. He looked at me. He looked away. He said nothing. And it was this silence—that particular, weighted silence—which first planted in my mind the seed of unease.

"What is it, Mr. Hessler?" I asked.

He swallowed. I could see the movement of his throat in the light from the binnacle. "The course, sir," he said. "I've only just—" He stopped himself, as a man stops himself on the edge of an admission that cannot be unmade.

I went to the chart-room. I did not hurry. I remember that—my deliberateness, as though some part of me already understood what I would find and was pacing its own approach to the knowledge. The chart was there, pinned to the table, and beside it the slate with the noon reckoning, and beside that the work-book in which Mr. Vranna, my chief officer, entered his calculations with a hand that I had always considered steady. I took up the book. I read the figures. I read them again. I took the dividers and measured the distance between the last verified position—a fix obtained off the island of Perim, nine days past—and the position Vranna had plotted for noon that day. I measured it a third time. The dividers trembled in my fingers, and I set them down with a care that was, I think, the care of a man handling something that has already begun to destroy him.

The course was wrong. Not slightly wrong—not the gradual, accumulative drift of a degree or two that might be attributed to current or magnetic disturbance. It was wrong in a way that spoke of sustained, deliberate falsification. For seven days the *Meridian* had been steaming a heading that bore no resemblance to the one I had set and which Vranna had recorded in the log-book with his own hand. We were, by my calculation, nearly a hundred and forty miles north-north-east of our plotted position, in waters I had not intended to enter, where the navigational hazards were of a character I had not prepared for.

I sat on the bench in the chart-room and I felt the heat press against me like a hand, and I understood something about the nature of trust which I had perhaps always known but never before confronted. Vranna. I had known him eleven years. We had served together as young men on the India run, he a third mate then and I a fourth, and I had watched him—had indeed admired him—for the quality of his attention, the gravity with which he approached the business of keeping a ship and her people from harm. He was not a clever man. He was not a man of insight or imagination. But he was thorough, and he was true, or I had believed him true, which amounts to the same thing when one is dealing with the invisible, interior province of another man's conduct. And now this.

I went to his cabin. The door was unlocked—there are no locks on a merchant vessel, or there were not then, not among officers—and I entered with the lamp held before me. He was asleep on his bunk, fully clothed, and the smell of spirits was so strong in that small space that it seemed to have acquired a physical presence, a density, a weight. On the table beside his berth stood a bottle, nearly empty, of a colour that was not quite amber and not quite brown. I looked at it. I looked at him. He slept with the absolute abandonment of the intoxicated, his mouth open, his face emptied of all its usual guardedness, and I saw him then as I had perhaps refused to see him for months: a man who had been sinking, by degrees, into a private catastrophe from which he had neither the strength nor the will to rescue himself.

I stood there. I do not know how long. The lamp guttered in the draught from the open door, and the shadows moved across his face as though it were a landscape over which clouds were passing, and I was aware—acutely, painfully—of the iron deck beneath my feet, of the steady vibration of the engines, of the two hundred souls aboard my ship who were at that moment sleeping in the trust that their captain and his officers knew where they were. And I thought of what would happen if I reported this. The enquiry. The disgrace. Vranna, at fifty-two, stripped of his certificate, finished—not merely as an officer but as a man, for the sea is all he had, and to be cast out of it is a variety of death from which there is no resurrection. I saw it all with a terrible precision, the whole machinery of consequence, and I saw too the other thing—the other possibility—that if I said nothing, if I corrected the course in silence and carried this knowledge like a stone in my chest, we might yet make port, and no one need ever know.

But that is a lie, is it not? For I would know. And he would know that I knew. And between us, from that moment forward, there would exist a conspiracy of silence—a compact of deceit—which would corrode the very authority I depended upon to keep this ship alive. A captain who has concealed the dereliction of his chief officer has, in that act, become derelict himself. He has made himself an accomplice. And yet—and here is the moral absurdity, the thing that appals—what alternative remained? To ruin a man for a weakness he could not control? To break him because he had broken already?

I left his cabin. I closed the door. I went to the bridge and I altered the course myself, and I stood there in the ghastly heat of that Red Sea night with my hands on the wheel-house rail and I understood, for the first time, that command is not what I had thought it was. It is not authority. It is not knowledge. It is the capacity to bear what cannot be borne and to act in the space between what is right and what is possible, where no clean choice exists, and where every decision is a kind of betrayal.

Hessler never spoke of it. Whether he understood what he had seen that night, or what I had done in the hours that followed, I cannot say. He was a young man, and young men see what they are shown. What is hidden from them, they often accept as simply the shape of the world—and perhaps they are not wrong, for the world has shapes enough to fill a lifetime with misapprehension.

We made port eleven days later. Vranna stood his watches until we anchored, and he was, to all outward appearance, the same officer he had always been. I signed his discharge certificate with a hand that did not tremble. I gave him a good character. I have sometimes wondered whether that was an act of mercy or an act of cowardice, and I have concluded that the distinction, in that place and at that hour, was one I was not equipped to make. We live, as we must, in the dark of our own judgments, and it is only afterward—afterward, when the heat has lifted and the sea is behind us—that we believe we see clearly. But I am not certain we ever do. I am not certain we see anything at all.
