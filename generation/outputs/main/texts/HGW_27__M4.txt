The trouble with living in the Daylight City, as anyone in the Bureau of Illumination could tell you, is that it is a great deal easier to maintain a permanent noon than to explain why you should want to. I had been three years in the Bureau before the notion so much as crossed my mind, and I am not a stupid woman; I was simply a busy one, which in those latitudes amounts to the same thing. My name is Ellen Vane, and I was a Junior Luminance Adjuster, Third Grade, which meant that I spent my days in a grey room watching dials and correcting, by the turn of a vernier, the great suns that hung above the city like so many captive moons.

You must understand the arrangement of the place. The Daylight City stands in a broad valley, ringed by black mountains that nobody visits, and above it, at a height of some four hundred feet, are suspended the twelve Helions—vast, pearly, luminous spheres, each a hundred yards across, which burn day and night with a cold and perfect radiance. There is no night. There has not been a night for sixty years. The founders of the city, being practical men, reasoned that darkness was a relic of savagery, that crime and melancholy and superstition all flourished in it, and that a community which could afford twelve suns could very well afford to do without the moon. They were right, so far as they went. The city is clean, cheerful, industrious and quite remarkably free from footpads. The only thing it has mislaid, in the process, is the knack of being afraid of the dark—and, with it, the knack of being anything at all when the lights go out.

I discovered the failing in the autumn of my third year, during the routine inspection of Helion Number Seven. The Helions are not, as the public imagines, simple lamps. Each is a shell of phosphorescent glass enclosing a core of radiant matter that must be fed, cooled, and tuned, and it was my business to tune Number Seven. I had climbed the service mast—a giddy business, the wind up there being considerable—and was standing on the little iron gallery with my instruments about me, when I noticed the first irregularity. The light was pulsing. Not the gross flicker of a failing lamp, but a slow, rhythmic throb, like a pulse, a fraction of a shade deeper at one moment and paler at the next, with a period of perhaps eleven seconds. I checked my chronometer and checked it again. Eleven seconds. I noted it in my book, and thought no more about it until the following Thursday, when Number Three did the same, and then Number Eleven, and then, in the space of a fortnight, all twelve of them, in a slow and stately rotation, as though the suns themselves had begun to breathe.

I reported the matter to my superior, Mr. Harbottle, a large, pink, comfortable man who had risen through the Bureau by never once in forty years having been surprised by anything. He listened to me with his customary patience, examined my figures, and returned them to me with a smile.

“Resonance, Miss Vane. The Helions are tuned to a common frequency, and a common frequency will beat. It is a well-known phenomenon. The founders allowed for it.”

“The founders,” I said, “allowed for a beat of eleven seconds?”

“The founders,” said Mr. Harbottle, with the least trace of asperity, “allowed for everything. It is the great merit of founders.”

I might have let it rest there—I was a Third Grade Adjuster, and my prospects depended on a certain docility—had I not been sent the following month to the Central Accumulator, the great vault beneath the city where the radiant matter is refined and stored. It is a place of enormous silence and a smell of hot metal, and I was left alone in it for the better part of an afternoon, waiting for a clerk who never came. And there, idly turning the pages of the maintenance ledger, I found the entries for the last sixty years set down in a score of different hands, and I saw what no one in the Bureau had troubled to see: that the consumption of radiant matter had risen, decade by decade, in a curve as smooth and remorseless as the flight of an arrow. The Helions were not merely breathing. They were starving. The founders had built twelve suns and provided for them a store that any reasonable man would have called eternal; but the founders had reckoned without the city’s growth, without the doubling and redoubling of the load, without the thousand little conveniences—the luminous paint, the glowing paving, the perpetual lamps in every nursery and corridor—that had been added, year upon year, by men who assumed that the store was infinite because it was large. It was not infinite. At the present rate, the Accumulator would be dry in a little under nine years. And when it was dry, the Helions would not fail gracefully, one by one, in some orderly fashion that could be met with candles. They would go out together, in a single breath, and the Daylight City would discover, all at once and for the first time in sixty years, what the world is like at midnight.

I went to Harbottle, and then to the Deputy Director, and then, in a final access of zeal, to the Director himself, a small, dry, courteous man named Pellow, who heard me out and then asked me, very gently, what I proposed. I said that the consumption must be reduced; that the Helions might be dimmed by a third at those hours when the city slept, and a system of public warnings instituted, and the people taught, gradually and without panic, to accustom themselves to a few hours of dusk. Mr. Pellow inclined his head.

“And who,” he said, “will teach them?”

“The Bureau. The schools. The newspapers.”

“Miss Vane,” said Mr. Pellow, “you have lived here three years. Have you ever seen a dark room?”

I had not. It had never occurred to me to look for one.

“Nor has anyone,” he went on. “We have bred a generation that cannot sleep without a light in the corridor and cannot walk abroad without a sun overhead. They are not cowards; they have simply never been asked. If the Bureau announces that the suns must be dimmed, there will be a run upon the candle-shops by nightfall and a committee of public safety by the end of the week, and the committee will vote the Helions up to full power to calm the public mind, and we shall be nine years nearer the end and not one hour better prepared. You cannot teach a whole city to be brave in a fortnight. You can only teach it, if you are patient, in a generation—and we have not a generation. We have nine years, and I am sixty-three.” He was silent for a moment. “What you have found,” he said at last, “is not a mechanical fault. It is a moral one. The machine is doing exactly what it was built to do. It is the people who have forgotten what they were built for.”

I left his office in a state of considerable agitation, and it was on the stair that I had my idea—an absurd, undignified, and entirely practical idea, which I shall set down here because it is the only thing I did in the whole business of which I am not ashamed. I went to the printing office of the Evening Intelligencer, and I paid, out of my own pocket, for a small advertisement in the lower left-hand corner of the second page, where it would be read by nobody who was not looking for it. It ran as follows: “TO ALL WHO ARE NOT AFRAID OF THE DARK. A meeting will be held on Thursday next, at nine of the clock, in the disused reservoir under Kettle Street. Bring no lights.” I had the reservoir in mind because it was the one place in the city that the Helions could not reach, being roofed in iron and forty feet beneath the pavement, and I had the phrase “bring no lights” in mind because I wanted, quite deliberately, to see who would come.

Eleven people came. Eleven, out of a city of two hundred thousand. There was a lamplighter, an old woman who kept pigeons, a schoolmaster, two children, a night-watchman who had never in his life had a night to watch, and five others whom I have forgotten. We sat in the dark of the reservoir, and I told them what I have told you, and we talked for two hours, and by the end of it the lamplighter—his name was Crump, and I have cause to remember it—had proposed that we should meet again the following week and each bring one other person, and that we should begin, quietly and without any announcement whatever, to learn the business of darkness. We began with the children, who took to it like ducks to water. We taught them to find their way by touch and by counting steps, to strike a match in a high wind, to sit still in the dark and not be afraid. By the spring there were three hundred of us. By the summer there were two thousand, and we had printed, on a hand-press in Crump’s cellar, a little pamphlet called *Hints for the Unlit*, which was read, I am told, in circles far beyond our own.

The end, when it came, came four years early, which is the way of machines. Helion Number Nine went out on a Tuesday in November, at twenty minutes past three in the afternoon, and the city went mad in the most orderly fashion imaginable. There was no rioting; there was something worse. People stood in the streets with their hands at their sides, waiting for someone to tell them what to do. The Bureau voted an emergency increase to the remaining eleven, which lasted them six weeks. And then, one evening at dusk—for we had dusk now, by degrees, and the city had grown used to a sort of perpetual overcast—the eleven went out together, and the Daylight City had its first night in sixty years.

I shall not describe the panic. It was brief and it was ugly and it was, in the end, survivable, which is more than the founders would have predicted. What I shall describe is the morning after, when Crump and I climbed the service mast to Number Seven with a box of matches and a handbook of instructions, and found, on the iron gallery, a small crowd of citizens who had come up the ladders in the dark to see what could be done. They were not afraid. They had brought candles, and they had brought sandwiches, and one of them—a girl of about twelve, I remember—had brought a notebook, in which she had written down everything she had learned about the dark. We lit the Helion at a quarter past six, and the light came up slowly, as it always does, and the city below us made a sound I had never heard before and have heard many times since: the sound of a great many people, all at once, being glad of the morning.
