The sea was a slate-grey flatness under the November sky, heaving with a quiet, relentless malice. It mirrored the disposition of Bryn Mawr, the house that clung to the Welsh cliffs like a barnacle refusing to be washed away. Inside, the air was thick with the scent of peat smoke and damp stone. Caron moved through the dim corridor, her hand trailing along the wainscoting, a habitual gesture that left no print on the dust-free wood. Her uncle, Idris, kept the house scrupulously clean, a sterile sanctuary against the wildness outside. She had lived within these walls for all her nineteen years, venturing into the village of Aberffrew only on rare errands, always under Idris’s watchful, pale-blue eyes.

It was in the attic, while searching for a specific length of blue velvet Idris had requested for a draught excluder, that Caron found the box. It was shoved deep into the eaves, behind a stack of empty birdcages. It was a small thing, made of dark, scuffed wood with a brass clasp gone green with verdigris. Caron brushed away a thick layer of cobwebs and flipped the latch. Inside, nestled in a bed of faded crimson silk, was a miniature portrait.

She drew it out, her breath catching in her throat. The frame was heavy gold, and the portrait within was exquisitely detailed. It depicted a young woman, perhaps a year or two younger than Caron, with dark hair escaping its pins in curling tendrils around a heart-shaped face. But it was the eyes that arrested her: large, intensely grey, and set beneath brows that winged up at the outer corners. They were her own eyes, set in her own face. The woman in the portrait wore a gown of deep emerald velvet and a solitary pearl drop on a black ribbon around her throat. Caron touched her own neck, feeling the frantic pulse beneath her skin. She had never seen a mirror that offered such a perfect reflection. It was like looking into a pool of still water and seeing herself staring back from a different century.

"You should not be up here."

Caron spun, the miniature clutched to her chest. Idris stood in the attic doorway, a silhouette against the weak light from the stairs. He was a tall man, gaunt and angular, his hair a shock of white against the gloom. He had not made a sound on the stairs.

"I was looking for the velvet," Caron said, her voice sounding thin and foreign in the dusty air. "I found this."

She held out the portrait. Idris crossed the floor in two strides, his long fingers closing around the frame. He stared at the painted face, and Caron saw a muscle jump in his jaw. For a moment, his severe features contorted into something raw and unguarded, a flash of agony that was quickly smothered.

"Who is she?" Caron asked. "She looks exactly like me."

Idris closed his eyes, his thumb tracing the edge of the gold frame. "She was my sister," he said, his voice low and rough. "Elen."

"Your sister?" Caron frowned. "You never told me I had an aunt. You never speak of your family."

"There is little to speak of." Idris opened his eyes, and they were hard as the slate cliffs outside. "Elen died young. It is a matter of no consequence."

"She looks exactly like me, Uncle. How can that be a matter of no consequence?"

"A trick of blood," he said, turning away. "Resemblances echo through generations. You have the family face. It means nothing."

He pocketed the miniature and walked toward the stairs, pausing to look back at her. "Do not come up here again, Caron. And do not ask me about Elen. The dead are best left in peace."

The door closed behind him, and Caron was left in the dark, the scent of lavender and old paper lingering where he had stood.

The question festered in her mind like a splinter. Idris was a master of deflection, his silence a wall she had never been able to breach. But the miniature had cracked that wall, and through the fissure, Caron felt a draft of something unseen and unsettling. She thought of the village, of Aberffrew, nestled in the valley below the cliffs. She had always been kept away from it, an isolated girl in an isolated house. But there was one man in the village who had known Idris’s family, who had been a friend to Bryn Mawr in the years before Caron’s birth.

The vicar, Mr. Pritchard, was a stout, ruddy-faced man with a shock of white hair and a habit of humming hymns tunelessly under his breath. He had been the vicar of St. Cadoc’s for thirty years, and in that time, he had christened, married, and buried most of the parish. He had christened Caron, though she had no memory of it. Idris rarely attended services, but he allowed Mr. Pritchard to call occasionally, bearing gifts of jam and copies of the parish magazine.

It was on such a visit, a week after the discovery in the attic, that Caron found her opportunity. Idris had been called away to deal with a tenant farmer whose roof had collapsed, leaving Caron alone in the parlor with the vicar. He was seated by the fire, a cup of tea balanced on his knee, humming "Aberystwyth" in a monotonous drone.

"Mr. Pritchard," Caron said, settling into the chair opposite him. "Did you know my uncle’s family?"

The humming ceased. Mr. Pritchard looked at her over the rim of his teacup, his small blue eyes sharpening. "I did, child. A long time ago."

"Did you know his sister? Elen?"

The teacup clattered into its saucer. Mr. Pritchard set it down on the side table with trembling hands. "Where did you hear that name?"

"I found a portrait," Caron said. "In the attic. She looked exactly like me. Uncle Idris said she was his sister."

Mr. Pritchard was quiet for a long moment, staring into the fire. The wind moaned against the windows, shaking the leaded glass.

"He told you that, did he?" the vicar said, almost to himself. "He told you she was his sister."

"She was not?"

Mr. Pritchard turned to look at Caron, and she saw something in his face she had never seen before: a deep, weary pity.

"Elen was not Idris’s sister, child," he said softly. "She was his wife."

The words fell into the quiet room like stones into deep water. Caron stared at him, uncomprehending.

"His wife?"

"Aye. They were married here, in St. Cadoc’s, twenty-two years ago. I performed the ceremony myself. Elen was the most beautiful woman I ever saw, with her dark hair and her grey eyes. Eyes like yours." He paused, his gaze searching her face. "She died in childbirth, Caron. Twenty years ago, in this house. The child lived."

Caron’s hands gripped the arms of her chair. The fire hissed and spat, sending a shower of sparks up the chimney.

"The child lived," she repeated.

"Aye." Mr. Pritchard’s voice was barely a whisper. "A girl. Elen died, and the girl was born. Idris was destroyed by it. He shut up the house, turned away the world. He told the parish the child was his niece, the daughter of a sister who had died in England. He said he had adopted her to raise her away from the city. And because he was a man of means, and because people do not question the grief of a father, no one contradicted him."

Caron’s mind was reeling. The walls of the parlor seemed to close in, the peat smoke thick in her throat. She thought of Idris’s pale eyes, his careful distance, the way he never touched her except briefly on the shoulder.

"He is my father," she said.

"And Elen was your mother. You are not his niece, Caron. You are his daughter."

The wind screamed against the house, and the fire guttered. Caron rose from her chair and walked to the window. The sea churned below, white-capped and furious. She pressed her forehead against the cold glass.

"Why would he lie?" she asked. "Why would he tell me I was his niece?"

Mr. Pritchard sighed heavily. "Grief does strange things to a man’s mind, child. Idris could not bear to look at you. You are Elen’s image, come back to haunt him. Every time he sees your face, he sees the woman he loved and lost. To claim you as his daughter would be to bind himself to that grief forever. To make you his niece was to put a pane of glass between himself and the pain."

Caron closed her eyes. She saw the miniature in her mind, the emerald gown, the pearl drop, the grey eyes. She thought of Idris’s hand on the gold frame, his thumb tracing the edge. She thought of the way he said her name, always carefully, as if it were a word in a language he no longer spoke.

"He has kept me here," she said. "Like a ghost in a bottle."

"He has kept you safe," Mr. Pritchard said gently. "But perhaps not wisely."

The front door opened, and a blast of cold air swept through the hall. Idris’s footsteps sounded on the flagstones, heavy and deliberate. Caron turned from the window. She looked at Mr. Pritchard, and he looked at her, and between them passed a silence dense with understanding.

"Say nothing," she said.

The vicar nodded, picking up his teacup with a hand that shook.

Idris entered the parlor, his coat damp with sea mist. He looked at Caron, then at the vicar, and something flickered across his face.

"Mr. Pritchard," he said. "I trust Caron has been entertaining you."

"She has, Idris," the vicar said, his voice steady. "We have been having a very interesting conversation about the past."

Idris’s eyes rested on Caron, and for a moment, she saw it: the flinch, the quick, involuntary tightening of his features, as if he had looked at the sun. He turned away.

"Good," he said. "The past is best left where it lies."

Caron watched him cross to the fire, his shoulders stooped beneath the weight of a grief that had become a house, a prison, a life. She was the ghost of Elen, haunting him daily, and he had built these walls to contain her. But the portrait had shown her the truth, and the truth was a crack in the stone. It would widen, season by season, until the sea came in.

She sat down and folded her hands in her lap. Outside, the tide was rising.
