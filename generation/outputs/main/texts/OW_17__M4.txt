The house in Bayswater was one of those respectable, heavy-lidded dwellings that seem to have been built in a more earnest age and to have spent the intervening years in a profound and slightly disapproving slumber. Its windows were draped in a plush of so sombre a hue that the daylight, on entering, felt obliged to apologise for its intrusion; its corridors smelt faintly of pot-pourri and of the past; and upon its walls hung portraits of deceased Mortlakes who stared down at the living with the pained expression of persons who had been promised an eternity of rest and had been given instead a perpetuity of family gatherings.

It was in the drawing-room of this house, one evening in the November of a singularly damp and unprofitable year, that Mrs. Honoria Vane—a widow of independent means and dependent opinions, who had for some thirty years believed herself to be in delicate spiritual communication with the other world, and had never once received a reply that contradicted her—arranged her candles in a semicircle and prepared to summon the dead.

‘You must none of you be flippant,’ she said, addressing her two nephews, who had been invited, or rather commanded, to attend, and who were now seated upon a sofa of green velvet with the air of men who had come to a funeral and found the corpse inclined to be jocular. ‘The spirits are excessively sensitive. They detect mockery at a distance of a hundred miles. I knew a medium in Cheltenham who was struck quite dumb for a fortnight because a sceptic in the next room had smiled.’

‘I hope the sceptic was struck dumb as well,’ said the elder nephew, whose name was Rupert, and who was a young man of considerable elegance and no visible occupation. ‘It seems only fair that the affliction should be distributed.’

‘Rupert,’ said his aunt, with the severity of a woman who had never in her life been amused, ‘you are precisely the sort of person who would be turned into a pillar of salt and complain of the seasoning.’

The younger nephew, whose name was Cecil, and who was of a more nervous and conciliatory disposition, hastened to assure his aunt that they were both entirely in earnest, that the age was grossly material, that he for one had always felt a peculiar chill upon entering a churchyard, and that he had once seen a shape in the mirror at his club which had proved, upon investigation, to be the under-waiter, but which might very well have been something else. Having thus poured oil upon the troubled waters, he assisted his aunt in extinguishing the gas, an operation which plunged the room into a darkness so complete and so velvety that Rupert remarked it was like being buried alive without the expense of the funeral.

Mrs. Vane seated herself at the round table, her hands spread flat upon its surface, her eyes closed, her chin uplifted, and her countenance wearing that expression of strained and slightly dyspeptic solemnity which she reserved for her commerce with the infinite. The candles guttered. The rain beat a tattoo upon the window. Somewhere in the house a clock struck nine with the reluctant air of one who had been asked to do so against his principles.

‘Is there anybody there?’ inquired Mrs. Vane, in the hollow voice of a woman who has practised it.

The table, to the considerable surprise of everybody, including possibly the table, gave a single emphatic knock.

Cecil started. Rupert examined the ceiling with the interest of a man who suspects a trap-door.

‘Is there anybody there?’ repeated Mrs. Vane, her voice rising a semitone in excitement.

Two knocks, rapid and decisive.

‘It is the code,’ she whispered. ‘One for no, two for yes. Oh, be very quiet, both of you. There is a presence in the room. I feel it distinctly—a coldness, a sort of spiritual draught.’

‘I feel it too,’ said Rupert. ‘It is coming from the window, and I fancy it is what is commonly called the weather.’

‘Hush. It is a spirit.’ Mrs. Vane drew a long breath and addressed the darkness. ‘Are you a friend?’

Two knocks.

‘Are you—’ she hesitated, and a tremor of genuine feeling passed across her painted features—‘are you one of us? Are you, I mean, a member of the family?’

The table rocked. Then, slowly and with a dreadful deliberation, it spelled out, knock by knock, letter by letter, while Mrs. Vane called out each in a voice of mounting rapture: ‘M—O—R—T—L—A—K—E. Mortlake! It is your grandfather!’

Rupert sat up. ‘My grandfather,’ he observed, ‘was a man who made his fortune in tallow, and I never knew him to spell anything correctly in his life, including his own name, which he generally rendered as Mortlick. If he has acquired literacy in the next world, I can only say that death has been very good to him.’

‘He is here,’ cried Mrs. Vane, ignoring him, ‘and he desires to speak with us. Oh, this is the proudest moment of my life. Grandfather, dearest Grandfather, what would you have us do?’

The table, which had been trembling like a nervous horse, now broke into a series of knocks so rapid and so violent that the candles danced in their sockets.

‘He says—’ Mrs. Vane paused, and a look of bewilderment, not unmixed with disappointment, crossed her face. ‘He says he wishes—he wishes you to confess your sins. Both of you. Each in turn. He says he will not rest until—until the family is cleansed.’

There was a silence. The rain, which had been merely persistent, now became impertinent.

‘Confess our sins,’ said Rupert at last, in the tone of a man repeating a phrase in a foreign language of which he is not entirely master. ‘To a table. In Bayswater. In the dark. I have heard of stranger things, but not many, and most of them were in the newspapers.’

‘He is very insistent,’ said Mrs. Vane, whose ear was now pressed almost to the wood. ‘He says—oh, he says the most shocking things about your father’s will. He says—’

‘My dear aunt,’ said Rupert, rising and striking a match, which he applied to the nearest candle with the calm of a man who has decided that the joke has gone far enough, ‘I have no doubt that the spirit of my grandfather is a most industrious and inventive spirit, but I happen to know that my father’s will was drawn up by a solicitor in Lincoln’s Inn who was himself a sceptic, and that it is beyond reproach. I also happen to know—’ and here he looked about the room with a pleasant smile—‘that there are only three persons in this house who could possibly be producing these knocks, and one of them is my aunt, who is a woman of the strictest honesty, and the other two are my brother and myself, who are certainly not doing it, because I have been watching both of us.’

‘Then it is the spirit,’ said Mrs. Vane triumphantly.

‘Then it is the spirit,’ agreed Rupert, ‘and I can only say that the spirit has a very odd notion of amusement, and that if it wishes to hear confessions it had better apply to the Church, which has made a profession of it, rather than to a family which has always regarded its sins as private property.’

At this the table gave a knock of so peremptory and so indignant a character that the candles flickered, and Cecil, who had been silent and thoughtful, suddenly laughed.

‘I beg your pardon,’ he said, ‘but I have just remembered something. Aunt Honoria, when you arranged the candles, did you lock the door?’

‘The door? No. Why should I lock the door?’

‘Because,’ said Cecil, ‘I fancy the draught that Rupert noticed is not entirely meteorological. There is a very clever young person in this house—I refer to your new parlourmaid, the one with the red hair and the expression of a woman who has read too much and profited too little—and I observed her this afternoon in the corridor outside this very room, practising a series of knocks upon a tea-tray with an earnestness that I thought at the time was merely eccentric. She is, I believe, a niece of the medium in Cheltenham, and she has been engaged, if I am not mistaken, to give you a spiritual evening as a birthday present, having heard you say that you had never in your life received a message that was not entirely satisfactory.’

Mrs. Vane rose to her feet. Her face, in the candlelight, was a study in the collapse of the ideal. ‘Bella? Bella is a good girl. Bella would never—’

But even as she spoke, the door opened, and there appeared in the aperture a young woman with red hair, a tea-tray, and the unmistakable air of one who has been listening at keyholes and has heard herself discussed.

‘I am very sorry, ma’am,’ said Bella, with a curtsey that was somehow both respectful and defiant, ‘but I could not let it go on. You were getting that excited, and the gentleman was getting that sarcastic, and I thought to myself, if I do not put a stop to it there will be a scene, and I have seen quite enough scenes in Cheltenham to last me a lifetime. It was only meant to be a little fun, ma’am. I never thought you would take it so serious.’

Mrs. Vane looked at the tea-tray. She looked at her nephews. She looked at the candles, which were burning low and beginning to smell of tallow, a substance which had, in her family, a long and honourable history.

‘So there is no spirit,’ she said, in a small voice.

‘There is no spirit,’ said Rupert gently, ‘but there is a very excellent tea-tray, and I propose that we have some tea, and that we all confess our sins to one another in the ordinary way, over the cups, which is the only method I have ever found to be at once agreeable and efficacious.’

And so they did. And Mrs. Vane, who had spent thirty years in pursuit of the supernatural, discovered that evening that the natural, when it is kindly and well-buttered, is very nearly as wonderful, and a great deal more comfortable.
