It is a truth universally acknowledged by all who have wandered the cobbled quagmires of the Marais district in the year seventeen hundred and ninety-three, that the tumbrils rolling towards the Place de la Révolution rattled with a most terribly democratic equality. Whether the weary legs dangling over the wooden side belonged to a duchess burdened by her own diamonds, or a crossing-sweeper burdened by his mother’s rheumatism, the wooden wheels jolted over the stones with precisely the same pitiless, bone-shaking indifference. It was into this jostling, shouting, and altogether frightful current of human misery that the hackney-coach containing Monsieur le Comte de Brassard, his lady, his three pale daughters, and Miss Pinch, was unceremoniously upset.

Miss Honoria Pinch was a young person of English extraction, with a figure rather too angular and a nose rather too sharp, who had been hired in London by the Comtesse, on the express condition that she should instruct the Demoiselles Brassard in the English tongue, and, as the Comtesse elegantly phrased it, *se tenir comme il faut*. Miss Pinch had endeavoured to do both. She had stood very straight, and she had repeated the English verbs until she was as hoarse as a raven in a London fog. But the fates, assisted by a fiercely patriotic housemaid who had betrayed the family’s midnight flight to the National Guard, had decided that Miss Pinch should not instruct her pupils in the peaceful drawing-room of the Rue des Mimosas, but within the damp, gray, and heavily grated walls of the Prison de l’Espérance.

If the Comte de Brassard had considered the guillotine a terrible leveller of aristocratic distinctions, he was destined to discover that the prison-cell was an even greater one. The Comte was a gentleman of immense pride, a gentleman who had always considered a cravat not merely a piece of linen, but a moral obligation; yet, when he found himself thrust into a narrow, mildewed chamber with his family and his governess, he looked upon the bare stone walls as a personal affront. The Comtesse, who had never in her life so much as poured her own tea, sat upon a broken stool, wringing her laced hands, and declaring, with a tragic shudder, that the air was *insupportable*. The three Demoiselles, Adèle, Babette, and Céleste, clung to each other like a trio of shivering greyhounds, entirely useless, and looking very much as though they expected the stones to rise up and mend their stockings out of sheer respect for their ancient name.

They were, in short, a family of exquisite helplessness. And it was here, in this dreary establishment of the Republic, that Miss Honoria Pinch came into her extraordinary kingdom.

“I am persuaded,” said the Comte, glaring at a crust of black bread that lay upon the floor as if it were a venomous reptile, “that this is not fit for pigs. It is a libel on the bakers of France! How can they expect a man to—”

“If you please, Monsieur le Comte,” said Miss Pinch, stepping forward with a brisk, business-like air that sat rather oddly upon her angular frame, “if you will permit me, I will make a soup. It is what my aunt, Mrs. Grumple, used to make on a Saturday night in Camden Town, when the butcher’s boy had absconded with the week’s allowance. It requires only a pot, and water, and a fire, and a certain disregard for appearances.”

The Comte stared at her. The Comtesse stared at her. The Demoiselles stared at her. But hunger is a radical politician, and it was making rapid strides in the Brassard bosom. Miss Pinch, without waiting for a polite reply, gathered up the despised crusts, borrowed a dented tin pot from a sympathetic turnkey (who was himself mightily impressed by her resolute chin), and set about her culinary operations. She produced from the depths of her travelling-cloak a small, tightly-corked bottle of Worcestershire sauce—a relic of her homeland, which she had clutched through the whole disastrous journey—and with this secret weapon, she transformed the muddy water and black crusts into a steaming, savoury broth that smelled to the starving aristocrats like the very manna of Heaven.

From that hour, the revolution within the cell outstripped the revolution without. The Comtesse, who had formerly summoned Miss Pinch with the imperious snap of her fingers, now looked up with a wet, pleading eye whenever the governess approached the tin pot. The Comte, whose cravat had fallen into a state of limp and disgraceful dissolution, observed one morning that the air of the prison was excessively damp.

“It is the damp, Monsieur le Comte,” assented Miss Pinch, threading a needle with a piece of thread she had drawn from the hem of her own petticoat. “It plays the very mischief with the linen. If you would be so good as to sit still, sir, I will mend your stock.”

The Comte sat as still as a statue of marble, while Miss Pinch darned the torn fabric with a neatness and dispatch that would have made a laundress weep with envy. She mended the Demoiselles’ frayed frocks; she washed out their fine lace collars in a tin basin, hanging them to dry upon the bars of the window in a manner that gave the dismal cell the appearance of a festive bazaar. She darned, and she sewed, and she scoured, and she stirred, until the proud family, who would once have shuddered to share a street-corner with her, now gazed upon her needle as the wand of their salvation.

“You are very good, Miss Pinch,” said the Comtesse one evening, as Miss Pinch handed her a cup of hot water flavoured with a burnt crust. “Very good indeed. We are—we are indebted to you.”

“Not at all, Madame la Comtesse,” returned Miss Pinch, giving a sharp little tug to a knot she had just tied in the Comte’s shirt-sleeve. “It is what my uncle, the baker, used to say: ‘If the bread is hard, the teeth must be harder.’ And I may observe, sir,” she added, turning to the Comte, “that if you will but hold your arm a little higher, I can get this patch to lie flat, and you will look, when you go out, almost as respectable as you did when you went in.”

The Comte raised his arm, and sighed—a sigh that was half despair, and half a profound, unaccustomed gratitude. The Demoiselles, who had initially regarded the Englishwoman as a species of animated furniture, now followed her about the cell like a trio of devoted, if somewhat clumsy, spaniels. Adèle would fetch the water, Babette would watch the pot, and Céleste would hold the thread, all under the brisk, cheerful command of Miss Pinch.

And so it was, in that dismal year, that the tumbrils continued to roll, and the Republic continued its bloody work; but within the walls of the Prison de l’Espérance, the Comte de Brassard and his family learned a lesson which the guillotine itself could not have taught them more forcibly. They learned that genius is not confined to blood, nor usefulness to lace; and that a sharp nose, a brisk manner, and a bottle of Worcestershire sauce may, in the darkest hours of human history, prove a far more enduring bulwark against despair than all the pedigrees in Christendom.
