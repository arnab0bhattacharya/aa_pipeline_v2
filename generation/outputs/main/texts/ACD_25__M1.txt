The young man who stood upon the mat of my consulting-room was of a type that one sees a dozen times a day in the streets of London—the honest, somewhat stolid face of the working-man, broad shoulders in a blue serge coat, and hands which, though scrubbed to a fine redness, bore the callouses of the beat. Yet there was a certain anxiety in his grey eyes that spoke of more than the ordinary troubles of the constable’s lot.

“Doctor Watson, sir?” he asked, turning his helmet nervously in his hands. “They told me at the hospital you might be found here.”

“Come in,” said I. “You have the advantage of me.”

“Constable Higgs, sir. Number 47, S Division. I am come about my sister, sir. Martha Higgs.”

I motioned him to a chair. He sat upon the edge of it, as if fearing to soil the upholstery.

“She was lady’s maid to Lady Faversham,” he continued. “At The Grange, down in Surrey. She has been dismissed, sir. Dismissed without a character, and accused of the theft of a diamond necklace worth five thousand pounds.”

“A serious matter,” I remarked. “And the evidence?”

“There was none, sir. That is the trouble. The necklace was in the safe in her ladyship’s morning-room. My sister has the key, as is her duty. She put the jewel away on Tuesday evening, locked the safe, and went to bed. On Wednesday morning, when Lady Faversham went to fetch it for a dinner at the Hall, the safe was empty. Nothing else was touched. No sign of a forced lock. Naturally, the police were called, and naturally, since Martha had the key, the suspicion fell upon her.”

“And the local police?”

“Inspector Griggs is a man of the old school, sir. He looked at the key, looked at my sister, and said, ‘Where is it, Martha?’ She swears she never touched it, save to put it in the box. She is a good girl, sir. We are orphans, and she has kept herself respectable these ten years. But the inspector has charged her, and she is remanded at Guildford Gaol. I cannot believe she did it, but I am a policeman myself, and I see how black it looks. I have come to you, sir, because I have read of your friend Mr. Sherlock Holmes.”

At the mention of the name, my companion, who had been lounging in the window-seat examining a chemical reaction through a lens, turned his head sharply. Holmes had been so still that the constable had not noticed him.

“You have come to the right shop, Higgs,” said Holmes, stepping forward. “Sit still. Now, tell me exactly the hour your sister locked the safe.”

“Ten o’clock, sir. Precisely.”

“And the theft was discovered at nine in the morning?”

“Yes, sir.”

“And the safe? It is an old one?”

“Chubb’s, sir. About twenty years old, but in good order.”

Holmes steepled his fingers. “There is one point which strikes me as singular in this narrative. You say the necklace was worth five thousand pounds. Is it insured?”

The constable blinked. “Why, yes, sir. I believe so. Lady Faversham mentioned it to the magistrate—something about the premiums being very high.”

“High?” Holmes’s eyes gleamed. “Indeed. And when was the insurance effected?”

“I could not say, sir.”

“Find out, Higgs. Find out the exact date. It is a small point, but one which may prove of immense importance. Meanwhile, Watson, I fancy a run down to Surrey would do us good. The air is bracing, and the problem is not without its features.”

We took the 3:20 from Victoria. It was a chill, grey afternoon in late October, and the mist hung heavy over the Weald. As we rattled through the country, Holmes sat in silence, save for an occasional remark upon the agricultural methods of the neighbourhood, which seemed to me to have no possible bearing upon the case.

The Grange was a fine old Queen Anne house, standing back from the high road behind a screen of ancient elms. Lady Faversham received us in the morning-room—a handsome woman of fifty, with a hard mouth and restless eyes. She was clearly a person of immense self-possession.

“I have already told the police all I know, Mr. Holmes,” she said, tapping the arm of her chair with a lorgnette. “It is a painful business. Martha has been with me for three years. I trusted her implicitly. But there is no other explanation.”

“May I see the safe?” asked Holmes.

She rose and led us to a recess behind a heavy curtain. The safe was a massive iron affair, set into the wall. Holmes examined the lock with his lens, then turned his attention to the carpet, the window-sashes, and the fastenings of the door. He moved with the swift, silent precision of a bloodhound.

“You say the necklace was in this casket?” he asked, pointing to a velvet-lined box on the table.

“Yes. I took it out on Tuesday evening to wear at dinner, but decided against it. I locked it away myself, and gave the key to Martha to put on her ring. It is her duty to keep it.”

“And the insurance, Lady Faversham?”

She started slightly. “The insurance? Oh, yes. It is covered by the Northern Sun Office.”

“And the premium,” said Holmes softly. “It was increased, was it not, only a week ago?”

The colour drained from the lady’s face. “I—really, Mr. Holmes, that is a matter of private business.”
