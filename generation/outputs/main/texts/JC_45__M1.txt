The letter lay upon the table for three days before I touched it. It was a small, square envelope of the kind sold in the kiosks along the Quai du Mont-Blanc—cheap, yellowish, smelling faintly of glue and the damp of the lake—and yet it had travelled a vast distance to reach that small room with the cracked ceiling. It rested there, a foreign body in the stagnant atmosphere of my exile, emitting a kind of radiation that interfered with the silence. Outside, the Geneva rain fell with that steady, democratic persistence which made no distinction between the roofs of the Reformers and the hovels of the boatmen, washing the grey slate of the city into a uniform tone of melancholy.

I had recognized the handwriting on the face of the envelope immediately. It was the bold, upright script of my mother—a hand that had always seemed to attack the paper rather than write upon it, betraying a vigour of character that had outlasted her strength. It was a hand I had not seen for ten years, not since the winter I had fled across the border in the back of a cart, leaving behind the frost and the fear and the heavy silence of the house in the province of T—. And yet, as I stood by the window, watching the mist rise from the lake to obscure the distant, indifferent Alps, a creeping coldness took hold of me—a suspicion that had nothing to do with the temperature of the room.

In our circle, such arrivals were known as the 'White Envelopes'. They were a recognized phenomenon, a trap set with the bait of domestic affection. The police organs of the Tsar were not without a certain crude ingenuity; they understood that the strongest chains are forged not of iron, but of memory. A letter from a mother, from a wife, from a child left behind in the snow—it was a weapon more effective than the knout or the dungeon. We had all heard the stories. The comrade who received a letter of pardon, who returned to the embrace of his family only to find the embrace of the Third Section waiting in the hallway. The suicide of the exile in Zurich who could not bear the thought that he was the cause of his mother’s death, a death invented by a clerk in St. Petersburg to flush him out.

"Does the old woman live?" asked Radovsky, when I mentioned the letter to him at the café the next evening.

Radovsky was a man of the books, a theoretician who had fled the University of Kazan with nothing but a head full of Hegel and a digestion ruined by bad tea. He sat hunched over his glass of absinthe, his pale fingers stained with ink, looking at me with eyes that seemed to see through the flesh to the skeletal structure of the moral problem beneath.

"She writes that she is dying," I said. The words tasted of ash.

"Ah," Radovsky murmured, turning the green liquid in his glass. "And she begs you to accept the pardon? To return and be forgiven?"

"She says the Tsar is merciful."

Radovsky laughed—a short, dry sound that was more like a cough. "Merciful. Yes. The Tsar is merciful in the same way the sea is merciful to the drowning man. It accepts him. But tell me, my friend, is this the hand of your mother? Is the paper the paper of your house?"

"I cannot know," I admitted. "It is her hand. But it could be copied. It could be a forgery. It could be written under duress."

"It is a trick," Radovsky said, with the absolute certainty of the fanatic. "They wish to neutralize you. You are not dangerous here, drinking the lake water and dreaming of the past. But a martyr is dangerous. A man who returns and is hanged is dangerous. They want you to return so that you may die quietly in a prison cell, or better yet, so that you may betray your friends before you die. It is a mechanism of the state. Do not touch it. Burn it."

But I did not burn it. I took the letter back to my room, where the gas lamp hissed with a thin, hungry sound. I sat at the table and held the envelope up to the light. The paper was thin, almost translucent. I could see the shadow of the writing within, dark and dense. I thought of the house in Russia—the smell of the stove, the heavy carpets, the way the light fell through the frosted windows in the winter afternoons, turning the dust motes into golden sparks. I thought of her face, lined and severe, and the way she used to look at me when I was a boy, as if I were a problem to be solved.

To open it was to commit an act of faith—or an act of folly. If I opened it and it was a forgery, I would be a fool, a victim of a crude joke. If I opened it and it was true, if she was truly dying and truly believed in my salvation, then I was a monster for remaining here. The moral geometry of the situation was perfect and impossible; there was no angle from which I could view it that did not lead to ruin.

The silence of the room pressed in upon me. Outside, a boat sounded its horn on the lake, a mournful, vibrating note that echoed off the water and seemed to come from nowhere and everywhere at once. It was the sound of departure, of movement, of the world continuing its indifferent course while I sat frozen in the amber of my doubt.

I picked up the letter opener. The blade was cold. I slipped it under the flap of the envelope. The paper tore with a soft, intimate sound—a sound like a breath drawn in. I hesitated, my heart hammering against my ribs with a violence that surprised me. I was a man of action, a man who had thrown bombs and printed manifestos, yet I was paralyzed by a sheet of paper.

I drew out the single folded leaf.

It was her hand. The ink was brown, the strokes were bold. But as I read the words, the familiar shapes began to distort, to swim before my eyes. The sentences were simple, the grammar of a woman of the old school, but the sentiment was a cipher. *Come home. All is forgiven. The winter is long.* Was this the voice of a mother, or the voice of the Department of Police? Was it the cry of a dying woman, or the calculated lure of a hunter?

I read it again, and then a third time. The meaning did not clarify; it only grew more obscure, sinking deeper into the murky waters of implication. I realized then that it did not matter if she had written it. The letter had done its work. It had planted the seed of the past in the soil of the present. It had reminded me that I was not a free agent, a citizen of the world, but a creature of blood and obligation, tied by invisible threads to a land I hated and loved with equal ferocity.

I folded the letter and placed it back in the envelope. I did not burn it. I could not. I slipped it into the inner pocket of my coat, where it rested against my chest, a small, square weight of tragedy. I would carry it with me, this ambiguity, this burden of the unverified. I would walk the streets of Geneva, a ghost among the living, wondering if I was a son or a suspect, wondering if the silence of the lake was the silence of peace, or the silence of the grave. And in the distance, the horn sounded again, calling the boats to shore in the gathering dark.
