The crisis in the household of Lady Ashworth was not of the violent kind, nor did it involve the usual domestic tragedies of broken china or lost pearls, but it possessed a certain subtle, suffocating horror that was entirely modern. It was discovered, on a Tuesday morning, while the mist was still hanging over Kensington like a grey veil of bad taste, that the butler, Mr. Pym, had published a novel. It was a novel of such egregious vulgarity, such shocking disregard for the sanctity of the marriage bed, and such profound lack of moral instruction, that it had become the only topic of conversation in the clubs and the drawing-rooms from Mayfair to Belgravia. It was called *The Rose and the Razor*, and it was selling at the rate of three hundred copies a day.

Lady Ashworth, a woman of considerable height and very little imagination, stood in the breakfast room, holding a copy of the book in her gloved hands as if it were a dead frog. She looked at her husband, Lord Ashworth, who was attempting to eat his toast with the dignity of a man who has not yet realized that his social standing is currently being undermined by a servant who knows how to handle a typewriter.

"It is dreadful," Lady Ashworth said, her voice trembling with a mixture of outrage and aesthetic curiosity. "Look at the cover. That red. It is the colour of a bruise. And the title! *The Rose and the Razor*. One might as well call a book about the weather *The Thunder and the Lightning*. It is so obvious, and yet so terribly improper."

Lord Ashworth chewed his toast slowly. He was a large, comfortable man who believed that silence was the best answer to most questions, and that ignorance was a form of noble rest. "What do you intend to do about it, my dear?" he asked, wiping his mouth with a napkin that had been laundered by the very man in question.

"I intend to dismiss him," Lady Ashworth declared, setting the book down on the table with a thud that sent a shiver through the silver. "I will send for Pym. I will tell him that his conduct is incompatible with the position he holds in this house. He is a butler, not a novelist. A butler should be invisible. A novelist, however, is a very conspicuous thing, and I cannot have a conspicuous thing in my kitchen."

Lord Ashworth paused, a piece of toast halfway to his mouth. "And what is his name?" he asked, with the careful precision of a man stepping around a minefield.

Lady Ashworth blinked. "Who?"

"Mr. Pym. What is his Christian name? Or rather, what is the name he has chosen for the author of this... literary atrocity?"

Lady Ashworth frowned, tapping her fan against her hand. "I have no idea. Why should I know? I have never read it. I am too busy running the house to read the works of a man who polishes the silver. I found it in the hall when the postman left it. I only opened it to see who had sent it, and then I saw the name. I forgot it immediately. It is a common name. Something like... Arthur. Or perhaps Thomas. No, that is too simple. It was something more pretentious. Maybe Julian."

"Julian," Lord Ashworth repeated, testing the word. "Julian Pym. Does that ring a bell?"

"Not at all," said Lady Ashworth, though a faint flush of uncertainty crept up her neck. "Why do you ask? Do you know the man? Do you know his work? You are a man of letters, after all, or at least you are expected to be. You write those dreadful articles for the *Times* about the decline of agriculture."

Lord Ashworth smiled, a thin, tight smile that did not reach his eyes. "I write them, yes. But I do not read them. It is a great mercy. If I read my own work, I should have to die of shame. And I certainly have not read *The Rose and the Razor*. It is a scandalous book, my dear. I have heard of it. I am told it is full of lies and low passion. I am told it is a masterpiece of the grotesque. I am told that the author is a mystery man, a phantom of the literary world."

"Then let us dismiss him," Lady Ashworth said, gaining confidence. "We will tell him that we have heard rumours of his literary activities. We do not need to name the book. We do not need to admit that we have read it. We can simply say that we have received complaints. That is a very respectable way to end a relationship."

Lord Ashworth shook his head slowly. "It is not that simple. If we dismiss him now, before we have read the rest of the book, we shall be suspected of knowing. The servants are a gossipy lot. If we fire Pym, they will ask why. If we say it is because of the book, they will ask which book. If we admit we have read the first volume, we shall be expected to read the second. And there is a second, my dear. It is coming out next week. *The Razor and the Thorn*. I am told it is even more scandalous. I am told that in the first book, the heroine dies, but in the second, she is resurrected, which is a most absurd thing to do, unless one is a very poor ghostwriter."

Lady Ashworth sat down heavily in her chair. The room seemed to shrink around them. The clock on the mantelpiece ticked with a harsh, mechanical indifference, marking the seconds in which their lives were being rewritten by a man who likely did not know how to fold a napkin into a swan.

"So what do we do?" she whispered. "We cannot read it. It is too shocking. And we cannot dismiss him. It is too dangerous. We are trapped. We are like mice in a maze, and the maze is made of ink and paper."

"We must read it," Lord Ashworth said, with the weary inevitability of a man accepting a bill he cannot pay. "We must read it, my dear. Not because we care about literature. We do not. We must read it because we must know what we are dealing with. We must know the extent of the scandal. We must know if it is a one-off error or a pattern of behaviour. If it is a pattern, we may be able to use it against him. If it is a one-off, we may be able to hush it up. But we cannot decide until we have read it. It is the only logical course."

Lady Ashworth looked at the book on the table. The red cover seemed to pulse in the morning light, like a heart that had been stopped and then restarted. "But I am not a reader," she said. "I read the *Daily Telegraph* and that is enough. I do not understand metaphors. I do not care about character development. I care about the bills and the guests and the respectability of the family name."

"Then we shall read it together," Lord Ashworth said, standing up and buttoning his waistcoat. "It will be a shared burden. A joint venture. We shall read it in the library, where it is safe and soundproof. We shall read it quickly, so that we may forget it as soon as we are done. And then we shall decide. We shall decide whether to keep Pym, or to send him away with a generous pension and a letter of reference that says nothing of the truth. We shall decide whether to burn the book, or to keep it in a locked drawer, where it may rot in the dark, like a secret that has outlived its usefulness."

Lady Ashworth nodded, though her hands were shaking. "And if we do not like it?" she asked.

Lord Ashworth paused at the door. He turned back to look at his wife, and for a moment, his expression was not that of a husband, nor a lord, nor a man of letters, but simply a man who was tired. "Then we shall have to pretend that we do not know that we have read it," he said. "It is a difficult position, my dear. But it is the only position that is available to us. We are respectable people. We do not read scandalous novels. We do not know the names of the characters. We do not care about the plot. We are simply butlers of our own lives, serving up the morning, the afternoon, and the evening, with a smile on our faces, while inside, we are screaming. But we do not scream. We do not scream, because screaming is for the servants. And we are not servants. We are the masters. Or at least, we are trying to be."

He opened the door and stepped out into the hallway, where the dust motes danced in the beams of sunlight. Lady Ashworth remained seated, looking at the book. She picked it up again. The leather was smooth and cool under her fingers. She opened it to the first page. The words swam before her eyes, blurred and strange. She did not read them. She simply held the book, feeling its weight, its danger, its terrible, undeniable presence. She waited for Pym to come down from the kitchen, to bring the coffee, to bring the tea, to bring the news of the world, which was now, irrevocably, a world of ink and razor, of roses that bleed, and thorns that do not forgive. The clock ticked on. The mist outside thickened. The house held its breath. And the book, open on her lap, waited for the next page to be turned, by a hand that was afraid to touch it, yet dared not let it go.
