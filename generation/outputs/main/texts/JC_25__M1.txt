The heat of the evening lay upon the capital of Santa Rosa like a shroud of damp velvet, heavy and suffocating, muffling the cries of the vendors in the plaza and the distant, mournful hoot of the steamer in the river. It was a heat that seemed to emanate not merely from the tropical sun, which had long since surrendered its dominion to the shadows of the mountains, but from the very stones of the city itself, from the dense, humid foliage of the gardens, and from the anxious, perspiring bodies of the men who crowded into the Presidential Palace.

General Valerio had seized the reins of the Republic three weeks ago; a period of time which, in the feverish political climate of that coast, constituted a tenure of remarkable permanence, suggesting to the cynical observer a stability born not of love, but of a very efficient terror. He was a man of the people, or so the proclamations stated, a liberator who had marched down from the highlands with a ragged army of peons and a conviction of divine right that sat strangely upon his swarthy, heavy-browed countenance. Now, however, seated at the head of the long table in the Hall of Mirrors, he wore the white linen of a civilian and the sash of office with a solemnity that attempted to mask the predatory alertness of his small, black eyes.

The banquet was a masterpiece of diplomatic theatre. The General, advised by men who understood the value of foreign capital, had invited the merchants—the English, the Germans, the Americans, and even the taciturn French traders—to reassure them. It was a gesture intended to signify that business would continue, that the contracts would be honoured, and that the blood in the provinces was merely a necessary surgery, a cleansing of the body politic that would result in a healthier, more profitable organism.

Lieutenant Alvaro moved along the line of chairs, a decanter of heavy Spanish claret held firmly in his gloved hand. He was a young man, barely twenty-five, yet his face possessed that particular gravity often seen in soldiers who have aged prematurely under the sun of a civil war. His uniform was immaculate, the gold braid on his collar catching the flickering light of the chandeliers, but there was a stillness about him, a mechanical precision in his movements, that spoke of a mind withdrawn into a place where the clink of silver and the murmur of polite conversation could not reach.

He poured the wine for Mr. Henderson, the representative of the Pacific Steamship Company. Henderson, a stout man with a florid complexion and a mustache that bristled with British indignation, looked up with the patronizing air of a man who believes his presence is a gift to the savage world.

"Tell the General," Henderson said, his voice carrying the clipped, arrogant cadence of the city, "that we require assurance regarding the railway concession. There are rumours of... irregularities in the valley. Labour disputes. We cannot have disruptions."

Alvaro inclined his head slightly. "I shall convey the sentiment, Señor. The General values the continuity of commerce."

"Good," Henderson grunted, wiping his lips. "We don't care for politics, you understand. Only for the safety of the line. And the safety of our agents."

Alvaro moved on, his boots silent on the polished marble. He poured for the German, Herr Schmidt, a man of few words and many ledgers, and then for the American, Mr. Crowe, who smiled a thin, reptilian smile and whispered something about the price of mahogany. Alvaro listened to them all, his face a mask of military deference, his mind far away in the green, suffocating jungles of the interior, where the railway line vanished into the mist.

It had been only ten days since he had returned from the expedition to the province of San Esteban. The General had sent him there not as a fighter, but as an observer, an emissary to ensure that the local caciques understood the new order. Alvaro remembered the silence of the village of Santa Clara. It was a silence that had struck him before the smell did. The houses were thatched huts, low and sprawling, and they had been empty. The people had not fled; one does not flee leaving the cooking pots boiling and the hammocks still swinging.

He remembered the river, the Rio Dulce, running brown and sluggish past the village. He had stood on the bank and looked at the water, and he had seen the fish—large, white-bellied catfish—swarming near the surface, fighting over something that had snagged on the submerged roots of the mangroves. It was a scene of nature’s indifferent, voracious industry, utterly at odds with the political necessity that had necessitated the action.

"More wine, Lieutenant?"

The voice snapped him back to the Hall of Mirrors. It was the General himself speaking, a deep, gravelly sound that commanded the room. Valerio was looking at him, a benevolent smile touching his lips, the smile of a father to a son, though Alvaro knew the General had no sons, only enemies and instruments.

"Yes, Excellency," Alvaro replied.

He approached the head of the table. The General leaned back in his chair, his hand resting on the hilt of a ceremonial saber that lay upon the tablecloth. He looked out at the assembly of merchants with the air of a master of the house who knows that the wolves are at the door but believes he has bought them off with a good meal.

"These men," the General said, gesturing to the foreigners with a sweep of his hand that encompassed the glittering glasses and the sweating faces, "these men are the lifeblood of Santa Rosa. Without them, we are but a collection of beggars. With them, we are a nation."

A murmur of assent rose from the table. Henderson nodded vigorously. "Hear, hear. Stability is all we ask."

"And stability you shall have," the General declared, his voice rising, filling the room with a resonance that seemed to vibrate in the crystal. "There is peace in the land. The rebellion is crushed. The agitators are silenced. I have seen to it personally."

Alvaro poured the wine into the General’s glass. The liquid was dark, almost black, swirling with a viscosity that reminded him of the mud of the riverbank. He looked at the General’s face, at the heavy jaw and the eyes that held no light of conscience, only the cold fire of ambition. The General was lying, of course. Or rather, he was telling a truth that was a lie. There was peace, yes. The terrible, absolute peace of the grave. The peace of the village where the only movement was the catfish in the river and the vultures circling in the high, white sky.

Alvaro thought of the boy he had found in the underbrush, a small, brown thing with eyes wide open to the sun. The boy had not been dead long. Alvaro had stood over him for a long time, feeling the heat of the day pressing down on his shoulders, feeling the weight of the uniform he wore, the weight of the oath he had sworn. It was an oath to the Republic, but the Republic was an abstraction, a word in a book. The General was real. The blood was real.

"Is it not so, Lieutenant?" the General asked, turning his gaze sharply upon Alvaro. "You have seen the roads. You have seen the provinces. Tell them. Tell them that Santa Rosa is at peace."

The silence in the room was sudden and profound. The merchants stopped chewing. The servants froze. All eyes turned to the young lieutenant. He felt their gaze like a physical pressure. He felt the General’s will, a heavy, suffocating force, demanding complicity.

Alvaro straightened his back. He looked at Henderson, who was watching him with a mix of curiosity and boredom. He looked at the General, whose smile had hardened into a threat.

He thought of the silence of the village. He thought of the river.

"It is as the General says, Señores," Alvaro said. His voice was steady, toneless, stripped of all emotion. "The roads are open. The trains run on time. There is... quiet in the valleys. A great quiet."

The General beamed, satisfied with the confirmation. "You see? The Lieutenant is a man of truth. He has seen the new order."

The tension broke. The hum of conversation resumed, louder than before, as if to cover the moment of nakedness. The merchants returned to their roast pork and their discussions of tariffs and tonnage. The General resumed his pontificating.

Alvaro stepped back into the shadows of the alcove, the decanter heavy in his hand. He watched them eating and drinking, their faces flushed with wine and the assurance of their own safety. They were buying the Republic with their gold, and the General was selling it to them with the blood of the poor. It was a transaction as old as history, as cynical as the smile on the General’s face.

Alvaro looked at the wine left in the decanter. It was a rich vintage, imported, expensive. He thought of the brown water of the Rio Dulce, and the white bellies of the fish. He felt a coldness in his stomach, a sensation that had nothing to do with the temperature of the room. He was a part of this machine now, a cog in the mechanism of the lie. He had spoken the words. He had poured the wine.

He stood very still, watching the light play on the silver, listening to the sound of knives on plates, and he understood that he, too, was eating and drinking at the table of the General, and that the taste of it would never leave his mouth. Outside, the night closed in over the capital of Santa Rosa, silent and hot and full of unburied things.
