Mrs. Elspeth Gable believed in the fundamental order of things. A room was let for a week, then paid for at the week’s end. A lodger might receive a letter from a sister in the country or a bill from a tailor, and he might, if he were of a romantic disposition, light a candle in his window on a Friday evening to signal a sweetheart. But Mr. Alistair Finch, who had taken the second-floor front at 14 Gower Mews for a three-month term, paid for his entire stay in sovereigns on the day he arrived, received not a single piece of mail, and burned a single, steady candle in his window every night until the church clock struck two. It was this last habit that began to unsettle her.

At first, it was merely a peculiarity, the sort of quirk one learned to ignore in the trade. But the candle’s lonely vigil, a small, defiant eye of light in the slate-grey darkness of Bloomsbury, began to feel like an accusation. It was a signal, she was sure of it, but for whom, or of what? Mr. Finch himself was a study in quietude. He was a man of perhaps forty, with pale, watchful eyes and a way of moving through the house as if he were made of smoke, his footsteps on the stairs never quite solid. He took his meals in his room, read books he brought with him, and offered only the briefest of pleasantries. He was the perfect lodger, and for that very reason, he was the worst.

The unease grew like damp in the winter. Mrs. Gable found herself listening for the click of his door, the soft tread on the landing. She’d catch herself staring at the blank space on the hall table where letters were left, a space that remained stubbornly empty for Mr. Finch. The candle became the focal point of her anxieties. Each night, she would watch from her own parlour window across the narrow street as he drew the heavy velvet curtain aside just enough to place the brass candlestick on the sill. He never looked out. He simply lit the wick, and the small flame would bloom, a solitary star in the urban night. Then, precisely at two o’clock, it would be extinguished, and the window would become a dark, impenetrable square once more. It felt less like a habit and more like a duty, a grim and silent appointment he kept with the night itself.

On a Tuesday in late October, the silence broke. Mrs. Gable noticed that the milk jug and the small plate of bread and butter she left outside his door at seven each morning were still there at noon. Her heart gave a painful lurch. She knocked, first gently, then with more force. There was no answer. The door was not locked. The room was immaculate, the bed made with military precision. The candlestick stood on the sill, its wick a black, cold stub. But of Mr. Finch, there was no sign. His coat was gone from the hook, his hat from the shelf. Only his trunk, a heavy, brass-bound affair, remained in the centre of the floor, a silent, wooden witness.

Panic, cold and sharp, seized her. Her first instinct was to run for a constable, to report the strange, quiet man who had vanished like a ghost. But as she stood in the doorway, the thought of the police, with their heavy boots and their loud questions, their inevitable conclusion that she had been harbouring a criminal or, worse, a madman, gave her pause. It would be the ruin of her establishment. No, she thought, there was a better way. There was Mr. Silas Blackwood. He was a man of discretion, an investigator whose name was whispered in the drawing rooms of the discreetly distressed. He did not work for the police; he worked for the truth, and he charged handsomely for it. She sent her errand boy with a hastily scrawled note.

Mr. Blackwood arrived within the hour. He was a tall, thin man with a face like a greyhound’s, all sharp angles and intelligent, weary eyes. He listened to her story without interruption, his gaze resting on the room as if he were reading its history in the grain of the floorboards. He asked no questions until she had finished, her voice trembling slightly.

“A candle, you say, Mrs. Gable? Until two o’clock.” He walked to the window, peering through the glass at the street below. “And no letters. Not one.” He turned his attention to the trunk. It was locked, of course. “Did he receive any visitors?”

“None, sir. Not a soul. He was a shadow.”

Blackwood knelt by the trunk, examining the lock. “A shadow that paid three months’ rent in gold,” he murmured, more to himself than to her. He ran a delicate finger along the seam of the lid. From his pocket, he produced a small, leather-wrapped case of tools. With a series of precise, metallic clicks and scrapes that made Mrs. Gable’s teeth ache, the lock sprang open.

He lifted the heavy lid. The scent of cedar and camphor filled the air. Inside, neatly folded, were a few suits of clothes, a spare pair of boots, and a stack of books on astronomy and celestial navigation. It was all so ordinary, so disappointingly normal. Mrs. Gable felt a wave of anticlimax. But Blackwood was not finished. He tapped the bottom of the trunk. The sound was hollow. With the tip of a letter opener, he prised up a false bottom. Beneath it lay not jewels or gold, but a collection of strange items: a brass sextant, a set of meticulously drawn star charts, and a leather-bound logbook.

Blackwood opened the logbook. The pages were filled with a spidery, precise script. It was not a diary, but a series of observations. Dates, times, and notes on atmospheric conditions. ‘October 3rd. Clear. 2:00 a.m. Signal not received.’ ‘October 4th. Hazy. 2:00 a.m. Signal not received.’ The final entry was from the previous night. ‘October 22nd. Cloud cover. No possibility of sighting. All is lost.’

“He was a watcher,” Blackwood said softly, his eyes scanning the star charts. “A watcher waiting for a sign.”

“From who, sir? A sweetheart?”

“No, Mrs. Gable. From the stars.” He held up one of the charts. It depicted a section of the night sky, but one particular constellation was circled in red ink. “He wasn’t signalling anyone. He was making an observation. The candle wasn’t a message; it was a tool. A constant light source to help his eyes adjust in the dark, or perhaps to measure atmospheric distortion. He was waiting for a specific celestial event, something visible only at two in the morning. A comet, a meteor shower… something he believed would happen.”

Mrs. Gable stared at the strange instruments, the cryptic notes. The quiet, lonely man in her second-floor front had not been a fugitive or a spy. He had been an astronomer, a dreamer, a man lost in the vast, silent mathematics of the cosmos. His loneliness was not that of a criminal, but of a scholar, isolated by the sheer scale of his obsession. The final, despairing entry echoed in the room. ‘All is lost.’

“What does it mean?” she whispered.

“It means his vigil is over,” Blackwood said, closing the logbook. “Whatever he was waiting for did not come, or perhaps he saw something he did not expect. He left his trunk because the man who owned it no longer had a purpose for its contents.”

He closed the lid of the trunk, the sound echoing with a dreadful finality. Mrs. Gable looked from the trunk to the empty window where the candle had burned every night. The silence of the room was no longer quiet or peaceful; it was the profound, terrifying silence of an abandoned observatory, of a universe that had failed to answer a single, desperate question. The order of her world felt irrevocably broken, not by crime, but by a sorrow she could not begin to comprehend.
