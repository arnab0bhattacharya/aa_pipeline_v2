The letters came in on the Tuesday, with the milk and the coal-dust and the smell of wet pavement that was London, that was home; and she stood in the hall—Marianne, with the paper still damp at the folds—and thought, four years, how strange, how the light falls in the hall at half past eight exactly as it fell that morning in August when he went, when the cab drew up and the house seemed to hold its breath, as houses do, as if the very wallpaper with its little roses were listening. Four years, and the war is over. She read the words *demobilised* and *Southampton* and they were like stones dropped into water, and the rings spread out through the hall, through the kitchen where the kettle was just beginning to sing (that little song it sings, that shrill domestic song), and out into the street where the boy was still calling the news, and on and on, until the whole of London seemed to be made of ringing, of a bell that would not stop, though it was peace, though it was peace at last.

Peace. And Edward in his office in the City had read the same words an hour ago at his desk (the desk with the ink-stain shaped like France, he had often thought, like the very France he was supposed to be directing supplies to, directing the whole machinery of, as if one could direct a war from a desk, as if the war were a matter of ledgers and not of mud and larks and the boy's handwriting growing thinner each year), and he had laid the letter down and picked up his pen and laid it down again, and the clerk had come in with the figures for November and he had not seen them, had seen instead the garden at Bourton—no, at Whitby, at the house in Whitby where they had all been before the war, before the world had divided itself into before and after, and young Thomas had been fifteen and climbing the pear tree, and the light, the particular September light, had been falling through the leaves in that way it never falls again, never, no matter how many Septembers one lives through.

For the light changes. That is the thing about light. And in France the light had changed too, had become a thing of grey and white and the strange green of the flare that went up over no-man's-land and made the world look like the inside of a sea-shell, luminous and drowned, and Thomas, writing by it (the candle guttering in the tin, the paper propped on his knee, the whole dugout smelling of earth and wet wool and the particular exhaustion that was not sleep and not waking but something in between, some third country that one inhabited now and could not leave), Thomas had written: *Dear Mother, we are well. The larks still sing here, which seems odd. I think of the pear tree. Your loving son.* And he had folded the letter and thought, she will read this and think I am brave, and the thought was a kind of shame, because bravery was not a thing one was but a thing one did, moment by moment, like breathing, like putting one foot in front of the other in the mud that was not mud any more but a substance without a name.

And Catherine, in the hospital at Étaples, where the light came in white and absolute through the tall windows and made everything look as if it were under water or under glass—Catherine had written too, on the thin paper they gave them, her handwriting grown very neat and small with the years of it, the years of bandages and carbolic and the particular silence of men who had stopped speaking, who lay and looked at the ceiling with an attention that was almost religious, almost a kind of prayer, though she did not think they were praying, did not think they were thinking at all, only enduring, only being, as stones are, as the sea is, and she had written: *Dear Mother, I am well. It is nearly over. I shall come home.* And she had not written: *I do not know how to come home. I do not know how to be the person who left.* For that was the thing, the thing one could not say, the thing that sat in the chest like a stone, and one went on washing one's hands and folding the linen and the stone was there, and one went on, and the stone was there.

And Marianne in the hall, with the kettle singing and the light falling and the four years opening out before her like a corridor, like the long corridor of the hospital at Whitby where she had gone once to see about the boy's tonsils, before the war, before everything—Marianne thought: we shall all be together again. And the thought was a joy and a terror, for she had kept them all these years in the house of her mind, had kept Thomas at fifteen in the pear tree and Catherine at eighteen with her hair down and Edward at his desk with the ink-stain shaped like France, and now they were coming home, and they would be changed, and she would have to learn them again, learn their new silences and their new ways of looking at the ceiling, and the house would be full of them, and the house would be empty, for they would each be in their own country still, their own country of grey light and larks and the particular exhaustion, and she would move among them and not reach them, not touch them, though their hands were warm and their eyes were here.

For the war is over. And the light falls in the hall at half past eight exactly as it fell, and the kettle sings, and the boy calls the news, and the world is remade, and the world is the same, and they are strangers to one another, standing in the hall, in the light, in the peace, in the silence that is not peace but only the absence of shells, only the absence of the flare that made the world luminous and drowned, and the pear tree is in the garden at Whitby, and the larks are singing in France, and the letters are on the table, and the war is over, and the war is over, and the war is over.
