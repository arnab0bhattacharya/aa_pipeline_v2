The morning light in the capital of Santa Cruz de la Sierra came not as a beneficent visitation but as a slow, inexorable seeping of grey through the fog that clung to the tin roofs and the cobbled plaza, a light that seemed to owe more to the exhaustion of the night than to any promise of the sun. In the office of *El Clarín*, which was a narrow room on the second floor of a building whose plaster had long since given up its pretence of whiteness, Señor Anselmo Vargas sat at his desk and regarded the proof-sheets of the previous day’s edition with the fixed and unseeing gaze of a man who has been staring too long into a fire. The article, which had run beneath a headline of considerable typographical enthusiasm, was still there, black upon the greyish paper, a thing of ink and paper that had seemed, when he wrote it, to be merely the expression of a noble sentiment, the inevitable flowering of a just indignation. Now it sat upon his desk like an indictment.

He had written it, he remembered, in a fever of that peculiar elation which comes to men who believe they are witnessing the dawn of a new era. The revolt of the garrison at Puerto del Rey—a squalid affair of mules and machetes, of half-drunk soldiers and ambitious sergeants—had appeared to him, through the distorting lens of his own hopes, as the first tremor of a great upheaval. He had praised the rebels in language that now seemed to him extravagant, even dangerous. He had called the President—that old man with the face of a weary lizard—a tyrant and a fool. He had invoked the spirit of liberty, the dignity of the people, the inevitable triumph of the right. He had written, he now recalled with a cold sinking of the heart, that the soldiers of the republic would never fire upon their brothers.

The news of the failure had come late the previous evening, brought by a frightened clerk who had heard it in a café where the officers of the government were drinking to their own success. The revolt had been crushed at Puerto del Rey with a brutality that was said to be exemplary. The leaders—those same sergeants whose names Vargas had printed with such careless admiration—had been shot against the wall of the custom house before the sun had set. And now the soldiers were coming to the capital. They were coming, it was said, to restore order, to arrest the traitors, to teach the press a lesson it would not forget.

Vargas rose from his desk and went to the window. The fog was lifting, and he could see the cathedral square, the huddled Indians with their burdens, the idle soldiers lounging before the barracks, the closed shutters of the shops. It was a scene of such profound and ordinary tranquillity that it seemed to mock the agitation of his soul. The world, he thought, with a bitterness that was already familiar to him, goes on. The sun rises, the mules are driven to market, the priests mumble their masses, and a man may sit in a room and write words that will bring the soldiers to his door, and the world will not so much as turn its head.

He thought of flight. The frontier was not far—three days on horseback, perhaps, if one knew the paths and had the money to pay for silence. There was a cousin of his wife’s who kept a store in the mountains, a man of no particular convictions who might be persuaded to shelter him. But the roads, he knew, would be watched. The telegraph had already carried the news to every garrison in the province. And what would he be, in the mountains, but a man without a country, a fugitive from a justice that was no justice, a creature of the shadows? He had seen such men—the broken remnants of failed revolutions—drifting through the border towns, their eyes hollow with the memory of what they had been, their voices muted by the fear of what they had become. He did not think he had the stomach for it.

He thought of recantation. He could write another article, a retraction, a confession of error. He could declare that he had been misled, that the reports from Puerto del Rey had been false, that he had never intended to counsel disloyalty. He could grovel, in print, before the President and the army and the respectable citizens who had always suspected him of dangerous opinions. The thought brought a flush of shame to his cheeks that was almost physical. He had written, in his youth, a pamphlet on the dignity of the writer’s calling, a thing of windy rhetoric that he now remembered with a kind of embarrassed tenderness. To recant would be to deny everything he had ever believed, to spit upon the grave of his own youth. And yet—he was not a young man anymore. He had a wife, a daughter, a house with a mortgage, a position that was modest but not contemptible. Was it not the duty of a man to preserve himself for better days? Was there not a kind of courage in endurance, in silence, in the patient waiting for the tide to turn?

He thought of waiting. He thought of sitting in this room, at this desk, with the proof-sheets before him, until the soldiers came. He imagined the knock at the door, the sound of boots on the stairs, the faces of the officers—young men, probably, with the blank, incurious faces of those who have been taught to obey. He imagined the cell, the damp walls, the interrogations, the long months or years of oblivion. He imagined his wife’s face when she heard the news. He imagined the silence that would fall upon the office of *El Clarín*, the silence of a press that had been silenced.

And yet, even as he imagined these things, he felt a strange, perverse exaltation stirring in him, a sense that he was standing at last upon the firm ground of reality. All his life, he thought, he had been a man of words, a dealer in phrases, a weaver of sentences that had no more substance than the smoke of a cigarette. He had written about liberty and justice and the dignity of man, and he had believed, in a vague and comfortable way, that these were things that existed somewhere, in books, in speeches, in the abstract. But now he was being asked to pay for them. He was being asked to choose between the comfortable lie and the uncomfortable truth. And he found, to his own surprise, that the choice was not as difficult as he had feared.

He sat down again at his desk. He took up his pen. The fog had lifted entirely now, and the sun was shining through the window, a hard, clear, merciless light that fell upon the paper and made the ink glisten. He wrote, in a hand that was steadier than he had expected, a short article—a very short article, no more than a paragraph—in which he stated that the news from Puerto del Rey was confirmed, that the revolt had been crushed, and that the editor of *El Clarín* wished to place on record his continued and unshaken belief in the principles he had always advocated. He did not recant. He did not flee. He simply waited.

When the soldiers came, an hour before noon, they found him at his desk, the proof-sheets still before him, the pen still in his hand. He looked up at them with a calm that was not entirely feigned. The officer in command, a young man with a thin moustache and the eyes of a tired greyhound, seemed embarrassed by the stillness of the room, by the absence of any drama, by the fact that the traitor was not, after all, a monster, but only a middle-aged man in a shabby coat who smelled faintly of ink and tobacco.

“Señor Vargas?” the officer asked.

“At your service,” Vargas replied.

And he rose, and put on his hat, and went down the stairs with them into the sunlight, where the mules were still being driven to market, and the priests were still mumbling their masses, and the world, indifferent and eternal, went on.
