The air in the harbour at Port Alverton tasted of salt and rust, the same as it always had. But beneath that, there was a newer taste, the dry, metallic tang of decay from the lands beyond the green belt. Elias stood on the stone quay, his hands shoved deep into the pockets of his worn trousers, and watched the *Sea Serpent* rock gently against the pier. She was no serpent. She was a sturdy, thirty-foot fishing vessel, her hull scarred with the memories of a thousand tides, her single mast thick and sound. She was their hope, a splinter of wood and rope pointed at an ocean of ghosts.

A hand slipped into his. Elara’s. Her palm was calloused from the forge, but her grip was warm, a small, defiant spark of life against the grey morning. He looked at her, at the wind whipping strands of dark hair across her face, at the fierce, quiet determination in her eyes that always managed to steady him. They had been chosen. Or, more accurately, they had volunteered. In a settlement of fewer than a hundred souls, volunteering was a heavy, sacred thing. It meant stepping away from the fragile safety of the last known community on the island of Britain to chase a rumour. A rumour of light on the far shore, a flicker seen by a scavenger with a telescope from the highest point in Cornwall, a year ago. A rumour of other people.

Aboard the boat, Silas was coiling a rope with the unhurried grace of a man who had spent seventy years at sea. His face was a roadmap of wrinkles, his skin tanned and cracked like old leather. He was the only one who truly remembered the sea before the Quiet, before the global systems failed and the world fell silent. He’d been a deep-sea trawlerman then. Now, he was their navigator, their teacher, their link to a past where crossing the Atlantic was a matter of commerce, not of desperate faith. He didn’t say much, but his presence was an anchor for them all.

“Almost ready,” Silas grunted, not looking up from his work. His voice was a low rumble, like stones shifting underwater.

“We have everything?” Elias asked, his voice tight. He was thinking of the sealed crates in the hold: salted fish from their nets, dried vegetables from their terraced gardens, purified water, seeds for a harvest they might never see. The weight of it all felt immense.

“Everything we can carry, lad,” Silas said. “And the strength in our arms. That’s more important.”

A small, scuffling sound made them all turn. Finn stood at the edge of the quay, his knapsack clutched in both hands. He was ten years old, with a thatch of unruly brown hair and eyes that held too much ancient knowledge for his face. He had been born after the Quiet. The world of engines and aeroplanes and constant noise was as much a myth to him as the old gods. He had attached himself to Elias and Elara like a burr, following them everywhere, his silent presence a constant reminder of what they were fighting for.

“Finn,” Elara said, her voice gentle but firm. “We talked about this. You have to stay with Martha and the others. It’s safer.”

The boy shook his head, a single, stubborn jerk. He didn’t speak. He just walked forward, placed his knapsack on the deck of the *Sea Serpent*, and then clambered aboard with a surprising agility. He sat down on a coil of rope near the stern and looked at them, his expression unreadable. He had made his choice. To leave him behind would be to create a wound in their small community, a tear in the fabric they were trying so desperately to hold together. To take him was to risk a child on a voyage into the unknown.

Elias looked at Elara, and in her eyes, he saw his own conflict reflected. They were the future, the young couple who were supposed to build a new world. But what future was there without children? What were they building, if not for Finn?

“Let him be,” Silas said quietly, finally looking up. “The sea has a way of deciding who belongs on a boat. And that one looks like he belongs.”

The decision was made in that moment, a quiet, unspoken consensus. Elara squeezed Elias’s hand once more before letting go to help Silas with the final checks. The people of Port Alverton gathered on the quay, a small, solemn crowd. There were no grand speeches. Martha, the settlement’s leader, a woman with a face like carved oak, simply nodded at them. Her eyes were grim, but held a sliver of something that might have been hope.

With a final push, they were off. The engine coughed to life, a sound that felt alien and powerful in the quiet harbour. The stone walls of Port Alverton, their home, their entire world, began to shrink. The faces on the quay blurred into indistinct shapes, and then they were past the harbour’s mouth, and the open sea stretched before them, vast and terrifyingly empty.

For the first few days, there was a tense, focused energy. Silas taught Elias how to read the old charts and the new, hand-drawn ones, how to feel the currents and trust the wind. Elara checked and re-checked their supplies, her movements precise and efficient. Finn sat for hours on the bow, his small body silhouetted against the immense sky, watching the water as if he could see secrets in its depths. He never complained, never asked for anything. He simply existed, a quiet, constant presence.

The ocean was a character in their drama, its moods shifting without warning. Some days, it was a sheet of polished blue-grey glass, and the silence was so profound it felt like a pressure against their eardrums. Other days, the wind would rise, and the *Sea Serpent* would climb great, slate-grey waves before plunging down into the troughs, the world reduced to a chaos of spray and wind and the groaning of the hull.

It was on one of these rough days, when the sky was the colour of a bruise, that the storm hit. It came out of the west like a living thing, a wall of black cloud and shrieking wind. Silas lashed the tiller, and all three of them worked to secure the sail, their hands raw from the coarse rope. Finn, small and wiry, wedged himself into a corner of the cabin, his eyes wide but not with fear. He was watching Silas, learning.

A wave, bigger than the rest, crashed over the deck. It swept Elias off his feet, slamming him against the mast. For a moment, the world was a roar of freezing water and blackness. Then he was gasping, his lungs burning, clinging to the mast as the water receded. Elara was there, her hand gripping his arm, her face pale but resolute.

“Hold on!” she screamed over the wind.

They held on. They fought for hours, a desperate, primal battle against the fury of the sea. When the storm finally broke, they were left exhausted and shivering in a world washed clean and eerily quiet. The sun broke through the clouds, and the ocean was calm again, as if nothing had happened. But something had. They had faced the worst, and they were still here. The *Sea Serpent* was battered, but she was whole. So were they.

Weeks turned into a month. The world was just water and sky. The novelty had worn off, replaced by a deep, grinding monotony. They ate their simple rations, took turns at the helm, and watched the endless, empty horizon. Doubt began to creep in, a cold sea-mist in their minds. What if the light was just a trick of the atmosphere? What if there was no one? What if they had left their home, their people, for nothing?

It was Finn who saw it first. He was in his usual spot on the bow, and he suddenly stood up, pointing a small, thin finger towards the east.

“Smoke,” he said. It was the first word he had spoken in weeks.

They rushed forward. There, on the horizon, was a faint, dark smudge against the pale blue sky. It wasn’t a cloud. It was too thin, too straight. It was smoke. A plume of smoke rising from land.

A wave of emotion crashed over Elias, stronger than any storm. It was relief, terror, and a wild, soaring hope. Elara let out a sound that was half-sob, half-laugh. Even Silas, the old sailor, was silent, his eyes fixed on the distant smudge, a single tear tracing a path through the salt on his cheek.

They were not alone. The world had not ended. As they altered their course, the wind filling their sail with new purpose, the smoke grew thicker, more defined. They were sailing towards it, towards the unknown people who had made it, towards a future that was suddenly, miraculously, no longer empty. The long, silent crossing was over. The real journey was just beginning.
