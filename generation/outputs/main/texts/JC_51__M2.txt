The darkness was not a thing that came upon Captain Elowen; it was a substance, a thick, impervious medium in which he existed, as one exists in the womb or in the grave. It had settled over his eyes weeks ago, a creeping fog that began at the periphery of the world and moved inward with the slow, inexorable patience of a tide. He had told no one. To admit it would be to surrender the helm, to hand over the soul of the *Phantom* to the men who watched him with the wary, calculating eyes of those who have learned that a captain’s blindness is a debt to be paid in blood and coin. So he stood on the bridge, his hand resting lightly on the wheel, feeling the vibration of the engines through the soles of his boots, trusting entirely to the voice of his mate, Silas Vane, who stood behind him like a shadow cast by a candle in a windless room.

Vane was a man of precise movements and sharp intentions, his ambition a cold, clear fire that burned in the depths of his chest. He read the compass, the lead line, the depth sounder. He spoke the figures in a low, monotonous drone, devoid of inflection, as if reciting a liturgy for the dead. “Two cables astern, sir. Current setting south-east. The starboard engine is holding steady.”

Elowen nodded, though the motion was a gesture of habit, a reflex of the body that no longer needed the eyes. He had to be careful. A hesitation, a twitch of the brow, a wrong turn of the head, would be enough. The owners in Penang, the Messrs. Harkness and Thorne, had already sent word that they were displeased with the delays. They spoke of efficiency, of schedules, of the cold arithmetic of profit, and they suspected that Elowen’s age had begun to tax his faculties. They did not know that his faculties were intact, that his memory of the treacherous reefs of the Gulf was as sharp as a blade, but they suspected decay, and suspicion, once planted, grows like a weed in the dark soil of the mind.

The sea outside the portholes was a black mirror, unbroken by wind, reflecting only the faint, ghostly glow of the moon which hung low and heavy in the sky. The air was thick with the scent of salt and rot, the smell of the mangroves that lined the distant shore, a place of silent, watching things. Elowen felt the ship’s list, a slight tilt to port, and he corrected the wheel with a gentle touch, his fingers finding the rim of the brass by the memory of its shape. He was a ghost in his own vessel, a man commanding a ship that no longer saw, guided by a man who saw too much.

Vane’s voice came again, closer now, almost touching his ear. “The bearing on the lighthouse at Krapok is bearing down, sir. We are too close to the bank.”

Elowen’s hand tightened on the wheel. He knew that bank. He knew the shape of the water there, the way it held back the current, the way the mud sucked at the hull. He had navigated it a hundred times, by day and by night, in rain and in sun. But now he was blind to the red light of the tower, blind to the white face of the moon on the water. He had to trust Vane. And he knew, with a cold certainty that settled in his stomach like a stone, that Vane was not merely reading the instruments. Vane was reading him.

“Hold course,” Elowen said. His voice was steady, but there was a dryness in it, a rasp that betrayed the effort of maintaining the illusion.

Vane did not move. The silence that followed was heavy, charged with the unspoken. The engine’s thrumming filled the void, a rhythmic pounding that seemed to beat in time with Elowen’s own heart. He could feel Vane’s gaze on the back of his head, a physical pressure, a weight. The mate was testing him. He was waiting for the slip, the error, the moment when the captain’s hand faltered and the ship plunged into the shallows. It was a trap, laid with the precision of a fisherman setting a net.

Elowen closed his eyes, though it made no difference. He turned his head slightly, as if listening to the wind, though there was no wind. He let the silence stretch, letting the tension build until it was almost unbearable. He could feel the sweat beading on his forehead, cold and clammy. He thought of the letter from Harkness and Thorne, the one that had arrived with the last supply ship, its words sharp as shrapnel. *We require your immediate report on the condition of the crew and the vessel. Any signs of incompetence will not be tolerated.*

Incompetence. A kind word for blindness. A polite term for the end of a life.

“Sir?” Vane’s voice was soft, almost sympathetic, which was worse than anger. “The depth is changing. We are in two fathoms.”

Two fathoms. That was too shallow. The *Phantom* drew twelve feet. They were on the mud.

Elowen’s hand moved. He spun the wheel hard to starboard, a violent, decisive motion that cut through the air. The ship groaned, the wood creaking under the strain, the engine roaring as the propeller bit into the water. He was blind, but he knew the feeling of the helm, the resistance of the water, the angle of the turn. He was navigating by the feel of the ship, by the memory in his bones, by the instinct that had kept him alive for forty years at sea.

Vane gasped. For a moment, the mate’s composure cracked. Elowen could hear the shift in his breathing, the sudden intake of air. The ship turned, the hull scraping against the bottom, a sound like a scream, a long, terrible shriek of metal and wood. Then, with a shudder, they were clear. The depth sounded deepened. The current caught them, pulling them back toward the open water.

Elowen let out a breath he didn’t know he had been holding. His hand trembled on the wheel, a slight, involuntary vibration that he quickly suppressed. He turned back to his forward stance, facing the dark glass of the bridge, facing nothing.

“Steady,” Vane said, his voice flat again, but the coldness in it had changed. It was no longer the coldness of calculation, but of fear. Or perhaps it was something else. Respect? No, not respect. Fear of the unknown. He had expected a man who was gone, a relic, a thing to be managed. He had found a man who was still there, a predator in the dark, waiting.

Elowen did not speak. He stood in the silence, the wheel cold under his hand, the sea vast and indifferent beyond the glass. He had won the moment, but he knew it would not last. The owners would hear of the incident. They would investigate. And when they did, they would find the truth. They would find that the captain who commanded the *Phantom* was a man who saw nothing, yet saw everything. And in that sight, in the terrible clarity of the dark, there was a power that Vane, with all his ambition and his sharp eyes, could never understand. The sea does not care for the eyes that watch it. It cares only for the hands that hold it. And Elowen’s hands were still true.
