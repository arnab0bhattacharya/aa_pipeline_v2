The sky over Blackstone Edge had gone the colour of a bruised plum, and from the mill yard they could see the first of them falling, thin scratches of light that seemed too slow, too deliberate, to be anything but ill-omened. Josiah Crane stood at the gate with his hands clasped behind his back and watched the streaks come down beyond the reservoir, and when the first dull thud carried across the valley he did not flinch, though the clerks behind him did.

By nightfall the whole of the moor above Hartsmoor was burning.

It was not the tame, sulking fire of a mill chimney. It came in waves, gouts of flame where the burning stones had struck the heather, and the wind off the tops drove it down toward the town in long orange fingers. The firebell at the works rang out over the terraces, and men came out of their houses with buckets and shovels, and the constable on his bicycle went from door to door saying the mill was wanted, the mill was wanted, all hands to the moor.

Josiah Crane went up to the counting house and stood at the window and watched them gather in the yard. He knew every one of them by name and by debt. There was Ackerley from the carding room, and the two Byrom lads, and old Sowerby who had been at the mill since before Josiah's father died. And there, at the front, with his coat off already and his sleeves rolled past the elbow, was Daniel Thwaite.

Josiah's mouth tightened. Daniel Thwaite had been the ringleader of the strike in the spring, the one who had stood on a crate in the market square and talked about a fair wage and a union card until half the town was out, and Josiah had broken the strike the way such things were broken, and then he had made sure that Daniel Thwaite's name was on a list that went to every mill owner within twenty miles. Blacklisted. No work in the valley, no work in the next valley. The boy should have been in Leeds by now, or Manchester, or starved. Instead here he was, in Josiah Crane's yard, rolling his sleeves up like he owned the place.

The constable came puffing up the steps. "They want a leader, Mr. Crane. They'll not go up in a rabble, they say. They want someone to take charge."

"Then I shall take charge," said Josiah.

He came down into the yard with his hat on and his stick in his hand, and the men fell quiet in the way they always did, and he felt the old certainty settle on him like a coat. But before he could speak, Daniel Thwaite had climbed onto the water butt and turned to face them all.

"Listen," he said, and his voice carried in the way that voices do when a man has nothing left to lose. "The fire's coming down the beck side. If it reaches the gorse above the reservoir it'll take the whole hillside and then it'll be in the town by morning. We go up in three parties. Ackerley, you take the men from the carding room and get up the old quarry road, and you work the flank from the north. Byrom, both of you, take the spade men and cut a break along the wall above the reservoir. The rest of you come with me up the middle and beat it out where it's lowest."

"And who made you captain?" said Josiah.

The yard went silent. Daniel looked down at him from the water butt, and there was no hatred in his face, only a kind of tired patience that Josiah found more insulting than anger.

"Nobody, Mr. Crane," he said. "But the fire doesn't care who's captain. If you've a better plan, say it and I'll follow it."

And that was the thing. Josiah had no better plan. He had never fought a fire on a moor in his life. He had fought strikes, and tariffs, and the price of raw cotton, and he had won at all of them, but he had never stood on a dark hillside with the heather cracking and popping under his boots and the smoke coming down so thick he could not see his own hands.

They went up. Daniel led, and the men followed him without a word, and Josiah found himself in the middle of the column with a wet sack in his hands and his good boots filling with peat water. The fire was a living thing up there, a red beast that ran along the ground and leapt the becks and threw sparks a hundred feet into the air. The heat came at them in gusts. Men beat at the flames with sacks and shovels and their own coats, and the smell of burning heather was like burning sugar, sweet and sickening, and the smoke made them cough until their ribs ached.

Josiah saw Daniel go into a wall of flame after a lad who had stumbled, and come out again with his eyebrows gone and the boy under his arm, and he saw him stand in the smoke and shout orders that men obeyed without question. He saw the break being cut above the reservoir, and the fire checked at the wall, and the long slow fight along the beck side until the sky in the east began to grey.

It was near dawn when it was done. The moor was a black waste with red eyes still glowing in it, and the men were lying on the wet grass like dead things, and Daniel Thwaite stood among them with his hands black and his shirt in ribbons and his face streaked where the sweat had run through the ash.

Josiah came and stood beside him. Below them the town was beginning to wake, the first chimneys smoking, the first carts in the streets, all of it saved and none of it knowing it.

"You'll want paying," Josiah said.

"I'll want my name off your list," said Daniel.

Josiah looked out over the black moor. "Aye," he said at last. "Well. I daresay there's a foreman's place in the carding room, if you've a mind to it. It wants a man who can hold men together."

Daniel turned and looked at him a long moment, and Josiah could not read what was in his face, whether it was triumph or contempt or something simpler.

"I'll think on it," Daniel said. "After I've had some sleep."

And he walked off down the hill toward the town, and the men got up one by one and followed him, and Josiah Crane stood alone on the burnt hill with his stick in his hand and watched them go.
