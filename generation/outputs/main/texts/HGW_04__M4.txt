The news came upon Edith Barrow in the small front parlour of Number 7, Lorne Villas, on a Tuesday afternoon in early October, and it came in the flat, conclusive tone her mother had used for years to close the discussion of any inconvenient topic. Edith had her matriculation results in her hand—distinction in mathematics, credit in English, a triumph that had made the headmistress at the Croydon High School for Girls write the word “exceptional” in her letter—and she had allowed herself, during the walk home along the noisy length of the London Road, to imagine the future as a series of brightly lit rooms opening one upon another: the scholarship examination at the college, the lecture theatres, the laboratories, the eventual degree. She had even, in the privacy of her own head, permitted herself the word “chemist,” which in those days still had about it a faint and thrilling whiff of the improper, as though one proposed to spend one’s life among retorts and Bunsen burners.

Her mother listened to the reading of the results with her hands folded upon her lap and her lips pressed into a line of patient endurance, and when Edith had finished she said, “That is very nice, dear, very nice indeed,” in a voice that suggested a funeral conducted with commendable economy, and then she said that they must have a little talk.

The little talk occurred at dinner, which was a cold affair of mutton and pickled cabbage, and it was conducted in the presence of Edith’s brother Harold, who was twenty-one and reading for the bar, and who ate his mutton with the placid and slightly bored air of a young man who has heard his sister’s education discussed before and knows precisely how the discussion will end. Their mother, Mrs. Barrow, was a widow of some twelve years’ standing, a small, neat, energetic woman with a high colour and a habit of pronouncing her decisions as though they were acts of God, and she began by observing that Edith was now nineteen and that a girl of nineteen who had done well at school had done quite enough, and that the family purse, which had been stretched to its utmost to keep Harold at his studies, could stretch no further.

“You must see, Edith,” she said, helping herself to more cabbage, “that your brother’s career is the important thing. A man must have his profession. There is no question of that. And I have been speaking to Mrs. Fennick, and she says that the drapery establishment in North End is wanting a cashier—a young lady of good appearance and education—and she thinks you would do very well there. The wages are not large, but they are something, and you would be at home in the evenings.”

Edith sat quite still, and it is a curious fact, and one worth noting, that her first sensation was not one of anger or even of disappointment, but of a kind of scientific astonishment, as though she had been observing an experiment in natural philosophy and had seen the apparatus behave in a manner wholly contrary to the laws of nature. She had passed her examination with distinction. She had beaten, by a comfortable margin, the girls from the grammar schools at Croydon and Streatham and Wimbledon. She had, in short, demonstrated a capacity for the acquisition of knowledge of a sort that is not common, and here was her mother proposing to set her behind a cash desk in a draper’s shop to hand change to ladies buying stockings, while Harold, who had twice failed his intermediate examination and whose principal intellectual interest was the sporting page of the *Daily Mail*, continued to consume the family resources at Gray’s Inn.

“Harold,” she said, and was surprised at the steadiness of her own voice, “is being kept at his studies.”

“Your brother is a man,” said Mrs. Barrow, with a finality that was meant to be crushing.

“I had noticed that,” said Edith. “He has a moustache. But I do not see that the moustache is a reason for keeping him at the bar and putting me behind a cash desk. I have done better in my examinations than Harold has ever done in his. I have a distinction. He has a failure. And yet he is to continue and I am to sell tape.”

Harold put down his fork and looked at her with the mild, pitying expression of a young man who has been reading the *Daily Mail* and knows the world. “I say, Edie,” he said, “that’s a bit thick. A man’s got to have a profession. You can’t expect Mother to keep us both at it. And after all, what’s the good of a woman going in for all this science and stuff? You’ll only get married.”

“I might not get married,” said Edith. “And if I did, I should prefer to marry with a trained mind rather than without one. I have a distinct aptitude for chemistry. Mr. Wainwright said so. He said I had the best hands in the laboratory.”

“Hands!” said Mrs. Barrow, with a small, contemptuous sniff. “It is not a question of hands, Edith. It is a question of money. I have not got the money. There is no more to be said.”

“There is this to be said,” Edith replied, and she found that the words came to her with a curious and almost pleasurable precision, as though she were reading them off a slide under a microscope. “There is this to be said: that you have the money for Harold and not for me, and that the reason is not money at all but a habit of mind. You have never considered me as anything but a piece of furniture that might be expected to earn its keep. You have considered Harold as a person. I have passed my examination with distinction, and you have congratulated me and then told me to go and be a cashier. Harold has failed his, and you have paid his fees again and told him to take care of his health.”

Mrs. Barrow’s colour deepened. “I will not be spoken to in this way,” she said, “by a chit of a girl who has been filling her head with nonsense. Your father would have known how to deal with you. He would have put his foot down.”

“My father,” said Edith, “was a schoolmaster who taught mathematics for thirty years and never had a boy who could beat me at algebra. He would have put his foot down, I think, on the other side.”

There was a silence. Harold ate his mutton. Mrs. Barrow looked at the tablecloth as though she were reading a difficult and unpleasant passage in a book. Edith observed, with a curious detachment, that her own heart was beating very fast and that her hands, which she had clasped in her lap, were trembling, and that these physical phenomena were of precisely the sort one would expect in a subject undergoing a severe emotional stimulus, and that it was possible to note them and to go on talking at the same time.

“I shall not take the position at the draper’s,” she said. “I shall sit for the scholarship examination at the college in November. If I win it, the fees are paid. If I do not win it, I shall find some other way. I am not going to spend my life handing change across a counter because you have decided that Harold is a person and I am a convenience.”

“You will do as I tell you,” said Mrs. Barrow, “while you live under my roof.”

“Then I shall not live under your roof,” said Edith, and was herself surprised at the calmness with which she said it, and at the sudden, clear, cold vision that came to her of a world in which roofs were not, after all, indispensable, and in which a girl of nineteen with a distinction in mathematics and a capacity for hard work might conceivably make her own arrangements.

She rose from the table, and she noticed that her mother’s face had gone from red to a sort of mottled white, and that Harold was looking at her with an expression of genuine bewilderment, as though a piece of furniture had suddenly announced its intention of walking out of the room.

“I shall go and pack my things,” she said. “I have a little money of my own—Aunt Louisa left me three pounds—and I shall go to London in the morning. There are lodgings in Bloomsbury that Mrs. Fennick was speaking of only last week, and there is a woman there who coaches girls for the scholarship examinations. I shall write to you when I have an address.”

She went out of the room, and she heard her mother say something about “wickedness” and “flying in the face of Providence,” and Harold say something about “hysterics,” and then she was in the hall, with her hand on the banister, and she found that she was shaking from head to foot and that she was also, in some curious and inexplicable way, happier than she had ever been in her life.
