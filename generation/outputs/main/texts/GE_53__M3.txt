It is in the nature of calamity to announce itself not by a gradual accumulation of ominous signs, but by a sudden, precipitate shock that scatters the faculties of the afflicted, much as a high wind strips the leaves from a sheltered tree. Mrs. Thurston, widow of the late Reverend Thurston and proprietor of the highly respectable establishment for young ladies in Millbridge, was partaking of her morning tea when the note was brought to her. It was a warm morning in June, 1834, and the air coming through the open window carried the mingled scents of the lilac bushes and the coal-dust from the nearby canal basin. Mrs. Thurston, a woman whose robust intellect and firm sense of propriety had earned her the confidence of the Midland gentry and upper tradesmen, felt a singular tremor in her hand as she broke the seal. The handwriting was that of her senior pupil, Clara Vetch, a girl of seventeen, possessed of a dark, restless beauty and a latent capacity for rebellion that Mrs. Thurston had often attributed to the over-stimulating effects of modern poetry, rather than to any inherent deficiency of moral fibre.

The note was brief, but it carried the weight of a millstone. Clara had departed in the night, accompanied by Monsieur Levasseur, the music master, to be married by license at a distant church. They were, Clara trusted, already on the road to London, beyond the reach of a father’s anger and a schoolmistress’s jurisdiction.

Mrs. Thurston laid the paper down with a deliberate slowness, her mind temporarily arrested by the sudden collapse of the edifice of trust she had spent fifteen laborious years in constructing. She did not immediately conjure up the practical consequences; rather, she felt a profound pity for the blinded rashness of youth, which mistakes the feverish palpitations of a nascent passion for the enduring resolves of a matured will. That a girl so carefully instructed in the geography of the Alps and the principles of moral philosophy should confound an elopement with a French fiddle-player for the summit of human happiness was a contradiction that forced itself upon her with a melancholy irony. But this philosophic pause was necessarily brief; the immediate, pressing terror of the situation resided in the impending arrival of Clara’s father, Mr. Vetch, a wealthy manufacturer of worsted stuffs, whose immense mills belched smoke over the eastern valley, and whose disposition was as inflexible and unyielding as the iron machinery he employed.

Mr. Vetch was a man who had fought his way up from a barefoot apprenticeship to a position of formidable civic power, and he carried his wealth with the heavy, aggressive tread of one who expects it to command absolute deference. He valued education solely as a marketable accomplishment, a varnish to be laid over the surface of a girl to enhance her price in the matrimonial market, and he had paid Mrs. Thurston’s high fees with the explicit understanding that Clara should be rendered impervious to any disgraceful attachment. The music master had been engaged on his own recommendation, a man of melancholy aspect and fluent conversational powers, whose poverty was generally supposed to be the result of Continental revolutions rather than personal vice. Mrs. Thurston, reflecting on the subtle, insidious growth of the intimacy between the master and the pupil, saw with retrospective clarity how the sonatas of Beethoven, laden with a passionate vagueness quite unsuited to the English temperament, had served as a vehicle for emotions that a stricter curriculum of arithmetic and plain sewing might never have awakened.

It was scarcely ten o’clock when the sharp peal of the front-door bell announced Mr. Vetch. He was shown into the parlour, his heavy visage suffused with a choleric red, his hat grasped firmly in his large hand as if it were a weapon.

“Where is my daughter, ma’am?” he began, without the preliminary of a greeting. “I have had a letter from that French blackguard, crowing over the trick he has played us. He has got her, and he has got her under your roof, Mrs. Thurston!”

“Pray be seated, Mr. Vetch,” said Mrs. Thurston, her voice retaining its measured composure, though her heart beat with a painful velocity. “I will not dissemble. I received a communication from Clara not an hour since. She has gone, and I fear she is beyond our immediate recall.”

Mr. Vetch struck his hat against the table with a force that made the tea-spoons rattle. “Beyond recall! Aye, that is a fine, soft phrase for a thing that is a downright ruin. I gave her into your charge, Mrs. Thurston, a charge I paid fifty guineas a year for, and you tell me she has gone off with a beggarly fiddler! What is your school come to, ma’am, when a man may walk in and steal the goods from under the mistress’s nose?”

“The epithets you employ, Mr. Vetch, though natural to a father’s anguish, do not mend the matter,” Mrs. Thurston replied, drawing herself up. “I am not unconscious of my responsibility. But the supervision of a school is not the guarding of a fortress. We deal with human affections, which are prone to shoot up in the most unlooked-for directions. Monsieur Levasseur was accredited by gentlemen of standing, and his conduct, until this fatal event, was beyond reproach. He did not woo her openly; he relied on the subtle, unlooked-for sympathy that arises between a teacher and a pupil over a difficult passage of music. I cannot be held accountable for the clandestine plotting of two persons determined on deceit.”

“Deceit! I’ll deceit him!” exclaimed Mr. Vetch, his broad Midlands vowels roughening with fury. “If he comes within reach of my arm, I’ll make him wish he’d stuck to his fiddle-strings in his own country. But it’s not him I’m speaking to now. It’s you, ma’am. I ask you, what is your school worth, if a young woman can be made a fool of under your very eyes? I’ll have the law on you, Mrs. Thurston. I’ll have it known about Millbridge what sort of an academy you keep, where daughters are taught to run off with Papists and music-masters.”

Mrs. Thurston’s pale face flushed with a quiet indignation. “You speak in the heat of the moment, Mr. Vetch, and I will not retort as your words deserve. But I will say this: if you publish my disgrace, you publish your daughter’s. You cannot ruin my school without irreparably staining Clara’s name. And she, however foolishly, is now a married woman, or soon will be. To brand her before the world is to make her life a prolonged penance, and to cut off any hope of her returning to respectability.”

Mr. Vetch was momentarily checked by this consideration. He was a hard man, but he was not devoid of a shrewd, calculating affection for his own flesh and blood, and the thought of his daughter’s name becoming a byword in the counting-houses and chapels of Millbridge gave him a sharp, inward pang. He walked to the window and looked out upon the quiet street, where the daughters of the local drapers and attorneys were passing with their music-books, oblivious of the tragedy being enacted behind the muslin curtains.

“And what’s to be the end on’t?” he asked, his voice dropping a tone. “She’s married to a man without a shilling. Am I to keep them?”

“That, Mr. Vetch, is a question for your own conscience to determine,” Mrs. Thurston said, her tone softening, as she perceived the gradual subsidence of his combative heat. “I can only implore you not to act with a vengeance that will recoil upon your own head. The girl is young, and she has been duped by the romantic illusions which, I grant you, it is the duty of a school to counteract. But the deed is done. If you cast her off, you drive her into a depth of poverty and desperation from which no human effort may retrieve her. If you receive her, you may, at least, exercise some restraint over her future.”

Mr. Vetch turned slowly from the window, his brow heavy with the conflicting pressures of pride, anger, and a reluctant, unacknowledged paternal love. He looked at Mrs. Thurston, and saw in her calm, sorrowful face a reflection of his own baffled helplessness. He was a man who had conquered markets and subdued strikes, who had bent the material world to his will; and here was a catastrophe that no amount of capital could avert, a wrong that no legal process could set right.

“Well,” he said, at last, with a heavy sigh that seemed to exhale a portion of his rage, “I’ll not say what I’ll do yet. But I’ll not publish it i’ the streets, ma’am. I’ll go after them to London, and if I find him, I’ll make him feel the weight of my hand, if I don’t break every bone in his body. And as for you, Mrs. Thurston, I’ll say this: I thought you’d have been sharper. A father trusts a woman like you because he hasn’t the time to watch his child himself. You’ve failed me, and I’ll not send another girl to your school. But I’ll not cry out on you, for the sake of the lass. Only, see to it that you keep a stricter hand over your music-masters in future, for they’re a bad lot, from first to last.”

He placed his hat upon his head with a grim deliberation, and went out into the street, leaving Mrs. Thurston alone with the ruin of her hopes and the enduring, unspectacular duty of repairing, as far as might be, the shattered fabric of her remaining life.
