The house in Weedington Road was, in the opinion of Mrs. Mudge, a respectable establishment. It had a yellow-brick front, a flight of steps that were swept every morning, and lodgers who were, for the most part, clerks and commercial travellers. The exception was Mr. Armitage.

Mr. Armitage was a gentleman, or something very like one. He was a physicist. This meant, as far as Mrs. Mudge could determine, that he sat in his room on the second floor and did things with wires, glass tubes, and a peculiar, low humming noise that sometimes set her teeth on edge. He paid his rent on the first of the month, which was the principal thing, but he was otherwise a great trial. He had a habit of forgetting his meals, of leaving his door ajar at unseemly hours, and of staring at one with an intensity that suggested he was not seeing you at all, but rather the invisible forces of which you were merely a temporary and rather inefficient arrangement.

It was a Tuesday in November, the kind of damp, grey day that gets into your bones and makes the very washing seem a sodden, hopeless task. Mrs. Mudge stood in the basement scullery, wringing out a heavy shirt, her hands red and raw. The mangle groaned. The boiler hissed. The basket of wet linen beside her was a monument to a morning’s labour that was only just begun.

“Damn and blast it,” she said to the copper, for there was no one else to hear.

It was at this moment that Mr. Armitage appeared in the doorway. He was a tall, thin man with a shock of untidy brown hair and eyes that burned with a restless, intellectual fire. He was wearing his usual tweed jacket and was holding a small, brass instrument that looked rather like a barometer.

“Mrs. Mudge,” he said, his voice quick and clear. “A moment. I could not help but overhear your… articulation of frustration.”

“It’s the washing, sir,” she said, feeling a flush of embarrassment. “It’s a dreadful day for it.”

“A fundamental problem of the domestic economy,” he agreed, nodding as if she had just confirmed a complex theory. “The conversion of water and soap and energy into cleanliness is a slow, entropic process. And yet, what if it were not?”

He stepped into the scullery, his gaze fixed not on her, but on the wall behind the boiler. “What if the temporal dimension of the task could be… compressed?”

Mrs. Mudge stared at him. “Compressed, sir? I don’t rightly know what you mean. I can’t make the water heat any faster.”

“No, no, you misunderstand,” he said, with the impatience of a man explaining something obvious to a child. “It is not a matter of thermodynamics. It is a matter of chronology. I have made a discovery, Mrs. Mudge. A most profound discovery. In the room above, the one I have had the electrician wire so extensively, I have succeeded in creating a localized temporal field.”

He saw the blankness in her eyes and sighed. “I have made a room in which time runs slower. Much slower.”

There was a silence, broken only by the drip of water from a wet sheet. Mrs. Mudge felt a familiar mixture of awe and suspicion that this particular lodger always inspired. “Slower, sir? You mean… a minute in there is longer than a minute out here?”

“Precisely! An hour in the field might correspond to only a minute of external time. Or, to put it another way, I can run a process inside that room for an hour, and it will be finished before you have had time to put the kettle on.”

He was looking at her now, his eyes bright with a terrible, evangelical zeal. “I have observed you, Mrs. Mudge. I have observed your struggle with the endless, cyclical nature of the laundry. It is a perfect application. A practical demonstration. Let me help you. Bring your washing. All of it.”

She must have looked doubtful, for he softened his tone. “Think of it. The entire week’s wash, finished and dry, in the time it takes to boil an egg. It is an offer of… efficiency.”

The prospect was too dazzling to refuse. The drudgery, the endless hours of the wringer and the ironing board, all vanquished in a moment! It was mad, of course. It was the sort of thing that only happened in penny dreadfuls. But he was a scientist. He had a degree. And the rent was, as always, paid.

“Very well, sir,” she said, her voice trembling slightly. “If you’re sure.”

“Excellent!” he clapped his hands together. “We shall begin at once.”

His room was just as she had imagined: a chaos of papers, books, and strange brass and glass apparatus. But in the centre of the room, clearing a space of perhaps ten feet square, was the device. It was not what she expected. There was no great machine, no spinning flywheel or roaring engine. There were simply four polished steel posts, one at each corner of the space, connected by a web of fine copper wires that hummed with a faint, almost subliminal energy. On the floor was a circle of some dark, non-reflective material.

“The field is perfectly safe,” Mr. Armitage said, seeing her hesitation. “The boundaries are absolute. You will place the washing-basket within the circle. I will activate the field. You will wait. Then you will retrieve your laundry. The entire process will take, from your perspective, no more than a minute.”

She did as she was told. She placed the heavy wicker basket in the centre of the dark circle. The air in the room felt thick, charged, like the moment before a thunderstorm.

Mr. Armitage moved to a control panel on the wall, a bewildering array of dials and switches. “Now, Mrs. Mudge,” he said, his voice low and intense. “Observe.”

He threw a large lever. The humming in the wires intensified, rising in pitch to a sound that was less a noise and more a pressure in the ears. The air within the circle seemed to shimmer, to thicken, like a bubble of clear glass. Through it, the basket of washing looked perfectly normal, but somehow distant, like an object seen through a deep pool of water.

“Time is now proceeding in there at approximately one one-hundredth of its normal rate,” Mr. Armitage narrated, his eyes fixed on a chronometer. “An hour will pass inside in thirty-six seconds.”

Mrs. Mudge watched, mesmerised. She could see nothing happening. The shirts and sheets were just… there. But the man beside her was trembling with excitement, his knuckles white where he gripped the edge of his console. He was not watching the washing.
