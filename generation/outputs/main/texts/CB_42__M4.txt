The pensionnat of Mademoiselle Vérine stood in a narrow street of the old town of Bruxelles, a tall, shabby-genteel house whose windows looked upon a courtyard where a single pear-tree, stunted and crooked, yearly repented of its ambition to blossom. It was a place of perpetual small noises: the dry cough of the French master, the shuffle of slippers on the stairs, the shrill recitative of girls conjugating verbs of the first conjugation. And beneath these, like a bass accompaniment, ran the ceaseless, fretful, subterranean contention of the two governesses—Mademoiselle Delacroix and Miss Pemberton.

I was the English teacher. I had come to this city with a trunk of books and a foolish hope of independence, and I found myself, at six-and-twenty, lodged in a garret and daily engaged in the instruction of twelve young ladies in the composition of themes and the correct pronunciation of “th.” It was not the life I had imagined; but it was bread, and I had learned early that bread is not to be despised.

Mademoiselle Delacroix was the elder. She had been at the pensionnat fifteen years, and she wore her seniority as a queen wears her crown—that is, with a constant, anxious consciousness of its weight. She was thin, sallow, with eyes that moved too quickly and a mouth that smiled too seldom; yet there was a certain grandeur in her despair, as though she had been cast for tragedy and condemned to farce. She taught French and history, and she believed—sincerely, I think—that the school would dissolve into chaos without her.

Miss Pemberton was younger, perhaps thirty, and English. She had a round face, a high colour, and a manner of hearty candour that concealed a nature as wary as a cat’s. She taught music and geography, and she had a way of saying “My dear” to everyone, which made me feel at once soothed and suspicious. She had been at the pensionnat three years, and she spoke of her position as though it were a temporary exile from a better world—a world of which she never spoke precisely, but which I gathered to be somewhere in the vicinity of Bath.

Between these two there existed a hatred so pure, so constant, that it might almost have been called a passion. They never quarrelled openly; that would have been beneath the dignity of the pensionnat. They merely exchanged, in the presence of the pupils, civilities of such exquisite irony that the air seemed to curdle. Mademoiselle Delacroix would inquire, with a smile, whether Miss Pemberton had found the English newspapers sufficiently—she paused—*instructive*; and Miss Pemberton would reply, with a laugh, that she found them a great comfort, as they reminded her of home. Then both would look at me, as though I were a judge who had failed to deliver sentence.

I understood, soon enough, what they wanted. Mademoiselle Vérine, the headmistress, was old and growing deaf, and she had let it be known that she intended to appoint a *sous-maîtresse*—a deputy, a successor in all but name. The two governesses circled the old woman like birds of prey who dare not strike until the prey has consented to die. And each of them, in her own way, sought my alliance.

Mademoiselle Delacroix came to me first. She found me in the courtyard, one grey afternoon in March, watching the pear-tree contend with the wind.

“You are solitary, mademoiselle,” she said, standing beside me with her hands folded. “I, too, am solitary. It is the fate of those who think.”

I said nothing. She went on, in her low, rapid voice, of the school’s decline, of the lax discipline, of the pupils who learned nothing, of the parents who complained of everything. She spoke of Miss Pemberton as one speaks of a disease—with a kind of clinical disgust.

“She is not serious,” she said. “She is not—how do you say?—she is not *profonde*. She plays the piano and she smiles, and the girls adore her. But a school is not a theatre, mademoiselle. A school is a—a *forge* of the soul.”

I said that I had not observed any especial want of seriousness in Miss Pemberton. Mademoiselle Delacroix looked at me with her quick, unhappy eyes.

“You are young,” she said. “You do not see. But I have seen. I have seen fifteen years of it. And when I am gone—if I am gone—you will see too.”

She left me with a pressure of the hand that was meant to be affectionate, but which felt like a warning.

Miss Pemberton came to me the next day, in the schoolroom, while the girls were at their dinner. She sat on the edge of a desk and swung her foot, and she said, with her hearty laugh:

“My dear, I hope you don’t mind my saying so, but you look positively pale. That woman has been talking to you, hasn’t she? I can always tell. She has a way of draining the colour out of people.”

I said that Mademoiselle Delacroix had spoken to me of the school.

“Of the school!” cried Miss Pemberton. “Of herself, you mean. My dear, she is the most unhappy creature in Bruxelles, and she would make everyone else unhappy if she could. She has been here fifteen years, and she has never forgiven the world for it. I am sorry for her—I am, indeed—but I cannot let her drag me down with her.”

She leaned forward, and her round face grew serious.

“You are new here,” she said. “You do not know how it has been. She has ruled this school—oh, not openly, but with her sighs and her silences and her little remarks. And now that Madame Vérine is failing, she thinks the place is hers by right. But it is not. A school needs life, my dear—music, laughter, a little sunshine. Not perpetual fog.”

I asked her what she wished me to do.

“Do?” She laughed again, but it was a little forced. “I wish you to do nothing. Only—when Madame Vérine asks you, as she will ask you—speak the truth. That is all.”

I said that I would speak the truth. She looked at me for a moment, and then she said, “You are a good creature,” and kissed me on the forehead, and went away.

I do not know what truth I spoke. I know that when Madame Vérine summoned me to her parlour, and asked me, in her cracked, patient voice, whether I thought Mademoiselle Delacroix was fitted for the post of deputy, I said that she was a woman of great experience and devotion. And when she asked me whether Miss Pemberton was fitted, I said that she was a woman of great energy and cheerfulness. I said nothing of the hatred between them, nothing of the little ironies, the small cruelties, the daily warfare of sighs. I told myself that it was not my business—that I was a stranger, a teacher of English, and that I had no right to judge. I told myself that I was being just. I was, in truth, being a coward.

The appointment was made in April. It was Miss Pemberton who received it. Madame Vérine, I think, had grown tired of Mademoiselle Delacroix’s tragedy; she wanted, in her old age, a little sunshine.

Mademoiselle Delacroix received the news in the schoolroom, before the girls. She said nothing. She looked at Miss Pemberton, and then at me, and her face was like a white stone. Then she gathered her books together, and walked out of the room, and we heard her go up the stairs, slowly, as though she were carrying something heavy.

She left the pensionnat a week later. I saw her go from my window—a thin figure in black, with a small valise, walking down the narrow street in the rain. She did not look back.

That night, Miss Pemberton came to my room. She was flushed and triumphant, and she said, “My dear, I must thank you. I know you spoke for me.”

I said that I had spoken for no one. I had only answered Madame Vérine’s questions.

She laughed, and said, “You are too modest. But I know. And I shall not forget.”

She went away, and I sat alone in my garret, and I thought of Mademoiselle Delacroix walking in the rain. I thought of her fifteen years, of her thin hands, of her unhappy eyes. I thought of the word I had not spoken—the word that might have saved her, or at least have warned her. I had been silent, and my silence had been a lie.

The pear-tree in the courtyard did not blossom that year. The wind broke it in May, and it stood, a black stump, against the grey sky. I looked at it every morning from my window, and I thought: *That is what silence does.* It does not save. It only postpones the breaking.

I did not stay long in Bruxelles. I went back to England in the autumn, and I have never returned. But I have never forgotten Mademoiselle Delacroix, walking in the rain. And I have never again told myself that it was not my business.
