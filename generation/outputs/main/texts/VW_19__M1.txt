The dust lay like a grey thought upon the mahogany, a fine, settled sediment of the day’s toil; and the cold, which had crept under the doors and through the sash-frames all night, met her hand as she pushed it open, a cold that was not the chill of the air but of the room itself, a room put away for the dark hours. Mrs. Featherby—for so she was known to the solicitors and to herself in the small, sharp voice of her own conscience—stood for a moment on the threshold, her bucket a dull silver moon at her feet. The gas lamps in the street outside had just been put out, and a grey, watery light, the colour of a fish’s belly, was beginning to seep in, touching the spines of the legal volumes that stood in solemn, unread ranks, catching the brass handles of the heavy desks. It was a light that revealed nothing, that seemed rather to gather up all the shadows and hold them close.

She moved into the silence, a silence so complete it seemed to have a weight. Her own footsteps, muffled by the worn linoleum, were the only sound, a soft, rhythmic shush that marked the beginning of her day’s undoing of the previous one’s work. Mr. Pemberton’s desk was first. She knew its geography as well as the lines on her own palm. The inkwell, still faintly stained at its lip; the blotter, a map of forgotten signatures; the silver letter-opener lying just so. And there, half-hidden by a leather-bound brief, a letter. The paper was fine, a creamy white that seemed to absorb the nascent light. *My Dearest Arthur*, it began, the ink a dark, decisive blue. She did not read on. She did not need to. The two words were enough. They hung in the air of the empty office, a small, warm breath in the cold. My Dearest Arthur. She pictured him, Mr. Pemberton, with his neat moustache and his worried eyes, reading these words in the quiet of his own home. She pictured the hand that had written them, a woman’s hand, no doubt, a hand that knew how to form the letters with such a graceful, certain slant. A whole life was in that slant. She dusted around the letter, her cloth a gentle, circling bird.

She moved to the next desk, that of Mr. Gable, a younger man with a restless energy that seemed to leave a faint, charged atmosphere in his wake. His papers were not in neat piles but in a state of arrested chaos, as if a wind had blown through and then died. Here was not a letter of love but of business, though perhaps the two were not so different. *Further to our conversation of the 14th inst., we must insist upon the settlement of the account for the shipping of goods to Halifax. The vessel, the ‘Sea Serpent’, is now at anchor and the demurrage is accruing at an alarming rate…* Halifax. The word was a stone dropped into the still pool of the morning. It sent out ripples. Not Canada, not the Canada of her own thoughts, but a part of it, a port on its edge. She saw a ship, a great iron thing, grey against a grey sea, and the name upon its prow, the ‘Sea Serpent’. She saw the crates being unloaded, the rough hands of men shouting in a language she did not know. Her own son, Tom, had sailed from Liverpool. She had stood on the dock, the wind whipping her hair into her eyes, and watched his ship, the ‘Dominion’, grow smaller and smaller until it was a smudge, a memory, a promise on the horizon. He wrote of snow. Of a silence deeper than this one, a white, blinding silence that covered everything. He wrote of trees taller than church spires. She folded the thought of him away, a letter in her mind she could read at night, by the thin light of her own lamp.

Her path took her now to the tall windows at the back of the office, the windows that looked out upon the river. It was this view that made the office seem, at times, not a place of law and money but of something older, something that flowed on regardless. The Thames was not a river of poetry this morning. It was a sheet of hammered pewter, sluggish and immense, carrying the city’s silt and secrets on its slow, powerful back. A barge, low in the water and piled with what looked like sleeping coal, moved with a ponderous dignity towards the bridge. The air that seeped through the window frame smelled of damp stone and the faint, metallic tang of the water itself. She pressed her forehead to the cool glass, the condensation leaving a ghostly print. Down there, somewhere beneath the churn and the flow, was the churchyard where Albert lay. She had not been for a month. The ground would be hard, the grass stiff with frost. She saw his name, carved into the grey stone: ALBERT J. FEATHERBY. 1871-1918. It seemed such a small, neat summary for a man who had filled a house with his pipe smoke and his Sunday paper, his coughing in the winter and his slow, steady tread on the stairs. He was as much a part of the past now as the barges that had sunk and the bridges that had been washed away. And yet he was here, in the chill of the glass, in the grey of the water. The river took everything, she thought, and gave nothing back but its own relentless passage.

A clock struck seven, a distant, official chime from some tower across the water. The sound cut through her reverie, bringing the room back to her. The light was stronger now, the details of the office sharpening into their daytime selves. The letters on the desks were just letters again, the dust just dust. She turned from the window, picked up her cloth and her brush, and began the small, circular motions of her work. The dust rose in little clouds, catching the light for a moment before settling again. She was erasing the night, preparing the stage for the day’s small dramas of debt and desire. And as she worked, the faces of the men who would soon fill the room—their voices, their coughs, the rustle of their papers—began to form in the quiet, not as intrusions, but as the simple, necessary facts of the morning, like the rising of the river or the coming of the light. The room was not empty. It was waiting. And she, Mrs. Featherby, was the one who had prepared it. She wiped the mahogany until it shone, a dark, placid pool in the growing day.
