The rain in Liverpool was a different creature from the rain in Ceylon. It had no warmth, no scent of wet earth and frangipani. It was a cold, grey needle that stitched the sky to the rooftops and the rooftops to the slick, black cobbles of the street. Arthur Penhaligon stood at his window, a cup of tea growing cold in his hand, and watched it fall. He had been a planter once, a man who read the sky for its promises of sun, who knew the weight of a monsoon cloud. Now, at sixty, he was a man who read the rain for its simple, relentless misery.

The letter had arrived three days ago, a flimsy, cheap thing with a smudged postmark. It was from an old acquaintance, a clerk who had retired to Southport, a man named Frobisher who had always been too fond of gossip. *"Saw a ghost today, Penhaligon,"* the spidery script began. *"Or his double. In the Strand, by the shipping offices. Elias Craddock. I'd swear to it. Looking older, of course, and thinner, but that scar by his eye was unmistakable. Thought you should know, given the circumstances. I was sure he was dead."*

Arthur had read the letter four times, his heart a dull, heavy thud against his ribs. Elias Craddock. The name was a splinter of ice in his memory, a thing he had buried deep in the red soil of a hill country estate a thousand lifetimes ago. He had told himself the man was dead. It was a necessary fiction, a lie he had cultivated until it had taken on the weight of truth. Now, Frobisher’s words had cracked the foundations of his quiet, grey existence.

Within a week, the watcher appeared. He was a lascar sailor, Arthur was sure of it. The man had the lean, salt-weathered look of a seafarer, his skin the colour of a well-used teak sideboard. He wore a heavy, dark coat against the damp, but his head was bare, and his dark eyes seemed to miss nothing. He would stand on the corner opposite Arthur’s modest terraced house in Bootle, a silent, immobile figure beneath a guttering gas lamp, a ghost from another, warmer world. He never approached, never spoke, never made any sign. He simply watched, and his presence was a constant, gnawing pressure in Arthur’s chest. He was a message, a portent, a shadow cast by the past.

The watching frayed Arthur’s nerves until they were raw. He stopped sleeping, his nights filled with the scent of jasmine and the taste of arrack, with the remembered heat of the sun on his neck. On the fifth day, he could bear it no longer. He put on his coat and hat and, ignoring the lascar’s steady gaze, walked to the offices of a private enquiry agent he had seen advertised in the newspaper. The man’s name was Silas Webb.

Webb’s office was a single, cramped room above a pawnbroker’s. It smelled of coal smoke, old paper, and a faint, sweetish tang of pipe tobacco. Webb himself was a small, wiry man with a face like a crumpled paper bag, his eyes sharp and inquisitive behind small, round spectacles. He listened without interruption as Arthur, haltingly, laid out the bare facts: the letter, the sighting in the Strand, the lascar who watched his house.

“And this Mr. Craddock,” Webb said, steepling his fingers. “What does he want with you, after all this time?”

“I don’t know,” Arthur replied, his voice tight. “We were partners, once. In Ceylon. A tea plantation.”

“And how did the partnership end?”

Arthur looked at the rain-streaked window. He saw the tea bushes rolling like a green sea under a brilliant blue sky, he saw the whitewashed bungalow, and he saw Elias Craddock’s face, younger, but with the same reckless light in his eyes. “It ended,” he said, his voice barely a whisper. “It simply ended. I returned to England. He stayed.”

Webb’s eyes narrowed. “People do not send lascar sailors to watch a man’s house because a partnership ‘simply ended,’ Mr. Penhaligon. There’s a debt, I’ll wager. Or a grievance. Which is it?”

“I can’t say,” Arthur insisted, a flush of shame creeping up his neck. “It’s a private matter. A thing of the past. I just need to know what he wants. If he means me harm. Find the lascar. Ask him. Find Craddock. I’ll pay whatever is necessary.”

Webb sighed, a dry rustle of sound. He was a man who dealt in facts, not in the hazy regrets of old planters. But business was business. He took down the details and promised to report back within the week.

The days that followed were a torment. The lascar remained at his post, a silent sentinel. Arthur felt his gaze on him as he drew his curtains, as he lit his evening fire, as he paced his small parlour like a caged animal. He began to see Craddock’s face everywhere—in the faces of men on the tram, in the reflection of his own window. The past was no longer a distant country; it was an army massing at his gates.

Five days later, Webb returned. He refused Arthur’s offer of tea and stood, hat in hand, his expression unreadable. “I found your lascar,” he said. “His name is Ismail. He’s a sailor on a freighter out of Calcutta, the *Star of Bengal*. He’s been paid to watch you, that’s certain. Paid by a man he calls ‘the Sahib.’ A man with a scar by his eye.”

Arthur’s heart clenched. “Craddock. It’s him. What does he want?”

“That’s the difficulty, Mr. Penhaligon,” Webb said, his voice low. “Ismail wouldn’t say. He’s afraid of this Sahib. He says only that the Sahib is a man who does not forget. He says the Sahib is waiting for you to make the first move.”

“Make the first move? What does that mean?”

“It means he’s giving you a chance to settle your account,” Webb said, his gaze piercing. “He’s letting you know he’s here, and he’s waiting. The question is, what is it he thinks you owe him? This is where you must be plain with me. This is not a game, sir. This is a man’s life, perhaps yours. What happened in Ceylon?”

Arthur turned away, his shoulders slumping. The shame of it, the old, familiar cowardice, was a physical weight. He remembered the fire. The roar of it, the heat that cracked the bungalow’s timbers like gunshots. He remembered the ledgers, the ones he had so carefully altered, the ones that showed his own desperate, secret debts while Craddock’s name was clean. He remembered the argument, the fury in Craddock’s eyes as he realised the betrayal. And he remembered the fire, how it had started in the store-room where the ledgers were kept, and how he, Arthur, had run, had saved himself, had let the world believe Elias Craddock had perished in the blaze that had consumed their partnership and his own dishonour.

“There was a fire,” Arthur whispered, the words tasting like ash in his mouth. “I thought he died in it. I was… I was relieved.”

Webb was silent for a long moment. “I see,” he said finally, his voice devoid of judgement. “Then I suspect Mr. Craddock doesn’t want your money, Mr. Penhaligon. He wants you to admit to the fire. To the lie. He’s been waiting twenty years to hear you say it.”

When Webb had gone, Arthur sat in the dark, the lascar’s shadow a dark stain against the streetlamp’s glow. He thought of the life he had built on the ashes of that lie—a life of quiet, grey anonymity. It was a prison, he realised, but it was one he had chosen. Now, the jailer had arrived. He could run again, hide in another city, another country. Or he could face the ghost he had created.

He stood up, his joints protesting. He walked to the window and looked out at the lascar, who met his gaze without flinching. Arthur nodded, a slow, deliberate gesture. He would not run. He would not wait to be summoned. He would go to the docks, to the *Star of Bengal*, and he would find Elias Craddock. He would look him in the eye and he would give him what he was owed. The rain continued to fall, cold and indifferent, but for the first time in twenty years, Arthur Penhaligon felt a flicker of warmth within him. It was the warmth of a truth about to be told.
