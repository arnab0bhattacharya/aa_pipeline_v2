The letter from the solicitor had been dry as a pressed leaf, but its contents had set a quiet fire in Edith’s chest. A legacy, small but entirely her own. Her aunts had been scandalised. A young woman, unmarried, travelling to the Continent alone? To Sicily, of all places, that barbarous island of brigands and lemon groves? Her cousin Margaret had called it a flight of fancy, a waste of good money that ought to have been put towards a sensible trousseau. But Edith had simply packed her trunk. She had spent two years watching the light fade from her grandmother’s eyes, two years of hushed rooms and the smell of camphor and boiled milk. Now, she wanted to feel the sun on her face without a shawl, and to hear a language she did not have to understand.

Palermo in April was a city of noisy, sun-bleached stone, a labyrinth of alleys that spilled into sudden, brilliant piazzas. Edith had taken a room in a pensione near the Quattro Canti, a tall, shabby building with a courtyard where a single, desiccated lemon tree fought for the sky. Her room was small, with a balcony that looked over a choked side-street. She had no plan, no guidebook, and no desire for company. For the first week, she did nothing but walk.

She walked until her boots were grey with dust and her skin tasted of salt and grit. She learned the rhythm of the city not through its monuments, but through its daily rituals. She watched the fishmongers on the Vucciria, their hands swift and silver as they gutted swordfish with blades the size of oars. She saw the old women in black, sitting on their doorsteps like patient crows, their eyes missing nothing. She bought warm, sesame-crusted bread from a baker who spoke to her in a rapid, musical dialect she could not follow, and she would simply nod and smile, and he would smile back, his few remaining teeth like yellowed kernels of corn. She was a ghost at the feast, and she had never felt so substantial.

In the evenings, she would sit at a small table outside a trattoria and drink a glass of dark, bitter wine. She would listen to the ebb and flow of conversation around her, a river of sound she could not enter but could happily watch. She saw families, lovers, old men playing cards with fierce concentration. No one looked at her twice. She was not the grieving granddaughter, nor the spinster cousin, nor the object of pity or concern. She was just a woman in a dark dress, drinking wine in the twilight. The anonymity was a balm. It was a kind of freedom she had not known existed. The weight of her grandmother’s last days, the cloying scent of sickness, the endless, quiet duty, seemed to slough off her with every mile she walked under the Sicilian sun. She was happier than she had ever been.

After a fortnight, she grew restless. The city’s noise began to feel like a cage. She took a train west, to a coastal town called Marina di Sale, a place she had chosen at random from a timetable. It was little more than a clutch of whitewashed houses clinging to a hillside that tumbled down to a crescent of grey sand. The air here was different, sharp with iodine and the scent of wild thyme. She found a room above a taverna, run by a widow named Signora Agnello, a woman with a face like a crumpled map and hands as strong as a sailor’s.

Here, Edith’s days fell into a simple, sun-drenched pattern. She would wake early, walk down to the harbour to watch the fishing boats come in, their hulls heavy with the night’s catch. The men would shout to one another, their voices carrying clearly across the still water. Then she would walk along the shore, collecting the strange, smooth stones that the sea had polished. She found a small, perfect piece of blue glass, worn soft by the waves, and she kept it in her pocket, a talisman against a world she had left behind.

One afternoon, she climbed the path that wound up the hill behind the town. The sun was hot on her back, and the cicadas sang a deafening, electric song. She reached a small, ruined chapel, its roof long gone, the stone walls open to the sky. From here, she could see the whole coast, the deep, impossible blue of the Tyrrhenian Sea, and the distant, hazy shape of the Aeolian Islands. She sat on a fallen block of stone, her heart hammering not from exertion, but from a sudden, overwhelming sense of rightness.

She thought of her aunts, sitting in their stuffy drawing room in Clapham, their talk of propriety and duty. She thought of Margaret, who had already begun to look like a younger version of her mother, her life a preordained script of marriage and motherhood. Their world felt impossibly distant, a faded, sepia photograph. This, the hot stone, the scent of thyme, the vast, indifferent sea, was real. Her grandmother, in her final, lucid moments, had squeezed her hand and whispered, “Don’t be a martyr, my girl. Live.” Edith had thought she understood then, but she had not. She had been too tired, too steeped in the solemnity of the deathbed. Now, she understood. Living was not a grand, noisy affair. It was this. The quiet, solitary joy of being exactly where you were meant to be.

She took the blue glass from her pocket and held it up to the sun. It glowed with a light that was entirely its own. She heard a noise behind her, the soft crunch of footsteps on the path. She turned. It was a boy, perhaps ten years old, with dark, serious eyes and a flock of goats behind him. He stopped, seeing her, and for a moment they just looked at each other. He did not seem surprised to find a foreign woman sitting alone in a ruin. He simply nodded, a small, grave gesture of acknowledgement. Edith nodded back. The boy clicked his tongue, and the goats moved on, their bells tinkling, a faint, tinny music that faded as they disappeared over the crest of the hill.

Edith stayed on the hill until the sun began to sink, painting the sky in streaks of orange and violet. She was in no hurry to return. The silence was not empty; it was full. It was filled with the hum of insects, the whisper of the wind, the slow, steady beat of her own heart. She had come to Sicily to escape, but she had found something instead. She had found a world that was vast and indifferent and beautiful, and in its indifference, she had found a profound and unexpected peace. She was alone, and she was not lonely. She was, for the first time in her life, entirely her own.
