The letter from Somersetshire arrived upon a Tuesday, and by Thursday morning Mrs. Ellinor Vance was seated in the post-chaise beside her husband, her spirits divided between a proper anxiety for her mother and a secret satisfaction in the necessity of the journey, which promised at least a week’s release from the civilities of Bath. Mr. Vance, who had married her for her sense as much as for her person, observed her abstraction with his usual composure, and remarked only that the roads were uncommonly good for the season, and that they should reach Marchmont Priory before dark.

The crisis which had summoned them was described in Mrs. Ashby’s own hand, and in terms so affecting that Ellinor had wept over the letter twice; but upon their arrival, the invalid was discovered in the drawing-room, propped upon a sopha, it is true, but with a shawl arranged to the greatest advantage, and a volume of sermons lying open at her elbow, which she confessed she had not the strength to read. She was suffering, she said, from a general languor of the frame, a complaint too delicate for the comprehension of physicians, who were apt to prescribe only beef-tea and fresh air, and who could not be brought to understand that her nerves were of a texture unknown to the common run of humanity. A cold she admitted there had been; a trifling cold, caught, she believed, in the draught of the church porch on Sunday last, where she had lingered to speak a word of comfort to the widow Pratt; but a cold, in a constitution such as hers, was no light matter, and she had felt it her duty to summon her dearest Ellinor, lest the worst should come, and she should be left to expire among strangers.

“Strangers, Mama?” said Ellinor, kissing her cheek, which was warm and healthful. “You have Betty, and you have Mrs. Cradock, and the apothecary within a mile.”

“Betty is a good creature,” replied Mrs. Ashby, with a sigh, “but she has no sensibility. And as for Mr. Dobbins, I am persuaded he considers me a hypochondriac. He speaks to me of bark and of early hours, as though I were a ploughman. It is a hard fate, my love, to be tenderly organised and rudely understood.”

Mr. Vance, who had followed his wife into the room, now made his bow, and enquired after the invalid’s health with a gravity so respectful that Ellinor, who knew him, was obliged to turn aside her face. Mrs. Ashby received his attentions as her due, extended her hand for him to press, and begged that he would ring for tea, adding that she had ordered the best Bohea on purpose for his arrival, for she knew his taste, and had not forgotten, she hoped, how to do the honours of her own house, even upon a sick-bed.

The days that followed were of a pattern which Ellinor soon came to recognise. Her mother was never so ill as in the presence of company, nor so well as when there was no one to observe her; and Mr. Vance, who might reasonably have been expected to chafe at the confinement of a country house and the society of an invalid, displayed a patience so unwearied that it bordered, in his wife’s estimation, upon the unnatural. He read aloud from the sermons when Mrs. Ashby desired it, though the selection was always of the longest; he listened to the history of her symptoms, which was repeated daily with variations, and he bore the recital with an attention that never flagged; he even consented to play at piquet with her in the evenings, though she cheated openly and complained of the cards when she lost.

“You are very good to her,” said Ellinor one morning, when they were walking together in the shrubbery, and her mother was resting after the exertion of having her pillows arranged three several times. “I did not know you had so much patience in you.”

“She is your mother,” replied Mr. Vance, with a smile that she could not quite interpret. “And she is an invalid.”

“She is nothing of the kind,” said Ellinor, warmly. “She has a cold, and a very ordinary cold, which would be gone in a week if she would but take a little exercise and cease to think of it. You know it as well as I do. Why will you encourage her?”

“I do not encourage her. I merely attend to her. There is a difference.”

“There is none that I can see. You sit by the hour and hear her describe her sensations, which are all invention, and you look as grave as a judge, and you say, ‘Indeed, ma’am,’ and ‘You astonish me,’ and she is confirmed in every folly. I declare I believe you enjoy it.”

Mr. Vance made no immediate answer. They walked the length of the shrubbery, and turned by the sundial, and still he was silent; and Ellinor, who had spoken in something of vexation, began to be afraid that she had said too much.

“I will tell you,” said he at last, “what I enjoy, since you oblige me to confess it. I enjoy the study of a character. Your mother is a woman who has never in her life been of importance to any one but herself, and she has discovered, late, that illness is a kind of importance. It is the only career open to her. She cannot shine in beauty, for she is past her prime; she cannot shine in wit, for she has none; she cannot shine in good works, for she is indolent. But an invalid may command attention, may exact attendance, may make the whole household revolve about her pillow. It is a triumph of ingenuity, and I honour it.”

“You honour it!” cried Ellinor. “You speak as though she were a heroine in a novel.”

“She is a heroine,” said Mr. Vance, “in her own novel, and she plays the part with a consistency that I admire. Consider the address with which she has converted a trifling cold into a mortal disorder; the art with which she has made your journey necessary; the skill with which she has kept us here a fortnight, though the apothecary has pronounced her out of danger these ten days. She is not a bad woman, Ellinor; she is only a woman who has found a vocation. I find her entertaining.”

“Entertaining!”

“Exceedingly. I have known invalids who were dull; your mother is never dull. She has a genius for the malady of the moment. Yesterday it was her nerves; to-day it is her digestion; to-morrow it will be her spirits. She invents variations upon a theme, as a composer might. I assure you I have spent many a worse evening at a rout.”

Ellinor was silent. She was not convinced, but she was disconcerted; for she had a respect for her husband’s understanding, and it was disagreeable to her to find that he had been observing her mother with an amusement which she could not share. She had been accustomed to consider Mrs. Ashby’s complaints as a trial sent to exercise her filial duty, and it was a new idea that they might be regarded as a spectacle.

The occasion soon offered itself for putting his philosophy to the proof. Mrs. Ashby, having caught a second cold, as she declared, from the dampness of the linen-press, was pleased to keep her bed for three days, and to require that her daughter should sit with her from morning till night. On the second evening, when Ellinor was weary and her mother was peevish, Mr. Vance came into the room with a book in his hand, and seating himself by the fire, began to read aloud, without apology, from a volume of Cowper.

Mrs. Ashby listened for a while in silence, which was unusual in her; then she sighed, and desired that the window might be shut, for the air was upon her face. Mr. Vance shut it, and resumed. She sighed again, and observed that the candle smoked. He snuffed it, and read on. She complained that the pillow was too hard, and he rang for Betty, and had it changed, and returned to his book. At last she said, with a voice of great weakness, that she feared she should not live to see the spring.

“You will see the spring, ma’am,” said Mr. Vance, laying down the book, “and many springs after it. You have a constitution of iron.”

“Iron!” cried Mrs. Ashby, starting up on her elbow. “Iron! I, who have been a martyr to my nerves these twenty years!”

“A martyr, certainly,” said he, with a bow. “But martyrs, you know, are of a robust order. They endure. And you endure, ma’am, with a constancy that does you honour. I have seen you this fortnight endure three several changes of weather, the loss of a favourite hen, the insolence of a servant, and the disappointment of a visit from Mrs. Cradock, who sent word she was prevented, and you have borne it all with a fortitude that I can only admire.”

Mrs. Ashby stared at him. It was not the language of condolence, and yet it was not the language of contempt; it was delivered with so much gravity, and with an air of such sincere respect, that she could not determine whether she was being flattered or laughed at. She chose to consider it flattery, and lay back upon her pillow with a smile of conscious merit.

“You are very good,” she said, “very good. I am sure I do not deserve it. But I have always said, Mr. Vance, that you were the only man who understood me.”

“I hope I do, ma’am,” said he, and resumed his reading.

Ellinor, who had watched this scene from the window-seat, now rose and left the room. Her husband found her, an hour later, in the garden, walking by the light of a moon that was nearly full.

“You were right,” she said, without waiting for him to speak. “She is a heroine. And you are a very good actor.”

“I am no actor,” said he. “I am a spectator. There is a great difference. The actor suffers; the spectator enjoys. And I assure you I have enjoyed this fortnight excessively.”

“And I,” said Ellinor, “have been the only sufferer. You might have told me.”

“You would not have believed me. You are too near the stage. A spectator must stand at a distance, or he sees only the paint and the candles. You are her daughter; I am only her son-in-law. It is my privilege to be amused.”

Ellinor laughed, in spite of herself. “And what is my privilege?”

“To be patient,” said he, “and to be kind; and to remember, when she is troublesome, that she is playing the only part she knows, and playing it, I must say, with a spirit that deserves a better theatre.”

They returned to the house, and Ellinor went up to her mother’s room, and sat with her until she slept. And in the morning, when Mrs. Ashby declared herself wonderfully better, and spoke of the danger she had passed, and the care that had been taken of her, Ellinor kissed her, and said that she was very glad, and that they must come again at Christmas.
