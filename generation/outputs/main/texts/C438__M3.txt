The letter was written in the morning and sent by the afternoon post, which meant that by half past seven, when the doorbell rang, it was already too late. Clara stood at the window of the parlour and watched the hansom pull up. Out stepped Edwin, in the grey suit she had always disliked, carrying under his arm the same leather case he carried to the counting house every weekday. He paused on the pavement to adjust his cuff, and she felt a spasm of something that was not quite guilt but lay near it, a physical twisting low in her chest. She had written the letter carefully, choosing phrases she hoped would spare him, though she knew now that no phrasing could spare a man who would learn of his own dismissal from the behaviour of others before he ever read the words themselves. The post was unreliable in that direction. A letter sent in the afternoon might arrive the same evening or might sit in a hall tray until morning. She had gambled on the latter and lost.

Her mother appeared at her shoulder. "Clara, he is here. Why are you standing at the window like a servant? Go and let Mrs Hargreaves take his coat."

Clara turned. "Mama, there is something I must tell you."

"Not now." Her mother pressed her hands flat against her own hips, adjusting the sash of her lilac dress. "Your aunts are already in the dining room and I will not have a scene before Margaret, who has been circling like a vulture since Christmas hoping something will go wrong. Whatever it is, it can keep until tomorrow."

It could not keep until tomorrow, but Clara understood the tone. She descended the stairs and met Edwin in the hall, where Mrs Hargreaves was already reaching for his case. He looked up at her with the expression she had come to think of as his resting state: mild, expectant, faintly pleased, as though the fact of her presence were a small but reliable kindness extended to him by the world. He kissed her hand. She let him. His lips were dry and brief, the kiss of a man who had kissed her hand perhaps four hundred times and no longer registered the act as anything more than punctuation.

"You look well," he said. "Very pretty dress."

It was a green dress, and she had chosen it that morning with a grim deliberateness, as one might choose the clothes to be buried in. She had thought, then, that the letter would arrive before him, and that the evening would be a different kind of ordeal, the kind that follows a fire rather than the kind that precedes it.

"Edwin," she said. "Did you receive my letter?"

He looked at her with his head slightly tilted, the way a dog looks when it hears a sound it cannot identify. "Letter? No, I left the office at five. Has something happened? Is your father unwell?"

She opened her mouth, but from the dining room came the bright artillery of Aunt Margaret's voice, and her mother's hand was on the small of her back, propelling her forward, and the moment folded shut like a book.

They sat down to soup. The table had been extended for the occasion, though the occasion was nothing in particular, merely one of the monthly dinners her parents held to maintain the fiction that the family was a family and not merely a collection of people who shared a surname and a post town. Aunt Margaret sat to Clara's right, Aunt Dorothy to her left, and Edwin across, beside her father. The arrangement placed him directly in her sight line, so that she could not look at her plate without also looking at the space above his head where the japanned lamp cast its steady, respectable light. She ate her soup. It was watercress, and it tasted of nothing.

Aunt Margaret, who was large and red-faced and given to the kind of speech that does not require a listener, was holding forth on the subject of a neighbour's daughter who had married a man in trade and was now, reportedly, happy. She spoke the word "happy" as though it were a diagnosis.

"And of course," Margaret said, turning to include Edwin in her gaze, "you two will be next. When is the wedding to be? It must be soon, surely, Clara, you have been engaged simply ages. People will talk."

Edwin smiled his modest smile. "We are thinking of June," he said. "I am partial to June."

Clara set down her spoon. "June is very warm for a wedding," she said.

"Nonsense," said her mother. "June is perfect. We had the garden at Elmwood in mind. The roses are at their best in June."

Clara looked at Edwin. He was eating his bread, tearing it in the careful, portioned way he did everything, a man who had never in his life wanted more than was offered to him. He was kind. He had never raised his voice, never drunk past the second glass, never looked at another woman, never failed to ask after her health or her mother's health or the health of the cat. He had proposed to her on a Tuesday, in this very room, standing by the mantelpiece, and he had spoken for eleven minutes, she had counted, and in those eleven minutes he had not once said her name. He had said "marriage" and "partnership" and "future" and "comfort" and "arrangement" and "suitable" and he had produced from his pocket a ring that was exactly the ring one would expect, a small diamond in a gold band, and she had taken it because she could think of no reason not to, because refusing would have required a reason, and she had none, only a vast and formless reluctance that she had mistaken, for twelve months, for inadequacy in herself.

The fish came. Edwin ate it methodically. Her father asked him about the counting house and he answered in the terms her father enjoyed, speaking of ledgers and balances and the soundness of certain investments, and her father nodded and made approving sounds, and Clara understood that she was watching a transaction that had been completed long ago, in which she was the goods and her consent had been merely the final signature, the one that arrived in the post and was filed away and never read again.

She thought of the letter, which even now might be lying on the hall table of his boarding house in Pimlico, propped against the brass tray where he left his key each evening. She imagined him returning to it after this dinner, sitting down in his armchair, opening it with the penknife he kept in his waistcoat pocket. She imagined his face. It would not change. That was the worst of it. His face would not change because the machinery behind it was not built for sudden turns. He would read the letter and he would read it again and then he would sit for a long time and then he would fold it and place it in the drawer of his writing desk and in the morning he would go to the counting house and at lunch he would eat his sandwich and in the evening he would come here, perhaps, and speak to her father, and the arrangements would be undone in the same quiet, methodical way they had been made, and no one would raise their voice and nothing would break.

Aunt Dorothy, who was smaller and quieter than her sister and had the habit of saying things that landed slightly after one expected them to, leaned towards Clara and said, "You are very pale tonight, dear. Are you quite well?"

"I am well, Aunt Dorothy."

"You do not look well. You look as though you have done something you ought not to have done."

Clara met her aunt's eyes and found in them something she had not expected: not malice, not curiosity, but a kind of tired recognition, the look of a woman who had perhaps, forty years ago, written a letter of her own and not sent it.

The meat came, and then the pudding, and then the conversation turned to the suffrage meeting that had been held in Kensington Town Hall the previous week, which Aunt Margaret pronounced "a disgrace" and Aunt Dorothy pronounced "interesting" and Clara's mother pronounced "not a topic for the dinner table." Edwin said nothing on the subject, because Edwin never said anything on any subject that had not first been cleared by someone else. He looked at Clara across the table and smiled, and she smiled back, and the smile was the most dishonest thing she had ever done in her life, worse than the letter, worse than the year of silence, because it was given freely and it cost her nothing and it would be the last thing he remembered of her when he read the words she had written.

After dinner the men retired to the smoking room and the women to the drawing room, and Clara sat on the window seat and listened to her aunts discuss the cost of linen and felt the green dress tight against her ribs. Through the wall she could hear her father's laugh and Edwin's measured, careful voice, and she thought: he is there, and he does not know, and in an hour he will go home and he will know, and the world will be exactly the same as it was before, only emptier, and he will not understand why.

She pressed her forehead against the cold glass. Outside, the street was dark and the lamps were lit and the rain had begun, soft and steady, washing the pavement clean of whatever footprints had been left there during the day. She watched a woman pass under the light, walking quickly, her head bare, her coat pulled close, and Clara envied her with a ferocity that startled her, the simple ferocity of wanting to be someone who was going somewhere and did not have to explain.
