The rain fell in a slanting, persistent drizzle, turning the street-lamps of Prince’s Road into smudges of yellow light upon the glistening flagstones. It was a night fit only for the hearth, yet the man who stood before me was plainly a stranger to comfort. He was tall and lean, with the tanned, leathery skin of one long exposed to the Eastern sun, and his hands, which gripped the arms of my visitor’s chair, were those of a man accustomed to labour. His name, he told me, was Mr. Arthur Penhaligon, and he was lately retired from the tea-planting trade in Ceylon.

“I have come to you, Mr. Vance,” he began, his voice a low rumble, “because my own resources are at an end. I am a plain man and I dislike a mystery, yet I find myself at the heart of one.”

I nodded, encouraging him to proceed. In my profession, one learns that the preamble is often as revealing as the tale itself.

“For twenty years I was a partner in the Highfield Estate, up in the Kandyan hills,” he said. “My partner was a man named Josiah Croft. We started with little more than our wits and our hands, and we made a success of it. But seven years ago, there was a… a dissolution. I came home to England. I had every reason to believe Josiah Croft was dead. A letter from a mutual acquaintance in Colombo confirmed it. A fever, they said, contracted after the partnership was ended.”

He paused, his gaze fixed on the coal fire, as if seeing some distant, tropical flame.

“And now?” I prompted.

“Now, a week ago, I saw him.”

“Here in Liverpool?”

“Not precisely. I saw a man in the crowd at the Pier Head who was the very image of him, before the press of people carried him away. I told myself it was a trick of the light, a phantom born of the damp and my own memories. But then, three days ago, I saw him again. This time I am certain. He was standing in the shadow of a warehouse in Wapping, watching the road that leads to my house.”

A cold knot tightened in my stomach. The matter was acquiring a sinister aspect.

“And since then?”

“Since then, my house has been watched. Not by Croft himself, but by one of his creatures. A lascar. A dark, sullen fellow in a sailor’s jacket who has taken to loitering at the end of the lane. He does nothing. He simply stands there, smoking his cheroots and watching my windows. My servant has seen him every night. It is unnerving, Mr. Vance. It is the waiting that wears upon a man’s nerves.”

I rose and took a turn about the room. The case was not complex in its outline, but its details were shrouded in a fog as thick as the one outside.

“You wish me to ascertain Mr. Croft’s intentions?”

“Precisely. I want to know what he wants. I want to know why he has come back from the dead.”

“And the nature of your partnership’s dissolution,” I said carefully. “It is often the key to such matters. A debt, perhaps? Or a disagreement over the estate’s profits?”

Mr. Penhaligon’s face became a mask of stone. He rose to his feet, his tall frame casting a long shadow on the wall.

“That is my affair, sir, and my affair alone. I will not speak of it. I have told you all that is necessary. You are an investigator, not a confessor. Find out what Josiah Croft wants from me, and you will be well rewarded. If you cannot do this without prying into matters that do not concern you, then I must wish you good evening.”

His tone was final, his reserve as impenetrable as a fortress wall. I saw that to press him further would be futile. The man’s fear was real, but his secret was greater still.

“Very well, Mr. Penhaligon,” I said. “I will take the case. Give me the address of your residence, and I shall begin my observations tonight.”

He gave it—a respectable villa in Aigburth—and left me with a curt nod and the heavy tread of a man carrying a great weight upon his shoulders.

For two nights I kept a watch upon the house in Aigburth. It was as Penhaligon had described. The lascar, a man with a face like carved teak and a gold ring in his ear, was a punctual sentinel. He took up his post at dusk and remained until the small hours, a silent, smoking statue at the corner of the lane. On the third night, as the fellow made to leave, I fell into step beside him.

“A wet night for a stroll,” I remarked in Hindustani.

He stopped dead, his dark eyes narrowing with suspicion. He was not a man to be easily startled.

“What is it to you?” he replied in the same tongue, his voice a low hiss.

“Only that a man who watches so diligently must have a master with deep pockets,” I said, slipping a sovereign from my glove and letting it glint in the gaslight. “And a master like that might pay well to know if his interests are being served.”

The sailor’s eyes flickered from my face to the coin and back again. He was a mercenary; I had judged as much.

“My master pays me to watch,” he grunted. “That is all.”

“A watchman is a poor trade,” I countered. “A guide is a better one. My employer is a man of business. He has heard that a certain Mr. Croft, lately of Ceylon, has interest in a shipping venture out of this port. A venture involving tea. My employer wishes to meet with him.”

The mention of Croft’s name and the tea trade struck home. The lascar’s sullenness vanished, replaced by a shrewd, avaricious glint.

“Ah,” he breathed. “The sahib Croft. He is a man of great affairs. He is not in Liverpool now. He is at the ‘Sea Serpent’ in Bootle. He waits for a ship. But he is angry. He waits for money that is owed to him. A great deal of money. From a man who was his partner.”

“Penhaligon?” I asked, keeping my voice even.

The lascar spat on the pavement. “That is the name. The sahib Croft says he will have what is his, or he will have the man’s blood. He is not a patient man.”

He took the sovereign from my hand and melted back into the fog-shrouded street, leaving me with the solution to the mystery, and a new problem. Croft was not merely a spectre from the past; he was a creditor, and a dangerous one at that. The secret of the partnership’s end was almost certainly a financial one—a debt, a theft, some act of betrayal that Penhaligon would rather face a gunman than confess.

I returned to my lodgings and wrote a short, urgent note to Mr. Penhaligon, requesting he call upon me at his earliest convenience. I did not state the reason, but I knew the man’s anxiety would bring him.

He arrived within the hour, his face pale and drawn. “Well, sir? Have you news?”

“I have,” I said. “Josiah Croft is alive, he is in Liverpool, and he is here to collect a debt.”

The blood drained from Penhaligon’s face. He sank into the chair he had occupied two nights before, but now he looked a decade older. The fight had gone out of him.

“A debt,” he whispered. “So he knows.”

“He knows enough to have hired a lascar to watch your house and to threaten you with violence. The time for silence is past, Mr. Penhaligon. If you wish for my help in this, you must tell me the whole of it. How did your partnership end?”

He was silent for a long time, staring at his clenched hands. When he finally spoke, his voice was flat, devoid of all emotion.

“The last shipment,” he said. “Before the dissolution. A full chest of the finest silver-tip. It was to be the making of us. But it never reached Colombo. There was a storm, they said. The ship was lost. I received the insurance money. Croft… Croft was in the hills when the news came. He never knew about the insurance. He believed the venture was a total loss, that we were ruined. We quarrelled. He accused me of mismanagement. I let him believe it. I took the money and I came home. I told myself he had got his share in the estate, that this was my portion. I heard he died, and I thanked God for it. I have lived with the guilt of it for seven years, Mr. Vance. It has been a heavier cargo than any chest of tea.”

He looked up at me, his eyes filled with a profound and weary shame.

“He was not a good man, Croft. He was cruel to the coolies and harsh in his dealings. But he was my partner. And I cheated him.”

The case was laid bare. It was not one of murder or dark conspiracy, but of a cowardly act of greed and the long, slow poison of a guilty conscience. Croft had survived, and now he had come to claim his due, not through the law, but with the threat of the jungle and the dockside.

“You have made a grave error, Mr. Penhaligon,” I said. “But it is not yet irreparable. Croft wants his money. He does not want a scandal or a trial that would see him hanged for threatening your life. We shall arrange a meeting. We shall pay him what he is owed, with interest. And then you will be free of him, and of your past.”

Penhaligon nodded slowly, a flicker of hope in his eyes. “Whatever it costs, Mr. Vance. Whatever it costs.”

Outside, the rain had ceased. A cold, clean wind was blowing in from the Mersey, clearing the air. It seemed a fitting omen. The fog of the past was beginning to lift, and though the road ahead would be costly and difficult, it was, at last, a clear one.
