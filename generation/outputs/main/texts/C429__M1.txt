The fever had taken him on the train, somewhere between Milan and the border. He had disembarked at a small, unnamed station, a mere platform of splintered wood clinging to the side of a mountain, and collapsed into the grey, waiting snow. The next thing Dmitri knew, he was in a bed of straw and rough wool, the air thick with the smell of woodsmoke, goat’s milk, and something else he couldn't place—the clean, cold scent of stone and ice.

A woman’s face swam into view above him. It was a broad, ruddy face, framed by a simple linen cap. Her eyes were the colour of a winter sky, and they held no fear, only a calm, assessing concern. She placed a cool, calloused hand on his forehead. Her touch was so direct, so devoid of artifice, that he flinched. In his world, every touch was a transaction, a test, a prelude to a question or a blow.

He was in a low-ceilinged room, its walls made of dark, ancient timber. The only light came from a single window and the fire in a stone hearth. A man, older and more weathered than the woman, sat on a stool, whittling a piece of wood. He was a mountain, solid and immovable. He nodded at Dmitri. “You are awake. Good.”

His name, Dmitri learned, was Klaus. The woman was Elsbeth. They were farmers, shepherds. This was their home, high in the Valais, a place of rock and snow and stubborn life. He told them a lie, a simple one he had rehearsed. A Swiss name, a story of being a travelling bookkeeper, robbed and left for dead. It was a flimsy shield, but they accepted it without question. They asked for no papers, no proof. They simply brought him broth and saw to his needs.

The days bled into one another, marked by the slow turning of the light on the opposite peak and the rhythm of their lives. Klaus would leave before dawn to tend to his flock, returning with the scent of the high pastures clinging to his coat. Elsbeth would move about the cottage with a quiet efficiency, her hands always busy—kneading bread, mending clothes, stirring a pot over the fire. Their daughter, a girl of perhaps ten named Lena, would watch him from the doorway with wide, curious eyes.

They were decent people. It was a simple, uncomplicated decency, as natural to them as breathing. They shared their meagre food without a second thought. When his cough worsened, Elsbeth brewed a bitter tea from herbs she’d gathered on the slopes. She would sit with him for hours, not speaking, but simply being present, a quiet, reassuring force against the chill of his illness and the deeper chill in his soul. Her presence was a balm, but it was also an irritant. It scraped against the hardened calluses of his convictions.

He had been taught to see the world as a machine of oppression. The peasants were its cogs, ground down by landlords, priests, and the Tsar’s ministers. They were the lumpenproletariat, their consciousness clouded by superstition and fatigue, waiting for a vanguard like him to awaken them. He had seen their misery in the tenements of St. Petersburg, in the frozen fields of the Ukraine. He had fueled his own righteous anger with their suffering.

But this family’s poverty was not the same. It was clean. Their small home was tidy, their few possessions well-kept. They were not broken. They were worn, like the stones of their cottage, but their spirit was unbroken. They possessed a quiet pride in their work, in their family, in their simple, unadorned faith. Klaus would sometimes read from a large, leather-bound Bible, his lips moving slowly over the words. It was not the opulent, gilded book of a state bishop, but a plain, honest text. Dmitri, who had railed against the church as a tool of the state, found he could not summon the same venom for this quiet ritual. It seemed less like a chain and more like an anchor.

One evening, a blizzard raged outside, the wind howling like a hungry wolf. The fire crackled, casting long, dancing shadows. Lena, the girl, had been struggling with a piece of embroidery. Her small face was a mask of frustration. Elsbeth knelt beside her, took the needle from her hand, and patiently showed her the correct stitch. Her fingers, so capable and strong, moved with a surprising gentleness. She guided the girl’s hand, her voice a low murmur of encouragement. “Slowly, little one. See? Like this. Patience.”

Dmitri watched them from his cot, the firelight warm on his face. He thought of his mission. He was in Switzerland to kill a man. A minister of the Russian crown, on holiday, a man whose signature had authorized the brutal suppression of a strike in Odessa. A man who was, in the abstract, a monster. The act was to be a revolutionary statement, a blow for the workers, for the future. He had envisioned it with cold, detached clarity: the press of the pistol, the crack of the shot, the man’s life extinguished like a candle flame. A necessary act to tear down the rotten edifice of the old world.

But as he watched Elsbeth’s hands guiding her daughter’s, a terrible, corrosive doubt began to eat at him. This act of killing, this clean, abstract violence—it had no place in this room. It did not belong here, in the warmth and quiet of this family. How could he reconcile the image of a man’s blood on the snow with the simple goodness of this woman’s hands? He was sent to liberate the masses, but he was being undone by the decency of one small family. Their simple humanity was a more potent argument than any pamphlet he had ever read. It suggested that the world he wanted to build, a world of iron and ideology, might have no room for them, for their quiet kindness, for their stubborn, peaceful faith.

The fever broke a week later, leaving him weak but clear-headed. The time had come to leave. He knew the minister’s itinerary. He had to be in Zermatt by the end of the month.

On his last morning, he stood by the door, his worn coat buttoned against the cold. He had no money to give them. To offer payment would be an insult. It would cheapen the gift they had given him, a gift he could not name and could not repay. Klaus shook his hand, his grip firm and final. Elsbeth gave him a small, cloth-wrapped bundle. Bread and cheese for the road. She looked at him, her winter-sky eyes seeing more than he was comfortable with.

“Be careful on the mountain paths,” she said. “They can be treacherous.”

He nodded, unable to speak. He walked out of the cottage and into the brilliant, blinding sunlight. The air was sharp and clean in his lungs. The world of rock and snow stretched out before him, immense and indifferent. He started down the path, his destination clear in his mind. But with every step away from that small cottage, he felt the weight of what he carried—his mission, his revolver, his purpose—grow heavier. He had come to this country to commit an act of clarity, to make a simple, brutal statement. But he was leaving the mountains a divided man, the simple decency of a Swiss peasant family lodged in his heart like a splinter of ice that refused to melt.
