The garden party at Marchmont House was, by general consent, the most exhausting triumph of the season. Lady Ariadne Ffolliott had arranged the afternoon with that meticulous ruthlessness which her friends called taste and her enemies called strategy: the lawns shaved to velvet, the roses coaxed into a fever of bloom, and a string quartet concealed among the lilacs to sigh out something Italianate whenever the conversation flagged. Beneath the striped awning the women glittered like a case of jewels, and the men, in their grey frock-coats, wore the expression of persons who had dressed with care and were resolved to be amused at any cost.

It was Lady Ariadne herself who discovered the loss, and she discovered it in the manner of one announcing a death. She had been seated upon the little rustic bench beside the rose-arch, engaged in the delightful occupation of being flattered by Colonel ffrench-Molyneux, when her hand went to her waist, and then to the other side of her waist, and then, with a movement of terrible precision, to the pocket that was not there.

“My diary,” she said. Her voice carried the length of the lawn. “My morocco diary. It is gone.”

The effect was immediate and extraordinary. Mrs. Bellamy, who had been laughing a shade too brightly at the Bishop’s anecdote, stopped laughing as though a hand had closed upon her throat. Mr. Cottenham, who had been murmuring to the youngest Miss Devereux something about her eyes, turned the colour of the claret he had not yet been given. Even the Bishop, a man of the world in gaiters, permitted his smile to curdle at the corners. For it was known—it was terribly, universally known—that Lady Ariadne kept a diary, and that in this diary she entered, with the fidelity of a recording angel and the pen of a hanging judge, every compliment she had ever received.

“Every compliment?” a stranger had once asked, and Lady Ariadne had closed her fan with a snap. “Every compliment, Mr. Vane. A woman who does not keep accounts will soon find herself bankrupt.”

The search began. It was conducted with the frenzy of a treasure-hunt and the stealth of a conspiracy. Ladies lifted cushions with the air of women defusing a bomb; gentlemen peered beneath the tea-table and exchanged glances in which terror and speculation were exquisitely mingled. For who among them had not, at some hour of weakness or gallantry, said something to Lady Ariadne? And who could say what Lady Ariadne had written down?

“She will have transcribed it,” whispered Mrs. Bellamy to Lady Maud Ashby, behind a screen of hydrangea. “She transcribes everything. She has the memory of an elephant and the malice of a woman who has never once been contradicted.”

“I only said her singing reminded me of a lark at daybreak,” said Lady Maud, in the voice of one confessing to murder.

“I,” said Mrs. Bellamy, “told her that her profile belonged in a gallery. And I added—God forgive me—that the gallery would have to be built expressly.”

Meanwhile the young man who had found the diary was standing beneath the cedar, reading it.

His name was Hilary Marchbanks, and he was the only person present who had never paid Lady Ariadne a compliment. This was not from any deficiency of manners; he was, in fact, a young man of considerable polish, with a face that suggested intelligence and a fortune that suggested patience. He had simply never found anything to say to Lady Ariadne that was not true, and the truth, where she was concerned, had a way of being impolite. She had noticed the omission, of course. She had noticed it the way a general notices an unfortified hill, and she had been civil to him all afternoon with the special civility of a woman who intends, at the proper moment, to be devastating.

The diary was bound in crimson morocco and clasped in gold, and it opened, as such books do, at the most dangerous page. Mr. Marchbanks read:

*“Mr. Cottenham: that my laugh was the only music he could imagine in heaven. (Note: he had taken three glasses of champagne and was looking at my throat at the time.)”*

He turned the page.

*“Colonel ffrench-Molyneux: that my eyes were two blue stars fallen into a rose-garden. (Note: the Colonel is sixty-four and has said the same to every woman in Surrey.)”*

He turned again.

*“The Bishop: that my virtue was a sermon and my wit a psalm. (Note: the Bishop wishes to be invited to dinner.)”*

Mr. Marchbanks smiled, and there was in the smile something that was not quite kindness and not quite cruelty—the expression of a man who has discovered that the world is even more absurd than he had hoped. He closed the book and looked across the lawn at Lady Ariadne, who stood among her guests like a queen among prisoners, her hand pressed to her heart, her eyes bright with the righteous fury of the robbed.

“I have found it,” he said.

The silence that fell was the silence of the last trump. Every guest became, in that instant, a statue of his own guilt. The Bishop set down his teacup with the care of a man laying down a revolver. Mr. Cottenham looked at the cedar as though it might open and receive him. Mrs. Bellamy’s fan ceased its motion, and her face assumed the fixed and glassy serenity of the very much frightened.

Lady Ariadne advanced upon Mr. Marchbanks with the tread of a tragedy queen. “You have read it,” she said. It was not a question.

“I have,” said Mr. Marchbanks.

“Every page?”

“Every page. It is a remarkable document. It has the unity of a work of art and the moral of a sermon. I confess I was particularly struck by the entry concerning the Bishop.”

The Bishop made a small sound, like a man who has been struck in the stomach by a football.

“But,” Mr. Marchbanks continued, turning the diary over in his hands, “I find that it has one defect. It is incomplete.”

Lady Ariadne’s eyes narrowed. “Incomplete?”

“There is no entry,” said the young man, “concerning myself.”

The guests stared. Lady Ariadne stared. The string quartet, having reached the end of its piece, began another, and the notes fell like water into the silence.

“I have never paid you a compliment,” said Mr. Marchbanks, “and so I am the only man here who has nothing to fear from your book. It is a curious position. It gives me, I think, a certain authority. I shall return the diary on one condition—that you permit me to supply the missing entry.”

Lady Ariadne’s lips parted. She was a woman who could not resist a scene, and here was a scene being offered to her upon a silver salver.

“Very well,” she said. “What is it?”

Mr. Marchbanks opened the diary at the last page, and taking a pencil from his pocket—for he was a young man who believed in being prepared for emergencies—he wrote, slowly and with great legibility:

*“Mr. Marchbanks: nothing. (Note: he was the only man who did not try to buy my good opinion, and so he is the only man whose opinion I would like to have.)”*

He closed the book and handed it to her. Lady Ariadne read the words in silence. A flush, which was not the flush of anger, rose slowly to her cheeks. She looked at him for a long moment, and then she laughed—a real laugh, not the little silver one she kept for company.

“You are an impertinent young man,” she said, “and I am not at all sure that I like you.”

“That,” said Mr. Marchbanks, bowing, “is the first honest thing anyone has said to you all afternoon. I shall write it in my own diary tonight.”

And he walked away across the lawn, past the Bishop, who was beginning to breathe again, and past Mrs. Bellamy, who had already resolved to say something very cutting about him at the earliest opportunity, and past the roses, which continued to bloom with the beautiful indifference of flowers who have never been flattered and never will be. Lady Ariadne watched him go, and then she opened her diary once more and read the entry a second time, and smiled—a smile that was not for the guests, nor for the Colonel, nor for the Bishop, but for herself alone, and for the curious and delightful discovery that she had, after all, been paid a compliment worth recording.
