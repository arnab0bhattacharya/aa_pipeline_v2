The letter from Mr. Cranmer’s man of business lay on the library table, its every line a fresh indictment. The figures were immovable, a landscape of ruin more stark than any the Derbyshire peaks could offer. Rowsley Hall, with its grey stone and its draughty corridors, its parklands and its tenant farms, was a magnificent, decaying carcass, and Arthur Cranmer, its new master, was the fly trapped within. The estate was his, and with it a debt of forty thousand pounds, a sum so vast it seemed to have a physical weight, pressing the very air from the room.

He had two choices, both distasteful. He could sell, auctioning off the history of his family to the highest bidder, a coarse-grained man from the city who would strip the woods for timber and turn the old hall into a lunatic asylum. Or he could marry. Not for love, for what had love to do with the price of wheat and the repair of a thousand yards of dry-stone walling? He must marry money.

The object of his necessary pursuit was Miss Honoria Blackwood. Her father, Josiah Blackwood, was a manufacturer of cotton-spinning machinery in the nearby town of Chesterfield. His fortune was new, loud, and substantial, built on iron and steam and the tireless turning of spindles. Miss Blackwood was his only child, and her dowry was rumoured to be the very sum that would silence Mr. Cranmer’s creditors for a generation.

He had met her twice. The first time, at a county assembly, she had been a vision in a gown of deep blue silk, her hair a shade of rich chestnut, her manner one of quiet self-possession. She was not handsome in the delicate, porcelain way of the ladies he knew, but there was a vitality to her, a directness in her dark eyes that he found both compelling and unnerving. The second time, he had engineered a call at the Blackwood house in Chesterfield, a modern, red-brick edifice that stood in stark contrast to the mellow stone of his own ancestral home. The visit had been a study in polite formality, Mr. Blackwood booming about the price of raw cotton while Mrs. Blackwood, a woman of formidable silks and diamonds, had assessed him with the cool appraisal of a woman judging a horse at a fair.

Today, he was to ride over to Rowsley Hall itself. Miss Blackwood, her father had written, had expressed a desire to see the famous old house and its park. It was an opportunity, Cranmer knew, and he had spent the morning instructing his few remaining servants to make the place look less like a monument to neglect and more like a home. They had failed. As he walked his horse up the weed-choked drive, the house seemed to stare back at him with hollow, accusing windows.

He found her not in the house, but in the walled garden. She was standing by the old sundial, her gloved hand tracing the mossy engravings. She wore a simple pelisse of dark green velvet, and a small, feathered bonnet. She turned as he approached, and there was no coy start of surprise, only a calm acknowledgement.

‘Mr. Cranmer,’ she said. ‘Your letter said I might find you here. I confess, I wished to see the garden. My father’s works have no such quiet.’

‘It has been quieter of late than I would like,’ he replied, the words tasting of ash. ‘The garden, like the house, has suffered from a want of attention.’

‘So I see,’ she said, her gaze sweeping over the overgrown rose beds and the ivy that was slowly strangling the ancient apple trees. ‘It is a beautiful spot, though. It has a soul, your garden. One cannot say that of a factory.’

He offered her his arm, and they walked slowly along the gravel path. He spoke of the house’s history, of the Cranmers who had fought for the Stuarts and the one who had been a friend to the great Mr. Locke. She listened attentively, her questions intelligent and pointed. She asked about the tenant farms, about the yield of the land, about the state of the roofs. He found himself answering with a candour that surprised him, the usual evasions of a proud man crumbling under her steady, practical gaze.

‘And the woods?’ she asked, gesturing to the dark expanse of oak and ash that bordered the park. ‘They are extensive. My father says good timber is a fortune in itself.’

‘They are entailed,’ he said, a little too quickly. ‘They must remain.’

She stopped and turned to face him, her expression unreadable. ‘Mr. Cranmer, we are both of us practical people, I think. Let us not pretend that this is a morning call of pure sociability. You are not the first gentleman to look upon my father’s fortune as a means to mend his own. You will not be the last.’

The bluntness of it struck him dumb. He felt the heat rise in his neck, a flush of shame and anger. ‘Miss Blackwood, I—’

‘Please,’ she said, holding up a hand. ‘I am not offended. I am, in fact, relieved. I find the usual prevarications exhausting. You need money. I have it. The question is not whether you require it, but what you are prepared to offer in return.’

He stared at her, his carefully prepared speeches turning to dust in his mouth. ‘I offer you my name, my home… a position in society.’

‘A name that is mortgaged to the hilt, a home that is falling down about its master’s ears, and a position that would make me the object of pity and gossip from here to London.’ She said it without malice, merely as a statement of fact. ‘That is not a very compelling bargain, Mr. Cranmer. You must understand that my father’s money is not merely a pile of guineas. It is a living thing. It is invested in mills, in machines, in the labour of a thousand hands. It is a responsibility. I will not see it poured into a bottomless well of aristocratic pride without a return.’

He felt a strange, unwilling admiration for her. She was not the simpering miss he had expected, but a woman of formidable intelligence, a true daughter of her father’s world. ‘What terms, then,’ he asked, his voice low, ‘would you propose?’

She began to walk again, her pace measured. ‘First, the debts will be settled by my father, but the estate will be placed in a trust. I will be a trustee. No sale of land, no felling of timber, no expenditure above a hundred pounds will be made without my consent. We will run Rowsley Hall as a business, because that is what it must become to survive. The home farm will be improved, the tenant farms put in order, the woods managed properly. It can be made to pay, Mr. Cranmer. It can be made prosperous. But not by clinging to the past. It must be dragged, kicking and screaming if need be, into this new century.’

He stopped, his mind reeling. She was not merely asking for a husband; she was demanding a partner, a steward for her capital. She was asking him to surrender the very thing he was trying to save: his authority, his pride, the last vestiges of a gentleman’s absolute rule over his own domain.

‘You would make me a pensioner in my own house,’ he said, the words rough with bitterness.

‘No,’ she said, and for the first time, a hint of warmth entered her voice. ‘I would make you a partner. I have no wish to be mistress of a ruin, Mr. Cranmer, nor wife to a man who is a slave to his own past. I have seen what my father has built from nothing. He has made something useful, something that provides for hundreds of families. Can you say the same for this?’ She gestured to the great, silent house. ‘It is beautiful, but it is a beautiful ghost. I have no wish to marry a ghost. I will marry a man who is willing to build something with me.’

They stood in the shadow of the hall, the silence stretching between them. The sun was beginning its slow descent, casting long shadows across the lawn. Arthur Cranmer looked at the house, his inheritance, his burden. He saw the crumbling masonry, the blank windows, the weight of centuries. Then he looked at the woman standing before him, her chin lifted, her eyes clear and unwavering. She was offering him not rescue, but a new kind of servitude. And yet, for the first time since he had inherited his ruin, he felt a flicker of something that was not despair. It was the faint, unfamiliar spark of hope. He had wanted to marry money. He had not expected to have to earn it.
