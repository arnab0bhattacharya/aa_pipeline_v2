I remember how singularly unremarkable the weather had been on the Tuesday. I had walked to the station in a mild drizzle, carrying my umbrella more out of habit than necessity, and spent the day at the office in Molesey tabulating industrial claims with a perfectly undisturbed mind. It was the very picture of suburban routine. Yet when I woke on the Wednesday morning, it was to a profound and unaccountable silence.

I lay in bed for some moments, listening. The clock on the mantelpiece ticked, but the familiar rumble of the early train from Weybridge was entirely absent. I went to the window and drew back the curtains. The lower garden was gone. In its place stretched a sluggish, yellowish expanse of water, swirling with eddies, which had swallowed the fence and submerged the summer-house to its roof. The Thames had risen overnight with a quiet, devastating purpose, and the world I knew had ceased to function.

I dressed and went downstairs. The kitchen floor was damp, and a foul, muddy smell pervaded the house. I put on my rubber boots and stepped out of the front door, only to find the water lapping at the very threshold of Marlowe Terrace. A small group of my neighbours had gathered on the higher ground by the church, staring at the flood with a sort of stupefied irritation. Mr. Prout, who kept the draper’s shop, was there in a heavy ulster, arguing with our local councillor, Mr. Voss.

“The pipes must have burst,” Mr. Voss was saying, tapping his walking stick against his boot. “The drainage board is quite aware. They are sending men from Kingston this afternoon.”

“It’s the river, you fool,” Mr. Prout interrupted, with a nervous, high-pitched laugh. “The river’s broken its banks. I’ve seen it in Chester, years ago. It’ll go on rising.”

I asked them about the trains. None had run since midnight. A clerk from the station had waded up the hill at dawn to say the lines were under three feet of water at the embankment. There was no telegraph, and the police constable had gone to fetch the inspector, who lived on the other side of the valley. We were, for all practical purposes, cut off.

By midday the water had crept up the steps of the terrace. I spent the morning moving my heavier furniture to the upper floors, a tedious and exhausting business for a man of my sedentary habits. From the landing window I could look out over a vast, spreading lake. The willows by the railway embankment were mere bushes in the flood, and the gasworks beyond stood up out of the water like a dreary island. Here and there a punt or a swamped boat drifted by, carrying loads of sodden furniture. It was extraordinary how quickly the orderly fabric of things gave way. By noon, the gas pressure had dropped to nothing, and the lamps would not light. We were reduced to the primitive state of waiting in the damp and the cold, entirely dependent on rumour.

The first real panic set in on Thursday morning. The water had not receded; it had climbed steadily through the darkness, and when daylight came, the ground floors of all the houses in the lower street were submerged. I had spent a wretched night in an armchair in my bedroom, listening to the lapping of the water against the bricks. I was hungry, for my larder had been downstairs, and I had saved nothing but a loaf of bread and a bottle of whisky.

I went out early. A strange, nautical atmosphere had settled over the suburb. People were moving about in rowboats, punts, and even tin baths, paddling clumsily with spades and bits of plank. I hired a punt from a boy for half a crown and poled myself down towards the High Street. The scene was one of absurd and dismal ruin. The grocer’s shop was a wreck, its windows smashed, and tins of condensed milk floating out in a steady procession. A piano, upside down, bumped against the butcher’s door. Yet the people I saw did not seem to be in terror; they were exasperated, bewildered, and oddly argumentative.

I found Mr. Voss standing on a table outside the public house, which stood on a slight rise. He was making a speech to a knot of dishevelled men. He was saying that the authorities in London had been informed, that a relief train would be sent, that there was no need for alarm. But a man in a boater was shouting at him.

“What authorities?” the man cried. “There’s no authorities! The river’s taken the lower bridge. The water’s up to the windows of the Town Hall. We’re on our own, I tell you!”

I poled closer and asked a woman who was leaning out of a window if she had any bread. She looked at me with a pale, drawn face, and said she had not eaten since yesterday. Her husband, she said, had gone to look for a boat, and had not returned. I gave her half my loaf, and she wept. It is a curious thing, how the spectacle of another person’s distress clarifies one’s own position. I had been thinking of my office, of the claims that would go unprocessed, of my manager’s certain displeasure. Now I began to think of the water itself, of its quiet, implacable advance. It was not an administrative inconvenience. It was a force, vast and indifferent, and it was taking possession of our world.

By Friday the situation had become a matter of simple survival. The sky was still a uniform grey, and the rain fell in a steady, miserable drizzle. I had taken refuge with a dozen others in the upper rooms of the church. We had a fire of broken pews, and a meagre supply of food, begged from the few shops that still had dry stock. The talk was all of the river. Some said it had risen twenty feet; others said the whole valley was submerged as far as Staines. No one knew. We were like people on a raft, surrounded by a featureless sea.

I sat by the window in the afternoon, looking out at the drowned streets. The water was very still, and the houses were reflected in it, so that they seemed to stretch downwards into a dark, quiet underworld. It was a scene of complete and overwhelming desolation, and yet I could not feel the terror I thought I ought to feel. There was something in the vastness of the flood, in its quietness, that overawed the imagination. The little world of Marlowe Terrace, with its mortgages and its insurance policies and its petty rivalries, was gone, sunk under the yellow water. We had lived in it as though it were a permanent thing, a solid foundation for our lives. And now it was gone, and we were a handful of cold, hungry people, sitting in a church, waiting for the river to fall.

I thought of my office, of the dusty files and the gas lamps and the rattle of the trains. It seemed infinitely remote, a fragment of a dream. The only reality was the water, and the grey sky above it. I wondered if the river would ever go back within its banks, if the trains would ever run again, if the world would ever be the same. I did not believe it would. The flood had shown us how thin was the crust of our civilisation, how easily the waters of nature could rise and swallow it. We had thought ourselves secure, and we were not. We were at the mercy of the river, and the river did not care.
