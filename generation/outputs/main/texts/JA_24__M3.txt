Mrs. Bertram was a woman of very considerable accomplishments, all of which were directed towards a single object: the establishment of her daughters in life. Her drawing-room in Bath had long been the theatre of her hopes, but as those hopes had, season after season, met with nothing more substantial than the polite attentions of impoverished clergymen, she had at length determined to try the influence of a Sussex seaside resort. Seldom had any place enjoyed so sudden or so decided a reputation as Brighthaven; and Mrs. Bertram, having received the most unimpeachable intelligence that Lord Sandcroft was staying at the Royal Hotel with his family, ordered her carriage within the hour.

The young ladies, Maria and Clara, were placed under the strictest injunctions of elegance. Maria, who was four-and-twenty and had been "out" for seven years without acquiring either a husband or a permanent complexion, was to be the particular object of attention; Clara, at nineteen, was still in a state of tolerable bloom, and might serve as a foil. Their mother's instructions on the journey were so copious, so minute, and so entirely directed towards the proper display of their sentiments before a young nobleman, that neither sister could form the least notion of what was actually expected of her beyond a general injunction to look agreeable and say nothing that might betray an excess of understanding.

On the very first evening of their arrival, Mrs. Bertram descended to the assembly rooms with a degree of expectation which almost supplied the place of beauty. The rooms were tolerably full, the music playing, and the scent of sea-air mingling with that of candles and orange-flower water. She had not been seated many minutes before her friend Mrs. Fortescue—a widow of excellent connexions and very slender fortune—hastened to her side with the intelligence that Lord Sandcroft had been observed walking on the parade that very morning.

"He is quite young, I believe," said Mrs. Fortescue, lowering her voice, "not yet eighteen; but he has come down with his mother, Lady Sandcroft, and a very large establishment."

"A large establishment! How excessively proper," replied Mrs. Bertram, who immediately began to arrange her features into an expression of elegant indifference, the better to conceal the very reverse. "And is he tall?"

"Tall for his age, I am told—though I confess I did not remark him particularly. He was walking with his governess, I think."

"His governess!" repeated Mrs. Bertram, somewhat discomposed. A young lord of seventeen, attended by a governess, was not precisely the figure of manly independence she had painted in her fancy. She had imagined a youth of fashionable address, perhaps just down from Oxford, with an estate in the North and a mamma in Yorkshire; she had not calculated upon a boy still under tuition. But she was a woman of too much resolution to be discouraged by a first impression, and she immediately determined that the following morning should be spent upon the parade, where, if report spoke true, his lordship took his daily exercise.

The morning was bright, the sea was calm, and Mrs. Bertram, with her daughters on either arm, walked the parade with an air of composure that did infinite credit to her nerves. They had not proceeded far before she perceived a small party a little ahead, consisting of a lady of dignified appearance, a young person in a plain pelisse, and a boy—a boy so visibly young, so entirely destitute of any pretension to manhood, that Mrs. Bertram's heart sank within her, and she was obliged to press Clara's arm with a degree of force wholly inconsistent with maternal tenderness.

He was pale, slight, and rather short; his hair was arranged with a precision that suggested the hand of his governess rather than the art of a London barber; and he carried in his hand a small volume, which Clara, whose eyes were quicker than her mother's, presently discovered to be a Latin grammar. A lord, in a Latin grammar, on a seaside parade! Mrs. Bertram felt that her campaign had opened most inauspiciously.

Yet the thing must be gone through. Lady Sandcroft was known to Mrs. Bertram by name, and a slight previous acquaintance, of the sort that exists between persons who have once been at the same dinner-party in London, could not be suffered to pass unimproved. She therefore advanced, with her most engaging smile, and the meeting passed off with all the civility that two women, each equally determined to be pleased and equally uncertain whether they ought to be, could bestow.

Lord Sandcroft, on being presented to the Miss Bertrams, coloured to the roots of his hair, made a bow which was rather more of a stumble, and said nothing. His governess, a Miss Grange, who seemed to be a person of good sense and quiet manners, gently prompted him to say he was happy, which he did with an emphasis that rendered the sentiment almost ludicrous. Maria, who had been instructed to look agreeable, looked only bewildered; while Clara, less trammelled by expectation, found herself regarding his lordship with an expression of such undisguised amusement that Mrs. Bertram was seized with a secret horror of her youngest daughter's countenance.

The party walked on together for some distance, and the conversation was sustained chiefly by the two elder ladies. Lady Sandcroft was sensible, and not ill-inclined; but she had the look of a woman who had been disappointed in her son's progress, and who was anxious to keep him from the contamination of too much society. Miss Grange contributed occasionally a remark of such justness as to make Mrs. Bertram wonder why a woman of her abilities was employed in the instruction of a boy who appeared to have no aptitude for learning anything at all.

The real difficulty, however, was not the meeting itself, but the concealment of what had passed in Mrs. Bertram's mind during it. All Brighthaven must know that she had made the acquaintance of Lord Sandcroft; all Brighthaven must not know that she wished she had not. She was to speak of his lordship with that species of cheerful approbation which implies that one has no particular interest in the subject, and she was to ensure that Maria and Clara did the same. Clara, she foresaw, would be the chief difficulty; for Clara had a way of saying things which, though not absolutely improper, had a tendency to convey more than was intended.

On returning to their lodgings, Mrs. Bertram assembled her daughters in the sitting-room and delivered her sentiments with great energy.

"I need not tell you," she said, "that Lord Sandcroft is very young."

"No, Mamma," said Maria.

"He is not what I had been led to expect—but that is of no consequence. He will be of age in a few years, and will then, in all probability, be a very different person."

"Do you think he will be taller, Mamma?" asked Clara.

Mrs. Bertram was vexed, but she controlled herself. "He may be taller, Clara, and he will certainly be older. In the meantime, I desire that you will not—do you hear me, Clara?—you will not make any remarks, either in public or in private, upon his lordship's appearance, his age, or his Latin grammar."

Clara, who had not intended to be satirical, was silenced; and Maria, who never intended anything in particular, looked as if she had received a confidence. Mrs. Bertram then proceeded to dress for the evening's assembly with a determination to be satisfied, which, if it did not amount to contentment, was at least a very useful substitute.

At the assembly, Lord Sandcroft was persuaded to stand up for a country-dance with Maria. He went through his figure with the air of one performing an exercise prescribed by his governess; he trod on Maria's gown twice, and apologised with a politeness so elaborate that it occupied the whole of the next figure. Maria, who was not wanting in patience, bore it with exemplary composure; but she was afterwards heard to observe, in the privacy of their chamber, that she did not think a man could be considered a dancer until he had at least ceased to tread upon his partner.

Mrs. Bertram was a woman of great practical ability, and she saw, with the quickness of a general whose forces are inadequate, that the only course was to make the best of the matter before the world, and to make the best of the matter before herself, as soon as might be convenient. She therefore spoke of Lord Sandcroft at every visiting-circle in Brighthaven as "a charming boy, with a great deal of promise," and contrived so to manage her emphasis that no one could be quite certain whether she was patronising a nobleman or recommending a pupil.

Lady Sandcroft, who was herself a woman of penetration, soon perceived that Mrs. Bertram's attentions had undergone a very material cooling, and drew her own conclusions with a degree of good sense that did her credit. She was too well-bred to be offended, and too prudent to encourage; and she very shortly afterwards found a reason for removing to a different lodging on the opposite side of the town, where the sea-air was said to be more favourable for her son's studies.

Mrs. Bertram received the intelligence of their departure with a countenance expressive of every proper sentiment, and not one improper one. She called upon Lady Sandcroft with a card, which was returned with equal civility; and the acquaintance was concluded in a manner which left nothing to be desired, except the object for which it had been begun. Maria, on being privately questioned by her mother as to her feelings, replied with such perfect indifference that Mrs. Bertram was divided between vexation at her daughter's insensibility and relief at her own escape.

They remained at Brighthaven a fortnight longer, during which time Clara was observed to laugh more than once without any visible cause; and Mrs. Bertram, finding the sea-air agreeable, and the lodgings not inconvenient, very wisely determined to return to Bath, where, at least, the young men were older.
