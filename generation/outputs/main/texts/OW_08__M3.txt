It is a curious circumstance that while Americans are continually seeking to purchase our history, they are never quite willing to pay the price of our climate. Nevertheless, when Miss Cora Van Derpool, a young woman from Chicago whose father had grown fabulously wealthy by some obscure process connected with the refrigeration of dead pigs, arrived in London during the height of the Season, she carried with her a determination so fixed and a wardrobe so magnificent that even the most exclusive drawing-rooms in Mayfair were compelled to yield. Miss Van Derpool’s gowns were a perfect symphony of silks and velvets, and she wore diamonds in the morning with a simple earnestness that completely subdued the English aristocracy, who, as a rule, reserve their jewels for the evening and their debts for the morning.

She had come to London with the distinct intention of marrying a Duke. An Earl, she felt, would be a compromise with destiny, and a Viscount a mere misfortune. Her cousin, the Honourable Freddie Arbuthnot, a young man whose sole claim to social prominence was an unpaid tailor’s bill and a remarkably accurate memory for scandal, had provided her with a complete list of the available peers.

"There is only one," Freddie had informed her, as they sat in the shaded alcove of the Berkeley, sipping a hock which he had cleverly induced her to pay for. "The Duke of Datchet. He is ninety years old, entirely deaf, and confined to a Bath chair. His temper is uncertain, and his estate is heavily mortgaged. The rest of the dukes, my dear Cora, are either married, insane, or bishops. I believe the bishops are considered ineligible, even by the most ambitious of American heiresses."

"Ninety is quite a reasonable age," Miss Van Derpool had replied, adjusting a remarkably large sapphire upon her finger. "It implies a certain stability. And as for deafness, I am sure I shall find it a great convenience. A husband who cannot hear is under the painful necessity of believing whatever one chooses to write down for him. It is the perfect basis for domestic harmony. I shall marry the Duke of Datchet."

It was precisely this alarming resolution that reached the ears of Lord Marmaduke Thorne, the Duke’s nephew and heir presumptive. Lord Marmaduke was a young man of twenty-eight, possessed of a beautiful profile, an engaging smile, and not the slightest intention of ever earning a living. He existed entirely upon the expectations attached to his uncle’s longevity, a precarious form of sustenance that, it must be admitted, agreed with him wonderfully. The prospect of an American heiress marrying the old Duke, settling his debts, and producing an heir to supersede Marmaduke in the line of succession, was a catastrophe too dreadful to contemplate. Marmaduke determined to avert it by the simple expedient of courting Miss Van Derpool himself.

He presented himself at her hotel suite, bearing a magnificent bouquet of Parma violets and an air of polished melancholy. Miss Van Derpool was reclining on a chintz sofa, attired in a tea-gown of old gold silk that made her look rather like a Byzantine saint, if one can imagine a saint with such a keen appreciation for hard currency.

"I am told," she said, extending a hand that glittered with rings, "that you are the Duke’s heir. I suppose you have come to persuade me against the match."

"On the contrary," said Lord Marmaduke, arranging his violets with an artistic eye, "I have come to persuade you against yourself. To marry a man of ninety is to commit a crime against aesthetics. It is like placing a magnificent orchid in a cracked and withered earthenware pot. The flower suffers from the association."

"I have always understood," said Miss Van Derpool, "that English aristocrats were remarkably poor judges of flowers."

"We are poor judges of everything except our own convenience," returned Marmaduke, with a frankness that was entirely calculated. "My uncle is a charming relic, I grant you, but he has a passion for hot milk and a deep-seated aversion to spending money. He has not paid a tradesman in full since the Crimean War. His deafness, which you regard as a convenience, is in reality a fortress. No amount of written persuasion will induce him to part with a shilling. You would be a Duchess, certainly, but you would be a Duchess without petticoats, for the laundress would refuse to wash them."

Miss Van Derpool considered this for a moment. The image of a ducal coronet surmounting an unpaid laundry bill had a certain grotesque charm, but she was a practical young woman. "A title," she observed, "is like a beautiful jewel; its setting may be tarnished, but its intrinsic value remains."

"Ah, but a jewel you may wear in public," Marmaduke rejoined. "A title, in these modern democratic days, is a jewel you may only wear in private, and then only if the butler is not looking. Whereas I," he added, drawing himself up to his full, considerable height, "am a jewel you may wear anywhere. I am young, I am agreeable, and I have a complete set of Byron in my library, which I have never read."

"Your candour," said Miss Van Derpool, "is almost American. But you forget that I am determined to be a Duchess."

"Be a Duchess, then," said Marmaduke, with a sudden, despairing grace, "but do not make me a beggar. If you marry my uncle, I am ruined. I shall have to go into Parliament, or, worse still, into trade. I am entirely unsuited for both. I have a horror of public speaking and a profound ignorance of commerce. I could not tell the difference between a share and a dividend if my life depended upon it."

"That," said Miss Van Derpool, "is a misfortune you share with most of my father’s directors."

Marmaduke, seeing that argument alone would not suffice, resorted to sentiment. He spoke of the twilight, of the transience of youth, and of the deep, unutterable passion that had seized him the moment he beheld her entering the drawing-room at Stafford House. He did not, of course, feel any passion whatsoever, but he described it with such delicate sincerity that he almost believed it himself. He spoke of the stars, comparing her eyes to them, which was a conventional inaccuracy, as her eyes were a rather unromantic grey, and the stars, in London, are largely invisible owing to the smoke.

Miss Van Derpool listened with the attentive calm of a woman who is perfectly aware that she is being deceived, but wishes to hear how the deception will end. When Marmaduke had finished, she was silent for a long time, looking at the violets.

"You are very eloquent, Lord Marmaduke," she said at last. "In America, we should say you were trying to sell us something we did not want. But here, I suppose, it is what you call making love."

"It is what we call proposing," he corrected gently. "And I am proposing, my dear Miss Van Derpool, that you should take a penniless younger son instead of a deaf old Duke. It is an exchange, I admit, but it is one that has its advantages. A husband who can hear is under the painful necessity of agreeing with you, for the sake of peace. A husband who cannot hear is merely an obstruction."

"And what," she asked, a sudden amusement lighting her features, "would you do with an American wife, Lord Marmaduke?"

"I should," he replied, without a moment’s hesitation, "put her on a pedestal, from which she could dictate the fashions, reform the morals of the aristocracy, and spend her father’s money with a lavishness that would make the Prince of Wales green with envy."

Miss Van Derpool laughed. It was a clear, ringing laugh, entirely devoid of self-consciousness. "You are a very foolish young man," she said. "But you are amusing. And I have always found that amusement is the only thing that does not depreciate in value. I will not marry the Duke."

"You will marry me?" cried Marmaduke, scarcely able to conceal his triumph.

"I did not say that," she answered, rising and adjusting her tea-gown with a regal gesture. "I said I would not marry the Duke. I have decided to marry a perfectly obscure Englishman, with no title and no expectations, who owns a small estate in Shropshire and a large library. I met him at the Botanical Gardens yesterday. He was examining a rare species of fern, and he told me that he found society entirely unendurable. A man who prefers ferns to society is a man to be trusted. I shall take him back to Chicago, where ferns are very expensive, and he shall cultivate them in a hothouse, while my father teaches him the refrigeration of pigs."

Lord Marmaduke was left standing in the suite, holding his Parma violets, a monument to the futility of sentiment when deployed against the practical genius of America. He reflected, as he departed, that it was a curious thing that an American woman would prefer a botanist to a Duke, and a still more curious thing that she should prefer ferns to diamonds. But then, as he afterwards remarked to Freddie Arbuthnot, the Americans are a nation of paradoxes, and the only thing one can safely predict about them is that they will never do what is expected of them.
