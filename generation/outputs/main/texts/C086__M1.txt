The career of Arthur Pumble was a litany of elegant failures. He had been a poet whose verse was deemed ‘posthumous in spirit,’ a painter whose canvases were said to ‘offend the very notion of colour,’ and a novelist whose only manuscript was returned by a publisher with a single, devastating note: ‘Why?’ He had tried his hand at architecture, only to produce blueprints for buildings that seemed to lean with a profound, existential weariness. He had even, for a brief and disastrous month, been a violinist in a provincial orchestra, until the conductor, a man with a nervous tic and a passion for Beethoven, had publicly begged him to desist for the good of the composer’s immortal soul. In the parlour of his meagre Bloomsbury lodgings, surrounded by the ghosts of these ambitions, Arthur arrived at his final, and perhaps most sensible, conclusion. He would become a critic.

The logic, he explained to his landlady, was irrefutable. Creation required a divine spark, a gift from the gods. But criticism? Criticism required only a pair of eyes, a vocabulary, and a certain coldness of the heart. It was the art of demolition, and demolition, unlike construction, required no talent, only the willingness to swing a hammer. Having failed at everything that required a soul, he would now make his fortune by proving its absence in others. His first target was to be the annual exhibition at the Marlowe Gallery, a hallowed and predictable institution on Bond Street. He spent the morning there, not looking at the art so much as at the people looking at the art, their faces all wearing the same placid, appreciative mask. It was a mask he intended to rip away.

That evening, by the guttering light of a single candle, Arthur wrote. He did not write a review; he wrote an obituary for an entire movement. He chose as his subject the exhibition’s centrepiece, a vast and sentimental canvas by the celebrated Alistair Finch, titled ‘The Soul of England.’ It depicted a rosy-cheeked milkmaid gazing wistfully at a sunrise, her pail full, her expression one of beatific, idiotic contentment. Arthur’s pen flew across the page, fueled by cheap gin and a lifetime of thwarted ambition. He described Finch’s colours as ‘the jaundiced hues of a fading memory,’ his composition as ‘a testament to the tyranny of symmetry,’ and the milkmaid herself as ‘a creature of such profound vacuity that she seems less a symbol of England than a warning against its water supply.’ He concluded that the painting was ‘not merely bad, but a moral failure, a siren song of mediocrity designed to lull the nation’s aesthetic sense into a permanent, sugary sleep.’ He signed it with a pseudonym he felt captured the spirit of his work: ‘Nero.’

He sent the manuscript to The London Sentinel, a paper known for its stolid, uninspired coverage of the arts. The editor, a perpetually harried man named Abernathy, read it over his breakfast kippers and choked. It was vicious. It was libellous. It was, he realised with a growing sense of horror, utterly brilliant. He published it the next morning in the space normally reserved for shipping forecasts. The effect was instantaneous and seismic. London awoke to a collective gasp. Alistair Finch was said to have fainted upon reading it. The art world erupted in a firestorm of outrage and, to Abernathy’s astonishment, a tidal wave of public interest. The Sentinel sold out by ten o’clock. By noon, people were queuing outside the Marlowe Gallery, desperate to see the painting that ‘Nero’ had so spectacularly vivisected.

Arthur Pumble, in his shabby room, was oblivious to the storm. He was simply waiting for his next failure, expecting a curt rejection from Abernathy. Instead, a boy arrived with a telegram. ‘YOU ARE HIRED STOP WRITE MORE STOP ABERNATHY.’ The world, it seemed, had a desperate hunger for cruelty. His second piece, a takedown of a poet named Julian Croft whose sonnets were likened to ‘sentimental drivel squeezed from a damp sponge,’ cemented his reputation. His third, on a sculptor whose marble nudes were described as ‘an affront to anatomy and an insult to the stone,’ made him a household name. ‘Nero’ was the most feared and, paradoxically, the most sought-after man in London.

The dynamic shifted in a way Arthur could never have predicted. To be praised by Nero became a mark of the highest distinction, but to be insulted by him became a badge of honour, a mark of relevance. An artist who was ignored by Nero was simply an artist who did not matter. Soon, the letters began to arrive at The London Sentinel. They were not letters of protest, but of supplication. A portraitist begged Nero to ‘please, sir, eviscerate my latest work, for it is surely as dull as you say.’ A young playwright sent him a ticket to his new show with a note: ‘I have striven for mediocrity, hoping to attract your gaze. Do not disappoint me.’ Every artist in London, it seemed, wanted to be insulted by Nero. They craved his particular brand of poison as if it were a holy water.

One evening, Arthur was summoned to the private viewing room of the Gilded Cage, the most exclusive gallery in Mayfair. Its owner, the formidable Lady Evangeline Blackwood, was presenting a new collection by a mysterious prodigy known only as ‘Silas.’ She had paid an exorbitant sum to secure Nero’s attendance. Arthur, now dressed in a fine wool suit that felt like a costume, entered the room to find it buzzing with the cream of London society. They parted for him as if he were a plague. Lady Blackwood, a woman with a face like a beautiful, sharp-angled hawk, glided towards him.

‘Nero,’ she said, her voice a low purr. ‘We are so honoured. I trust you will find the work… worthy of your attention.’

Arthur merely nodded, his heart a cold, heavy stone in his chest. He felt nothing. He walked through the gallery, his eyes sweeping over the canvases. The paintings were, to his surprise, genuinely good. They were bold, experimental, and full of a raw, aching life that he recognised, with a pang of something that might have been envy. This Silas had the talent he himself had always lacked. The room fell silent, watching him. They were waiting for the verdict, for the sharp, witty cruelty that would either destroy this new talent or elevate him to the pantheon. He could feel their expectation, their desperate, pathetic need for his judgment.

He stood before the final painting, a swirling vortex of blues and golds that seemed to capture the very essence of a London twilight. It was magnificent. He could feel the words forming, the devastating, brilliant insults that would send this Silas into obscurity and give the public its thrill. He could feel the power, the glorious, easy power of the destroyer. But as he looked at the painting, he saw not a target, but a mirror. He saw the years of his own failure, the ghost of the painter he had wanted to be. The coldness in his heart cracked, and for the first time in years, he felt a sliver of the old, painful hope.

He turned to face the silent, expectant crowd. Lady Blackwood was smiling, a predator’s smile. Abernathy was in the corner, his notebook ready. Arthur Pumble opened his mouth, the words of annihilation poised on his tongue. But what came out was a whisper, barely audible in the hushed room. ‘It is… perfect.’

A collective sigh of disappointment, sharp and immediate, filled the space. Lady Blackwood’s smile vanished. Abernathy looked as if he had been struck. The spell was broken. They did not want praise; they wanted blood. Arthur turned and walked out of the Gilded Cage, leaving behind the murmurs of confusion and the ruin of his career. He walked into the cool London night, the critic who had lost his talent for cruelty, and for the first time in a long time, he did not know what he was going to do next. But he knew, with a terrifying and exhilarating certainty, that he was free.
