It was not noon yet, though the sun, which had been high and hard over the church tower, was beginning to soften at the edges, pouring a thick, buttery light over the paving stones where the pigeons gathered. They were grey, they were purple, they were the colour of oil on water, and they strutted with a kind of nervous dignity, pecking at crumbs that were not there, turning their heads with sharp, mechanical jerks, as if listening to a clock ticking inside the stone. Edith Merton sat on the bench with her knees pressed together and her hands holding the sandwich, which was already going dry in the air. It was a great deal of trouble, she thought, looking at the crust; a great deal of trouble to make the bread rise and to slice the ham and to walk all the way from the office to this square of silence in the middle of the City, where the noise of the lorries was only a hum, like a bee trapped in a jar, and the only real sound was the rustle of the pigeons’ wings and the quiet, shuffling movement of the old men on the benches opposite.

They were there every day, these old men, the caretakers of the past, sitting in the same places with their newspapers folded on their knees and their hats pulled low, and Edith knew their faces as well as she knew the keys of the typewriter. There was the one with the white beard who looked like a patriarch from the Bible, and the one who coughed and spat, and the one who simply stared at the pigeons as if waiting for them to speak. And they looked at her, or rather through her, with eyes that had seen so many lunch hours that they were glazed over with a kind of silver film, like the back of an old mirror. What do they think of me? she wondered, chewing the ham. A girl in a brown coat. A girl with a sandwich. They do not see me at all. I am just a shape in the light, passing through their afternoon, and in a moment I will be gone and the pigeons will take my place on the bench. But I am here, she thought, pressing her thumb into the bread; I am here, and the sun is warm on my hands, and the clock at St. Bride’s is striking, and I am alive, though I do not know quite what that means, except that the sandwich tastes of ham and the stone is hard beneath me.

And then there was the letter. Or rather, not the letter, but the words, which were waiting for her in the office, tucked under the blotter where she had left them, and which had followed her here, dancing in front of the pigeons and the old men. Mr. Arbuthnot. The name itself was heavy, solid, like a block of granite. He had asked her to the theatre. He had stood by the filing cabinet, looking down at his shoes, and he had said, "Will you come?" and she had said, "I don't know," and he had said, "Think about it," and now here she was, thinking about it, in a churchyard, with a sandwich. It was a strange thing, to be asked to sit in the dark with a man and to watch people pretend to be other people. What would they talk about in the interval? Would he buy her an orange? Would he touch her hand? The thought made the sun seem very bright, and the pigeons very loud. She looked at the old men again. Had they been to the theatre? Had they sat in the dark with girls in brown coats and bought them oranges? It seemed impossible. They looked as if they had been born old, as if they had never been young and nervous and uncertain about ham sandwiches. They were carved out of the same stone as the church, and the church had stood there for hundreds of years, and would stand there for hundreds more, long after Edith Merton had finished her sandwich and long after Mr. Arbuthnot had gone home to his lodgings.

The light shifted. A cloud passed over the sun, and the world turned grey for a moment, the pigeons losing their purple sheen, the old men becoming shadows, the sandwich becoming merely bread. It was like a veil being drawn across the face of the world, and Edith felt a sudden chill, a sudden sense of the vastness of the city and the smallness of her own hands. She was just one of thousands, typing letters about coal and shipping and the price of wheat, and Mr. Arbuthnot was just one of thousands of men asking girls to the theatre, and the theatre was just a building where people sat in rows and the old men were just waiting for the end of the day, or the end of the week, or the end of everything. But then the cloud passed, and the sun came back, sharp and gold, striking the stone cross on the church wall so that it blazed white, and the pigeons cooed, and the old man with the white beard shifted his newspaper, and Edith felt the warmth return to her hands. It was all very strange, she thought; the way the light came and went, the way the noise of the street was there and not there, the way a sandwich could be eaten and then gone, leaving only a taste in the mouth.

She looked at the clock again. It was nearly time to go back. The keys would be waiting for her, and the smell of ink and dust, and Mr. Arbuthnot would be there, looking at his shoes or looking at her, and she would have to decide. Yes or no. To go or not to go. It seemed such a small thing, and yet it felt as large as the church tower, as large as the sky. She brushed the crumbs from her lap, and they fell among the pigeons, who pecked at them eagerly. She stood up, and her shadow fell across the paving stones, long and thin, merging with the shadows of the old men and the trees. She smoothed her coat. She felt the wind on her face, cool and fresh, smelling of coal smoke and rain. She thought of the theatre, and the dark, and the orange, and Mr. Arbuthnot’s hand. She thought of the pigeons, and the stone, and the old men. She thought of the letters she had typed, and the letters she would type, and the endless, flowing river of time that carried them all along, from the office to the churchyard, from the sandwich to the theatre, from the light to the dark. And she turned, and walked towards the gate, and the city rushed in to meet her, a roar of sound and motion, and she was gone.
