It was on a raw November afternoon, with the fog pressing like a damp grey blanket against the windows of my consulting-room, that I received the telegram which was to introduce me to the affairs of Marchmont College. It ran: “Master here in great perplexity. Forgery and burglary. Pray come at once. Bellingham, Marchmont College, Stoke Newington.” I had no case of moment upon hand, and the prospect of a puzzle, however provincial, was welcome enough to a man of my habits. Within the hour I was rattling northward in a hansom, and by half-past four I was shown into the study of Mr. Herbert Bellingham, the senior classical master.

He was a lean, stooped man of fifty, with the peering, near-sighted air of one who spends his days over small print and smaller boys, and his hands, I observed, were never still. He waved me to a chair, poured out a glass of sherry with a haste that betrayed his agitation, and plunged at once into his story.

“You will think me a fool, sir, to have troubled you, but I am at my wits’ end. Two days ago Mr. Craddock—the headmaster, you understand—received a letter from his bankers, Messrs. Thurlow and Pike of Lombard Street, inquiring whether a cheque for forty pounds, drawn in favour of a certain E. Halliday and bearing what purported to be his signature, was genuine. It was not. Craddock went to the bank, compared the thing with his own hand, and came back in a state I need not describe. The forgery is a clumsy one, sir—the ‘C’ in Craddock a mere loop, the crossing of the ‘t’ a full inch from the stem—but it was clever enough to deceive a clerk who had never seen the headmaster write.”

“And the boy?” said I.

“That is the crux of it. The cheque was enclosed in a letter which bore the postmark of this village, and the only boy who could conceivably have had access to the headmaster’s study, and who is known to have been in money difficulties, is Arthur Vane, the captain of the school. He is seventeen, sir, and a scholar of the first order—a boy of whom I had hoped great things. But his handwriting is as unlike this forgery as mine is unlike yours. I have compared them a hundred times. Vane’s hand is small, upright, and scholarly; the forgery is sprawling and ill-formed, the hand, I would swear, of a boy who has never been taught to write properly, or of a man aping a scholar’s hand and failing.”

“You have the letter and the cheque?”

“Here.” He produced them from a drawer. I examined them with care. The paper of the letter was common stuff, the ink a cheap blue-black. The handwriting was, as he had said, uncouth, with a curious leftward slant and a habit of beginning each word with a heavier pressure than it ended. The signature “J. Craddock” was laboured and uncertain, like the work of a copyist who had before him a model he did not understand.

“And the burglary?”

Bellingham’s face grew graver. “That is the second and stranger matter. Last night—or rather in the small hours of this morning—the headmaster’s study was entered. The window was forced with a chisel, the desk broken open, and a number of papers taken, together with a small sum in gold which Craddock kept there for petty expenses. Nothing else was touched. But here is the oddity: the thief left behind him, caught in the splintered wood of the window-frame, a fragment of cloth—a piece of a school blazer, sir, with the Marchmont colours, and, what is more, a button bearing the crest of the school.”

“Which narrows the field to some four hundred boys,” said I, with a smile.

“It narrows it further than you think. The blazer is not the ordinary school blazer. It is the blazer of the first eleven—the cricket eleven. There are but eleven boys in the school entitled to wear it, and of those eleven only three were in the building last night. Vane is one. The others are a boy named Pemberton, a heavy, sullen fellow, the son of a wealthy City man, and a little lad called Ainsworth, the wicket-keeper, a harmless creature who could not force a window with a crowbar, let alone a chisel.”

“And Vane’s blazer?”

“Is intact. I have seen it. So is Pemberton’s. Ainsworth’s, I may say, is at the tailor’s, being repaired. The fragment, therefore, matches no blazer in the school—which is the more remarkable, since the colours are unmistakable.”

I rose and walked to the window. The fog had thickened, and the gas-lamps in the quadrangle made little yellow smears of light. “You have a rival house, I take it,” said I, “or some master whose fortunes are not yours?”

Bellingham started. “There is Mr. Dunmore, who keeps the modern side. He and Craddock have been at odds these five years over the management of the school. But surely, sir, you do not suggest——”

“I suggest nothing. I observe. This forgery was not done by a scholar of the first order, for it is the work of a hand untrained in the formation of letters. This burglary was not done by a boy of the first eleven, for the fragment matches no blazer in the school. Yet the thief wore the colours, or a piece of them, and left it behind him—which is the act of a man who wishes the blame to fall where it does not belong. Tell me, is there a tailor in the village who supplies the school?”

“There is. Old Pascoe, in the High Street.”

“Then I will trouble you for the address, and for the name of the boy who is the most likely to know the secrets of the eleven.”

Bellingham thought. “Ainsworth,” he said at last. “The little wicket-keeper is everybody’s friend and nobody’s enemy. He is for ever in the pavilion, and he hears all that is said there.”

I found the boy in the pavilion, as I had been told, a sharp-faced, eager little fellow of fourteen, who regarded me with the mingled awe and curiosity which boys of his age reserve for men who are not masters. I put a few questions to him, and he answered with the readiness of one who loves to be important.

“Please, sir, I don’t know nothing about the blazer,” he said, “but I know this: two days ago I was in Pascoe’s shop, and Mr. Dunmore’s boy—his name is Tucker, sir, a day-boy—was there, and he was buying a piece of old Marchmont blazer cloth, the first eleven colours, and a button with the crest. Pascoe asked him what he wanted it for, and he said it was for a fancy-dress costume. I thought it queer at the time, sir, because Tucker is not in the eleven, and never will be.”

“And the chisel?”

The boy’s eyes widened. “There was a chisel in the tool-chest in the workshop, sir, and it’s gone. Tucker has the key of the workshop, being the only day-boy in the carpenter’s class.”

I returned to Bellingham with my news, and bade him send for the headmaster and for Mr. Dunmore. The latter came, a florid, hearty man with a loud voice and a manner of bluff good-fellowship which did not altogether conceal a certain uneasiness. When I laid before him the evidence of the cloth and the chisel, he blustered, then grew pale, and at last, with a glance at Craddock, broke down.

“It was not my doing,” he cried. “I never set the boy on. But I knew—I knew he had a grudge against Vane, who had beaten him for the scholarship, and I—I let him think that if Vane were disgraced, the scholarship might come to him. I did not know he would go so far.”

The boy Tucker, when confronted, confessed everything. He had forged the cheque, using a letter of the headmaster’s which he had stolen from the master’s desk during a message, and had copied the signature from a circular. He had worn the fragment of blazer to throw suspicion on the eleven, and had forced the window with the chisel to make the case complete. He was a clever, bitter, ill-taught lad, and his hand, as I had observed, was the hand of the forger.

When the business was concluded, Bellingham walked with me to the gate. “I owe you a debt, sir,” he said, “which I can never repay. But tell me—how did you know from the first that it was not Vane?”

“My dear sir,” said I, “a scholar’s hand is a scholar’s hand, and a forger’s is a forger’s. You had only to look at the two. The rest was a matter of the button and the blazer—and of asking the one boy in the school who was everybody’s friend and nobody’s enemy.”
