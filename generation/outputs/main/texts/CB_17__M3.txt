The wind from the wolds came shrieking across the desolate heath, rattling the leaded casements of Thornecroft Hall as though the very fiends of the air sought entry. It was past midnight, and the house was plunged in that heavy, absolute stillness which, to a wakeful and solitary mind, seems less like peace and more like the held breath of a sleeping giant. I had sat long over my extinguished fire, my hands folded on my lap, my soul wrestling with the profound isolation of my lot. I was a governess—neither servant nor family; an anomalous creature, permitted to educate, yet forbidden to belong. I had reconciled myself to the solitary chapel of my duties, when a sharp, low knock struck my chamber door.

I started. At this hour, and in this remote wing of the old house, such a sound was replete with a startling significance. I rose, crossed the cold floorboards, and turned the key.

It was Mrs. Eaton—Mr. Eaton’s sister. She stood on the threshold, wrapped in a dark wool shawl, her face, usually so placid and composed, now drawn and sharply pale in the flickering light of my solitary candle. Her eyes, ordinarily veiled by a calm, almost severe indifference, gleamed with a restless, feverish fire.

“Come in,” I whispered, instinctively drawing her into the room and shutting out the gloomy corridor. “What is amiss? Is it the child? Is Marian ill?”

“Marian sleeps the sleep of the innocent,” she replied, her voice low, vibrating with an intensity I had never before witnessed in her. “It is not the child. It is I. I could not rest. I dared not remain another hour in that room—beside that man—without uttering the truth to a living soul. You are a stranger here, Miss Arden; you have no ties to bind you to my brother’s interests; you are a woman, and you look as though you might comprehend a despair that does not dare to lift its voice.”

I motioned her to a chair, but she waved it aside, standing rigidly before the dead grate. “You think our household harmonious,” she continued, her gaze fixed upon the cold ashes. “You see a fair estate, a respectable name, a family at prayer in the morning and at cards in the evening. You see my brother, stern, perhaps, but just. You have thought him a good man.”

“He is my employer,” I said, feeling the necessity of caution. “I have found him exacting, but I have no cause to complain of injustice.”

“Because you do not know!” she broke forth, a sudden tremor seizing her frame. “You see the stone wall of his exterior, but you do not see the dungeon behind it! I am a prisoner, Miss Arden—a prisoner in a marriage made for land, for connection, for the aggrandizement of his name! He does not strike me—no; he is too profoundly proud for that. But he freezes me. He looks at me with a gaze that turns my blood to ice. He has alienated my few friends, he scrutinizes my expenses, he reads my correspondence. I am bound to a man whose touch is a contagion and whose voice is a decree.”

I looked at her. The anguish in her face was too vivid to be feigned. “I am very sorry,” I said, slowly. “But why do you tell this to me?”

“Because I must act, or I shall perish!” She stepped closer, her hand seizing mine with a fierce, convulsive pressure. “I have a friend—one who knew me before I was bartered into this misery. He resides near Beverley. He is willing to receive me, to shelter me, until I can secure my escape. But I am watched. My letters are opened before they reach the post. You, however—you walk to the village every morning with Marian. You are above suspicion. I ask you—no, I implore you—to carry this letter and to post it unseen. Will you do it?”

She drew from her bosom a small, folded paper, sealed with a wafer. It lay between us, a square of white in the shadows.

My heart beat with a sudden, painful violence. Here was a crisis thrust upon me, demanding an immediate moral choice. I looked at the letter, then at Mrs. Eaton. I thought of Mr. Eaton—his hard, grey eye, his thin, compressed lip, his cold, calculating tyranny. I thought, too, of the Scripture which bids us honor our commitments, and of the stern injunctions against deceit. Yet here was a soul in anguish, a woman crying out from the depths of a gilded tomb. Was I to refuse her? Was I to be the cold instrument of her continued oppression?

“I cannot do it,” I said, and my voice sounded strange and harsh even to my own ear. “I cannot be a bearer of secret intelligence. If Mr. Eaton were to discover it, my situation here would be forfeited.”

“Your situation!” she echoed, with a flash of indignant scorn. “And what is a situation, weighed against a human life? What is your bread, against my liberty? You are young, you are educated; you can seek another place. But I—I am chained! Will you not help me?”

“I would help you if I could,” I replied, feeling the keenness of her reproach. “But I must not do wrong to do right. If you are indeed so miserable, why not appeal to your family? Why not leave openly?”

“Openly!” She laughed—a short, bitter sound. “You do not know him. He would never permit it. He would lock me in my chamber, he would call me mad, he would strip me of my child. I must go secretly, or not at all.”

“Then I will not,” I said, firmly. “I will not carry your letter. I will not be a party to a deception, however charitable the motive. If I am to serve you, it must be by open means, or not at all.”

She stared at me. I saw the fire fade from her eyes, replaced by a cold, settling despair. She took up the letter, slowly, and replaced it in her bosom. “You are right,” she said, at length, in a toneless, weary voice. “I ask too much. I compromise you. Forgive me.”

She turned and left the room, her footsteps fading down the corridor. I remained standing by the door, my hands clasped, my soul a tempest of conflicting emotions. I had refused her; I had adhered to the strict letter of my duty. But was I right? Had I not, in my fear of consequences, denied a fellow-creature the only aid she dared to seek? The wind still howled without, and the candle guttered in its socket, casting long, wavering shadows on the wall. I felt, in the silence of that winter night, the heavy, solitary burden of a conscience that will not be stilled.
