It was in the autumn of 1893, a season of persistent drizzle and low grey skies, that the matter of the Ashworth telegrams was brought to our attention. The first intimation came in the form of a visit from Mr. Alistair Ashworth, the sole heir to the considerable fortune of the Ashworth & Son brewery of Faversham, Kent. He was a young man of perhaps five-and-twenty, well-dressed in the style of a country gentleman, yet there was a wildness in his eyes and a nervous tremor in his hands that spoke of a spirit under siege. He stood dripping upon our hearthrug, his fine travelling coat darkened with rain, and it was with a palpable effort that he controlled his voice.

“Mr. Holmes? Dr. Watson? I am come at the suggestion of my solicitor. I find myself in a predicament so bizarre, so utterly without reason, that I fear for my own sanity as much as my life.”

“Pray be seated, Mr. Ashworth, and put aside your coat,” said Holmes, his keen eyes having already taken in the man’s agitation from head to foot. “A predicament which threatens one’s sanity is, in my experience, the most likely to have a solid and earthly explanation. You have come from Kent, I see, though your boots are clean. You took a cab from Charing Cross.”

The young man blinked. “Why, yes. But how—”

“A simple matter of deduction. The mud on your coat is of the Wealden clay, distinctive in its reddish-brown hue. But your boots, having been brushed at your hotel, are free of it. The matter, then, if you please.”

Ashworth drew a calfskin wallet from his breast pocket and produced three flimsy yellow slips of paper, which he laid upon the table. They were telegrams.

“The first arrived a fortnight ago,” he began, his voice barely above a whisper. “I took it for a cruel joke, or a case of mistaken identity.”

Holmes took up the first message. It was brief and to the point. *“REGRET TO INFORM YOU OF THE DEATH OF MR. JOSIAH CROFT. SINCERE CONDOLENCES.”*

“I did not know a Josiah Croft,” Ashworth continued. “I showed it to my father, and we laughed it off. But two days later, a local man of that name, a retired corn-chandler, was found dead in his bed. The doctor called it a simple seizure of the heart. A coincidence, we thought, and a grim one.”

Holmes’s eyes were fixed on the second telegram. *“REGRET TO INFORM YOU OF THE DEATH OF MR. EDWARD PIKE. SINCERE CONDOLENCES.”*

“This one came five days after the first,” said Ashworth, his hands now clenched into white-knuckled fists. “Edward Pike was our head cooper. A man in the prime of his life. He was found dead in the cooperage two days after that message arrived. The verdict was an accident—a fall from a gantry. But I saw the man’s face, Dr. Watson. There was a look of terror upon it that no fall could account for.”

Holmes handed the third message to me. It was identical in form and wording to the others, save for the name. The name was Alistair Ashworth.

“And this?” Holmes asked, his voice soft and penetrating. “When did this arrive?”

“Yesterday morning,” Ashworth choked out. “By God, Mr. Holmes, I am the third name! These are not condolences; they are pronouncements. Death sentences, delivered by wire. I have not slept. Every shadow seems to hold a weapon, every face a threat. I came to London, but I know not where to turn.”

Holmes steepled his long, thin fingers and gazed at our client. “You have acted with some presence of mind. Tell me, who benefits from your death?”

“My cousin, Giles Ashworth, would inherit the brewery, of course. But he is in South Africa. My father is hale and hearty. There is no one.”

“And the brewery itself? Any disputes? Disgruntled employees?”

“We are a large firm. There are always minor grumbles. But nothing of this sort. Nothing that would lead to… to this systematic murder by prediction.”

Holmes rose and began to pace the room. “We must have facts, not theories. The telegrams were sent from Faversham Post Office, I presume?”

“Yes. The police there are of no use. They dismiss it as a malicious hoax.”

“Then we shall see for ourselves. Watson, your bag, if you please. Mr. Ashworth, you will stay here tonight. Mrs. Hudson will see to your comfort. We shall take the first train to Kent in the morning.”

The town of Faversham was a picture of placid industry, dominated by the vast, sweet-smelling edifice of the Ashworth brewery. The air was thick with the scent of malt and hops. Mr. Ashworth’s father, a bluff, florid man named Silas, met us at the station, his face a mask of paternal worry. He was a man of immense physical presence, with a voice like a rolling barrel, and he greeted us with a mixture of relief and scepticism.

“This is a wild goose chase, Mr. Holmes,” he boomed, shaking my friend’s hand with a grip like a blacksmith’s. “Some scoundrel with a grudge is trying to frighten the boy. But I’ll not have him cowering in London. He belongs here, at his post.”

“His post may be more perilous than you imagine, sir,” Holmes replied coolly. “I should like to see the post office, and then I should like to speak with the families of the deceased.”

The post office clerk, a precise and unhelpful man, could tell us only that the messages had been handed in by a boy, paid for in cash. A dead end. Our next visit was to the house of the widow Croft, a small, neat cottage where the old woman sat in mourning, her grief a quiet, settled thing. From her we learned little, save that her husband had been a man of simple habits and few enemies.

It was at the home of Edward Pike’s widow that a small but significant detail emerged. The woman, still distraught, showed us the room where her husband had kept his papers. “He was so proud of his work,” she sobbed. “Thirty years he gave to the brewery. He was to have a testimonial dinner next month.”

Holmes’s eyes scanned the modest mantelpiece, crowded with photographs and ornaments. He picked up a small, framed daguerreotype of the cooper in his work apron. “A fine-looking man,” he murmured. Then his gaze fell upon a small, carved wooden box on the bedside table. “May I?”

He lifted the lid. Inside, nestled on a bed of velvet, was a single, tarnished silver sixpence.

“A curious keepsake,” Holmes observed.

“He found it,” Mrs. Pike explained. “A week before he… before the accident. He said it was a lucky piece. He found it on the floor of the brewery office, near Mr. Silas’s desk.”

Holmes said nothing, but I saw the familiar spark in his grey eyes. He replaced the box and we took our leave. As we walked back towards the brewery, the great vats casting long shadows in the afternoon light, Holmes turned to me.

“Watson, what do you make of a lucky sixpence found in a brewery office?”

“A trifle, Holmes. Surely?”

“In this case, my dear fellow, there are no trifles. Every detail is a potential thread. Consider: two men are dead, and our client is threatened. The motive must be great. The method is one of psychological terror, preceding a physical act. It is the work of a cold, patient, and methodical mind. And I begin to suspect it is a mind much closer to home than our client imagines.”

He led us not back to the Ashworth residence, but to the brewery itself. He wished to speak with Silas Ashworth again. We found the old brewer in his office, a magnificent room overlooking the entire operation. He was examining a ledger, a frown on his face.

“Ah, Mr. Holmes. Any progress?”

“Some,” said Holmes, his voice dangerously soft. “I was interested to hear of the lucky sixpence that Mr. Pike found.”

Silas Ashworth’s ruddy face paled slightly. “A sixpence? What of it?”

“It was found near your desk, I understand. A curious coincidence. Just as it is a coincidence that a man who was to be honoured for his long service should die so suddenly. And a corn-chandler, a man of substance in the town, should also meet his end.”

“Are you suggesting something, sir?” the brewer demanded, his voice rising.

“I am suggesting,” said Holmes, taking a step forward, “that the motive is not fear, but silence. That the telegrams were not sent by a murderer, but by a man desperate to conceal a crime. A crime of embezzlement, perhaps? A missing sum from the brewery accounts, which a sharp-eyed cooper and an honest bookkeeper like Josiah Croft might have discovered?”

Silas Ashworth stared, his mouth agape. The bluff confidence had vanished, replaced by a ghastly, slack-jawed horror. It was all the confirmation Holmes required.

“You see, the telegrams were a masterstroke of misdirection,” Holmes continued, his voice ringing with cold certainty. “They were designed to look like the work of an external enemy. But their purpose was to remove the two men who knew the truth, and to discredit the third—your son—by making him appear to be the target of a madman. The panic would cloud his judgement, and your crime would remain hidden. You sent the telegrams yourself, Mr. Ashworth. You killed those men to protect your name, and you were willing to let your own son live in terror to complete the illusion.”

With a great, shuddering groan, Silas Ashworth collapsed into his chair, his head in his hands. The truth, stark and ugly, filled the room. The police were summoned, and the confession was soon forthcoming. The brewery books told the rest of the story: a shortfall of ten thousand pounds, gambled away on the exchanges in a desperate attempt to recoup earlier losses.

As we travelled back to London, the Kentish countryside blurring past the window, Holmes lit his pipe with an air of quiet satisfaction. Alistair Ashworth, pale but resolute, sat opposite us, his ordeal at an end.

“It is a curious thing,” Holmes remarked, puffing thoughtfully at his briar. “The criminal mind often seeks to complicate the simple. A straightforward theft might have gone unnoticed for months. But by adding murder and mystery, the man ensured his own downfall. He wove a web of terror to conceal a simple hole in a ledger, and in so doing, he trapped only himself.”
