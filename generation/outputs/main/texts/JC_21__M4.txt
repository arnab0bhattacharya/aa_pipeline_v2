The drawing-room, though he had been ushered into it a good half-hour before the appointed time, seemed to him to hold the very essence of that respectable and opulent inertia against which, in his more active moments, he had been accustomed to direct the somewhat rusty artillery of his rhetoric. It was a large room, and the dusk of a late October afternoon, filtering through the heavy curtains of crimson velvet, had gathered in its corners and lay in wait under the massive furniture, so that the light, which came principally from a fire of sea-coal burning with a dull, red heart in the great grate, served rather to model the forms of things than to reveal them. There were gleams upon the polished surfaces of mahogany tables, upon the brass fender, upon the gilt frames of pictures that were but spots of darkness on the walls; and the silence was of that profound and costly sort which is made up of the absence of all common sounds, and is broken only at long intervals by the subsiding of a coal, or the distant, muffled rumble of a carriage in the street beyond. It was a silence that seemed to him, in his present humour, to be waiting, attentive and a little contemptuous, for him to speak his part.

Mr. Horatio Verrall—for so he was known in those circles where his name was mentioned with a certain dubious respect—sat forward in a deep armchair, his hands clasped upon the knob of a slender umbrella, and his hat, a soft felt of a depressing blackness, resting on the carpet beside his feet. He was a man of fifty, perhaps, with a heavy, pallid face, the skin of which had the look of having been long exposed to vitiated atmospheres; his hair, thin and grey, was brushed carefully across a high forehead, and his eyes, which were large and of a pale, prominent blue, had a way of fixing themselves upon vacancy with an expression of profound and melancholy cogitation. His clothes were decent, even respectable, but they had the indefinable air of having been purchased at a time when the wearer had cherished different hopes and a different figure; the black frock-coat was a little too large for him at the shoulders, and the trousers, of a pepper-and-salt mixture, bagged at the knees. He was, in truth, a man who had lived for many years upon the subscriptions of the wealthy and the credulous, and the habit of dependence had stamped itself upon his very person, giving to his bearing a curious mixture of servility and self-assertion, as of one who is at once the master of a great cause and the humble recipient of its bounty.

He had been summoned—he corrected himself, he had been *invited*—to this house in Mayfair by a letter from a lady of whom he knew nothing beyond the fact that she was the widow of a wealthy manufacturer of Bradford, and that she had, in the phrase of the moment, advanced views. Her name was Mrs. Eleanor Ashby, and the letter had been couched in terms of a somewhat hysterical enthusiasm, speaking of a desire to devote her fortune to the cause of the oppressed, and of a profound admiration for the writings of Mr. Verrall, which, it appeared, had first opened her eyes to the iniquities of the social system. He had received many such letters in his time. They were, indeed, the very breath of his nostrils, the source of the modest income which permitted him to inhabit his two rooms in a quiet street off the Edgware Road, and to maintain that attitude of philosophic detachment which he was pleased to call his life. But this letter had been different. It had spoken of a sum—a sum so considerable that even Mr. Verrall, who was not easily moved, had felt a certain quickening of the pulse. And it had spoken, too, of a personal interview, of a desire to place the matter in his hands, of a confidence that he, and he alone, would know how to direct the great work which her money was to accomplish.

The door opened, and he rose, a little heavily, to his feet. A woman entered, tall and slender, dressed in black, and carrying in her hand a small volume which he recognised, with a curious sinking of the heart, as his own pamphlet on "The Coming Reconstruction." She was not young—perhaps five-and-forty—but her face, pale and eager, with large, dark eyes that seemed to burn with a steady, inward flame, had a kind of worn beauty that was more impressive than youth. She came towards him with both hands extended, and her voice, when she spoke, was low and vibrant with an emotion that seemed to him, in that moment, almost indecent in its intensity.

"Mr. Verrall," she said, "I cannot tell you what it means to me to see you here, in my house. For years I have read your words, and they have been like a trumpet in my ears. I have felt—how shall I say it?—I have felt that you, and you alone, understood the great tragedy of our civilisation, the suffering of the poor, the cruelty of the rich, the—the awful indifference of it all."

He bowed, and murmured something about the honour, the very great honour, and the gratification it afforded him to find his humble efforts so appreciated. He was used to this sort of thing, and the words came to him with a practised fluency. But to-night they sounded hollow in his own ears, and he was conscious of a strange and unwonted dryness in the throat.

She motioned him to his seat, and took her own upon a sofa near the fire, the light of which played upon her pale face and upon the silver clasp of the little book she still held in her hand. She talked for a long time. She spoke of her husband, a man of harsh and narrow views, she said, who had amassed his fortune by the labour of thousands, and who had died, at last, unrepentant and respected, leaving her the mistress of a great wealth which she had come to regard as a trust, a sacred trust, to be used for the uplifting of those from whom it had been wrung. She spoke of her own loneliness, of the futility of a life of ease, of the hunger of her soul for something real, something that would justify her existence. Her voice rose and fell with the rhythm of a deep and genuine passion, and her eyes, fixed upon his face, seemed to search there for some answering fire.

And Mr. Verrall, listening, felt the ground crumbling beneath him. In the old days, in the little Soho taverns, with the smell of stale beer and tobacco in his nostrils, and the eager, half-starved faces of his hearers around him, he had believed—or had he?—in the great revolution that was to come, in the downfall of the tyrants, in the brotherhood of man. The words had been real to him then, or at least the excitement of them had been real, the sense of power, the intoxicating consciousness that he was a voice, a force, a leader. But now, in this quiet room, with this woman's earnest, trusting eyes upon him, and the firelight flickering on the heavy furniture, and the silence of the great house all around them, he saw himself suddenly with a terrible and naked clarity. He saw a man, growing old, who had lived upon the hopes and the credulity of others; a man who had spoken of sacrifice and had made none; a man who had preached the gospel of the poor and had never known a day of real want; a man who had, in short, believed in nothing but his own comfort, and who had now come to the end of his tether, and knew it.

He tried to summon the old phrases, the old indignation, the old fervour. He spoke of the coming dawn, of the inevitable triumph of justice, of the sacred duty of those who had to hasten the day. But the words came haltingly, and he was conscious of a dreadful insincerity, a hollow ring, as though he were an actor who had forgotten his part. The woman listened, and her face, at first so radiant, began to cloud with a faint perplexity, a shadow of doubt.

"You speak," she said, at last, "as one who has seen a vision. And yet—forgive me—there is something in your voice—a weariness, a—a hesitation. Tell me, Mr. Verrall, do you not sometimes feel that the task is too great, that the forces against us are too strong, that the darkness is closing in?"

It was his opportunity. He had only to say the word, to speak of the need of patience, of the slow, sure growth of the idea, of the duty of the individual conscience. But the word would not come. He looked at the fire, at the glowing coals, at the little flames that danced and died upon the surface, and he seemed to see in them the image of his own life, a brief and futile flickering in the midst of an immense and indifferent darkness. He thought of the money, of the ease it would bring, of the years of quiet and comfort that might still be his. And he knew, with a certainty that was like a cold hand upon his heart, that he could not take it. He could not take it, because he had nothing to give in return. He had no faith, no hope, no belief in anything—not in the cause, not in the people, not in himself. He was a hollow man, and the woman, with her passionate sincerity, had seen through him, had stripped him bare, and he was ashamed.

He rose to his feet, slowly, and stood before her, a heavy, pathetic figure, with his hat in his hand.

"Mrs. Ashby," he said, and his voice was low and unsteady, "I am not the man you think me. I have—I have deceived you. I have deceived myself. I have lived for years upon the words which you have read, and I have believed them—no, I have not believed them. I have believed in nothing. I have been a voice, and nothing more—a voice in the wilderness, crying out because it was my trade to cry, and because there were those who would pay me for it. I cannot take your money. I have no light to give you. I am—I am a fraud."

He stopped, and the silence that followed was so profound that he could hear the ticking of a clock in the hall, and the faint, distant sound of a footman's step upon the stairs. The woman looked at him, and her face was very pale, and in her eyes there was a strange, wondering pity, as of one who looks upon a suffering that she cannot understand.

"You are tired," she said, at last, "and you are honest. That is more than I had hoped. Will you not sit down again, and tell me—tell me everything?"

But he shook his head, and moved towards the door. He was conscious of a great weariness, a weariness that was almost peace. He had spoken the truth, and the truth had set him free—free to go back to his two rooms, to his shabby clothes, to his lonely and useless old age. He did not know whether he was sorry or glad. He only knew that the game was over, and that he was, for the first time in many years, an honest man.

He bowed, and went out, and the door closed behind him. And Mrs. Ashby, left alone in the great drawing-room, with the fire burning low and the shadows gathering in the corners, sat for a long time motionless, the little book still in her hand, staring into the glowing embers with a face of deep and troubled thought.
