The news of the broken shaft reached Miss Ellin Carradine at the very hour when she had begun to permit herself a tremour of hope. She had stood at the counting-house window watching the pale winter sunlight fall upon the mill-yard, where the men moved with a will, and the great wheel turned, and the white fleece of the water came down the race with a cheerful sound. It had seemed to her, in that moment, that the long battle was won; that the sneers of the gentry and the cold withdrawal of Mr. Henry Ashworth were but the dust of a conquered field.

Then came the crash—a sound of iron shrieking against iron, a shudder that ran through the very stones of the building—and silence, sudden and dreadful, save for the hiss of steam and the shouting of men.

She was in the yard before she knew she had moved. The wheel stood still. A crowd of workers had gathered about the broken housing, their faces blank with dismay. Among them she saw Timothy Hoyle, the foreman, a man of fifty, whose whole life had been spent in mills, and whose word was law among the hands. He turned as she approached, and his countenance was not encouraging.

“The main shaft’s snapped, miss,” he said, touching his cap with a gesture that was somehow more ominous than rudeness. “’Twill be a month at least before we can run again—maybe two. The founder at Bradley’s will have to make a new one, and there’s no other mill in the valley that can spare us one.”

A murmur ran through the crowd. Miss Carradine heard it, and felt the chill of it in her bones. She had known opposition, contempt, the prophecy of failure from every quarter; but she had not known the doubt of her own people.

“We will send to Bradley’s at once,” she said, and was pleased that her voice did not falter. “Mr. Hoyle, you will go yourself. Take the gig. Spare nothing.”

“And what are we to do meantime, miss?” asked a woman in the crowd—Sarah Milner, whose husband had been the first man she had hired, and whose children she had seen fed from her own kitchen. “We’ve had no wage this fortnight, and the shop won’t give credit beyond Saturday.”

A low growl of assent followed. Miss Carradine looked at them—at their pinched faces, their threadbare coats, the hunger that already lurked in their eyes—and her heart swelled with a passion that was half anguish and half fury.

“You will be paid,” she said. “Every man and woman of you. I have said it.”

“Ay, but for how long?” cried a voice from the back—a young man, reckless with want. “Gentlefolk said as how a woman couldn’t run a mill, and it seems they was right. We’re all to starve because you would be mistress here.”

The words struck her like a blow. She felt the blood rush to her face, and for an instant her sight grew dim. Then she lifted her head, and her eyes swept the crowd with a fire that made the foremost shrink.

“You are not to starve,” she said, and her voice rang clear and hard as the frost on the cobbles. “Do you think I brought you here to see you perish? Do you think I have staked my fortune, my name, my peace, for a whim? I tell you, I will sell the very clothes from my back before you want bread. But I will not be doubted. Go to your homes. To-morrow I will have news for you. And let no man among you say again that a woman cannot do what she has set her hand to.”

They dispersed slowly, muttering, not convinced but silenced. Timothy Hoyle lingered.

“Miss,” he said, “I’ll go to Bradley’s, but I doubt they’ll have a shaft to fit. The mill was built for a smaller wheel than common. ’Twill be a costly job.”

“Let it cost what it will,” she answered. “Only get it done.”

He nodded and went. She stood alone in the yard, and the wind came down from the moors, keen and bitter, and the hoar-frost gathered on her cloak. She looked at the silent mill, at the dead wheel, at the black mouth of the race where the water still poured uselessly away; and for the first time since she had conceived her scheme, she felt the cold hand of fear close upon her heart.

That evening she sat alone in the little parlour of the mill-house, with the fire burning low and the candle guttering. She had dismissed her one servant, and the silence of the old house pressed upon her like a living thing. She thought of Henry Ashworth—of his handsome, supercilious face, of the words with which he had withdrawn his suit: “I cannot connect myself with a speculation so hazardous, so unwomanly. You have chosen your path, Miss Carradine; I trust you will not find it too rugged.” She had scorned him then; she scorned him now. But the scorn did not warm her.

A knock at the door startled her. She rose, and opened it to find Timothy Hoyle’s wife, Martha, a stout, motherly woman, with a shawl over her head and a basket on her arm.

“I thought you’d be lone, miss,” she said, “and I’ve brought you a bit of supper. You munnot sit and fret. The men are grumbling, but they’ll come round. They know you’re a right one at bottom.”

Miss Carradine looked at her, and suddenly, to her own amazement, she burst into tears. She, who had faced down the whole parish, who had borne the sneers of the gentry and the desertion of her lover without a quiver, wept like a child before this simple woman.

Martha Hoyle set down her basket and took the young mistress in her arms as if she had been her own daughter.

“There, there, my lamb,” she said. “You’ve been bearing too much alone. But you munnot give way. You’ve got a will of iron, and iron will carry you through. Only you munnot be too proud to take a bit of comfort when it’s offered.”

Miss Carradine dried her eyes. “I am not proud,” she said. “I am only afraid—afraid that I have led them all to ruin.”

“Ruin!” cried Martha. “Ruin, indeed! Why, miss, there’s not a man in that mill but what would have been in the poorhouse long since if you hadn’t come. You’ve given them work and wage and hope. And if the shaft is broke, why, it can be mended. It’s not the end of the world.”

“No,” said Miss Carradine, slowly. “No, it is not the end of the world.”

She looked at the fire, and a new light came into her eyes—a light that was not the reflection of the flame, but something fiercer and more steadfast.

“Martha,” she said, “I will not be beaten. I will not be beaten by a broken shaft, nor by the tongues of my neighbours, nor by the cowardice of a man who was not worthy to tie my shoe. I will mend the mill, and I will pay the hands, and I will make this valley ring with the sound of my wheel again. And if I have to go to London myself, and beg, borrow, or sell my last acre, I will do it.”

Martha Hoyle looked at her with a kind of awe. “You’re a rare one, miss,” she said. “You’re a rare one, and no mistake.”

The wind howled in the chimney, and the frost crept closer to the window-pane; but in the little parlour the fire burned brighter, and the two women sat together, and the night wore on. And in the morning, when Timothy Hoyle returned with the news that the new shaft would cost more than she had reckoned, and would take six weeks to make, Miss Carradine heard him out with a calm face, and then said:

“Very well. We will begin to-morrow to repair the race and the housing. We will keep the men at work. And I will write to my uncle in Leeds for the money.”

“And if he refuses, miss?”

She looked at him with a steady eye.

“He will not refuse,” she said. “And if he does, I will go to the bank. I am not afraid of them. I am not afraid of anything now.”

And Timothy Hoyle, looking at her, believed her.
