The air in the courtroom was not merely hot; it was a physical weight, a stagnant, suffocating blanket that seemed to press against the glass windows and settle upon the shoulders of the men within. It was that peculiar Singapore heat, heavy with the scent of damp earth, rotting fruit, and the faint, metallic tang of the harbor outside, which crept in through the louvered shutters even when they were drawn tight against the glare. I sat in the box, my hands clasped loosely in my lap, feeling the texture of the wood beneath my palms, a rough, dry reality that anchored me to the present moment while my mind drifted back to the night the *Sunderland* went down. It was a strange sensation, to be the judge of one’s own crime, or rather, to be the accused of a mistake that had felt, in the heat of the moment, less like an error and more like an inevitability, a surrender to the dark water that had swallowed us all.

The judge, a man of high rank and even higher self-importance, sat elevated on the bench, his face obscured by the shadow of his wide-brimmed hat, which he had not removed despite the interior of the room. He spoke in a voice that was dry and brittle, like old parchment being torn, asking questions that were more formalities than inquiries. He knew the answer. We all knew the answer. The chart was marked; the log was signed; the compass had swung. There was no mystery in it, no devil to exorcise, only the cold, hard fact of my own negligence. I had trusted the depth soundings too long, too blindly, believing that the water would hold, that the bottom was further out than the charts suggested. It was a fatal trust, a human weakness dressed in the language of nautical certainty.

Then, the clerk called the name. I felt a shift in the air, a subtle vibration that traveled down my spine. I turned my head, slowly, as if moving in deep water, to look at the witness box. There stood Aldous. He looked smaller than I remembered, his uniform ill-fitting, the collar of his shirt straining slightly against his neck. His face was pale, drained of its usual ruddy color, and his eyes, when they found mine, held a look that was not of accusation, but of a profound, weary sadness. It was the look of a man who has seen too much of the sea’s indifference and has come to expect nothing less from his fellows.

Aldous had warned me. I remembered the sound of his voice through the speaking tube, thin and reedy in the darkness of the night watch. *“Captain, the soundings are changing. We are closer to the shoal.”* I had dismissed it. I had been tired, my head heavy with the day’s heat, and the sea had been calm, deceptively smooth, offering up no hint of the danger that lay beneath the surface. I had told him to stand by, to trust the log. Now, standing there under the harsh electric light that buzzed like an angry insect, Aldous was about to tell the truth. The truth is a terrible thing, not because it is false, but because it is bare, stripped of all the comforting lies we tell ourselves to make sense of our actions.

The judge leaned forward, his elbow resting on the desk, and asked, “Did the Master take the advice of the Mate regarding the depth?”

Aldous did not answer immediately. He looked around the room, his gaze sweeping over the faces of the other crewmen, who sat in the gallery like a flock of mute birds, their eyes downcast, their bodies rigid with the tension of shared guilt and fear. I saw the Chief Officer, a man of few words and less imagination, staring at the floor, his jaw clenched so tight that the muscles stood out in knotted whorls. He knew what was coming. We all knew.

“I did, sir,” Aldous said. His voice was low, steady, devoid of any trace of anger or self-pity. It was the voice of a man stating a fact, like the reading of a barometer. “I reported the depth to the Master at two bells. I reported it again at three bells, when the soundings had decreased by two fathoms. I suggested we alter course to starboard to avoid the known shoal area.”

The judge wrote something down, the scratch of his pen on paper the only other sound in the room, a sharp, staccato rhythm that seemed to punctuate the silence. “And what was the Master’s response?”

Aldous paused. I watched his lips move, seeing the slight tremor that betrayed the effort it cost him to speak. He was not protecting me. That was the thing that struck me, the thing that cut deeper than the judgment itself. He was not protecting himself, either. He was simply laying out the truth, naked and vulnerable, for the court to inspect. It was a form of loyalty, I realized, a loyalty to the truth that superseded all other bonds. It was a cold, hard loyalty, but it was the only kind that mattered in the end.

“The Master said to stand by, sir. He said the charts were reliable and that we would clear the area without incident. He asked me to maintain my watch.”

The judge looked up, his eyes narrowing slightly. “Did the Master alter course?”

“No, sir.”

The word hung in the air, simple and final. It was the end of it. The *Sunderland* was gone, buried in the mud of the harbor bottom, her ribs cracked, her cargo scattered. I was alive, but I was no longer the man who had been in command. That man had died with the ship, or perhaps he had never really existed at all, just a figment of my own ambition, a ghost that had wandered into the dark and lost its way.

I looked at Aldous again. He was looking at me now, his eyes steady, unblinking. There was no triumph in his face, no satisfaction. There was only a quiet, terrible understanding. He had done his duty. He had spoken the truth. And in doing so, he had condemned me, not just to the court’s judgment, but to the memory of that night, to the memory of his voice in the dark, to the memory of my own silence. It was a mercy, in a way, a mercy that I did not know I deserved. To be judged by truth is better than to be judged by lies, for lies rot from the inside out, while truth, however harsh, is solid, enduring, and clean.

The judge continued to ask questions, but the words began to blur, to lose their meaning. The heat in the room seemed to intensify, pressing against my temples, making my vision swim. I could hear the sound of the sea outside, a low, rhythmic murmur that seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere, a sound that was both soothing and threatening. It was the sound of the water that had taken my ship, the water that had taken my life, and was now taking my place in the world.

When the hearing was over, I walked out into the blinding sunlight. The air was still heavy, still thick with the scent of the harbor, but it felt different now. It felt like a weight I had been carrying for a long time, and now that it had been lifted, I felt hollow, empty, a shell of a man. I looked back at the building, at the closed doors, and then at the crowd of men who were gathering on the street, waiting for news, waiting for justice. I turned away and walked towards the waterfront, my steps slow and measured, each one a step further away from the man I had been. I did not look back. There was nothing left to see, except the vast, indifferent sea, stretching out before me, grey and endless, waiting to take what was left of me.
