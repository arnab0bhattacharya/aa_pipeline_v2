I do not say that the man lied; I say only that he wrote, and that in the writing of men, as in the building of their houses, there is always a foundation of which the visitor knows nothing. The house of his memory was a cold one, built of stark and terrible stone, and I was asked to become its architect in a foreign tongue.

It was in Geneva, in the autumn of the year when the century was still new and yet already old with weariness, that this commission fell to me. I was employed by the house of Aubert et Fils, a firm of publishers whose reputation for grave and serious works was a kind of second religion to its proprietor, Monsieur Aubert. He was a Swiss of the old type, a man whose face was a map of careful decisions and whose hands, when they rested on a manuscript, seemed to be weighing not the paper but the soul of the writer. He called me into his office one afternoon, the light from the lake outside turning the dust motes in the air to a slow, silent dance. The air was thick with the smell of old paper and leather, a smell I had come to associate with a certain kind of truth.

‘Alexei,’ he said, and the sound of my name in his French was always a little foreign, a little out of place. ‘We have a work of the utmost importance. A memoir. From Russia.’

He slid a thick bundle of papers across the polished mahogany of his desk. It was tied with a simple string. The pages were not typeset; they were the man’s own hand, a dense and spidery script that seemed to crawl across the paper like a trail of insects. The author, he informed me, was a revolutionary of some note, a man who had suffered for his ideas and whose name, Orlov, was a kind of banner for the disaffected. The memoirs were to be a testament, a record of struggle, of sacrifice, of the bitter wine of betrayal. My task was to render this cry of the soul into the more orderly language of French, so that Europe might read and, perhaps, understand.

I took the bundle home to my small room overlooking the Rue des Eaux. Outside, the city of Calvin went about its quiet, pious business, the chimes of St. Pierre’s clock marking the hours with an iron regularity that felt like a judgment. For a week I did little but read. The prose was a torrent, at times clear and cold as mountain water, at times muddy with a rage that was almost incoherent. It spoke of clandestine meetings in damp cellars, of printing presses hidden in barns, of comrades marching towards a dawn they would never see. It was a story of immense courage and of a faith so absolute it was terrifying. And then, in the middle of the third chapter, I came upon the name.

My own name. Or rather, my father’s.

It was not a direct accusation at first. It was a shadow, a creeping doubt. Orlov spoke of a certain ‘Aleksei Petrovich V.’, a man of quiet manners and a scholar’s bearing, who had moved amongst the revolutionaries in St. Petersburg with a peculiar, dispassionate grace. This V., he wrote, was a man of letters, a translator of German philosophy, a person who offered shelter and a listening ear. He was trusted. He was one of them. And yet, Orlov wrote, there was a stillness about him, a watchfulness that was not the vigilance of the hunted, but the patience of the hunter. He spoke of documents that fell into the wrong hands, of safe houses that were raided at the very moment of their greatest secrecy, of a trail of disaster that seemed, with an unnerving consistency, to lead back to the quiet rooms of Aleksei Petrovich.

I read these passages again and again, the words swimming before my eyes. The lamp on my table cast a pool of yellow light, but the corners of the room were lost in a profound and absolute darkness. The silence of the city pressed in on the windowpanes. It was my father. My father, who had died two years before, a man I knew through the scent of his pipe tobacco, the weight of his hand on my shoulder, the patient way he would correct my Latin. A man of principle, I had always thought, though his principles were his own and he had never cared to explain them. He was a man who believed in order, in the state, in the slow and difficult work of civilization. Had that belief been a simple conviction, or had it been something else—a commission, a salary, a duty of a more sinister kind?

I began to live a double life. By day, I was the translator, the craftsman, fitting Orlov’s furious Russian into the measured cadences of French. I would choose a word, weigh its moral colour, its historical weight, and in doing so I felt I was not merely translating, but judging. I was the sole judge in a trial where the accuser and the accused were both ghosts. At night, I would walk the deserted quays of the lake, the water black and oily under the gaslights, and I would try to remember. I would search my memory of my father for some sign, some flicker of duplicity. But all I could find was the memory of a man reading in his chair, a man whose face was calm and unreadable. The more I searched, the more he receded from me, becoming a figure of smoke and shadow.

One day, Monsieur Aubert asked me of my progress. ‘The work is of great delicacy,’ I told him, my voice sounding thin and distant to my own ears. ‘The author’s passion is… formidable.’

He nodded, his eyes on my face. ‘The truth is often a formidable thing, my boy. It does not ask to be liked. It asks only to be rendered faithfully.’

Faithfully. The word echoed in my mind for days. Faithful to whom? To the man Orlov, who may have been a visionary or a paranoid, whose memory may have been a crucible of truth or a vessel of grievance? Or to the man I called my father, whose silence now seemed to me the most damning evidence of all?

I never found an answer. I finished the translation. The pages were sent to the printers, and the book, with its stark black cover and its title of blood and iron, was published to some acclaim. I never saw Orlov; he remained a name on a page, a voice in my head. But sometimes, late at night, when the house is quiet and the city sleeps, I take down my copy of the book. I open it to the chapter about Aleksei Petrovich V. and I read the words I myself set down in French. And I marvel that such a thing could be written, that it could be printed and bound and sold, that it can sit on a shelf in a quiet room in Geneva. I do not know if it is the truth. I know only that I have given it a form, and that it is a form which will outlast me, and my doubt, and the memory of my father’s unreadable face. The logic of it is beyond disputing.
