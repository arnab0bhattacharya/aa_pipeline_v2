Mr. Edward Ashworth sat in the small parlour of his mother’s parsonage at Highmorton, wholly unable to fix his mind upon the sermon he was endeavouring to compose. The living of Highmorton, held by his father for five-and-thirty years, had passed to him only a fortnight since; but a living of scarcely three hundred pounds a year, and a curacy in a neighbouring parish which he had lately been forced to resign, were not the fruits of clerical diligence likely to satisfy a man of six-and-twenty, conscious of his own merits, and entertaining, as he did, a very strong regard for Miss Lucy Vane.

Miss Vane was the daughter of a deceased country attorney, possessed of every virtue and accomplishment that could render a young woman estimable, but entirely destitute of fortune. She had, however, a warm heart, an affectionate disposition, and a mind that had been improved by reading; and Mr. Ashworth, having known her from childhood, had contracted an engagement with her, which, however satisfactory to themselves, was by no means equally pleasing to his friends.

Mrs. Ashworth, a woman of excellent sense and temper, who had known the anxieties of a small income for the whole of her married life, was sitting opposite to him at the work-table, and observed his abstraction with a mixture of pity and impatience. She knew the subject of his thoughts, and though she had hitherto forborne to press it, the occasion was now too urgent to admit of silence.

"You are thinking of Lucy, Edward," said she, laying down her work.

Mr. Ashworth coloured, and acknowledged that he was.

"I am not surprised at it," continued his mother; "and I am very far from wishing to speak harshly of a girl who is, I believe, in every respect, amiable. But you must consider, my dear son, that affection is not a substitute for bread. You are now in a situation which, with prudence, may afford you a comfortable independence; but a wife without fortune, and a family increasing upon you, would soon reduce you to distress. I speak from experience; I know what it is to struggle with difficulties."

"You do, madam," replied Edward, with warmth; "and I honour your fortitude. But surely a man may be happy without a large income; and Lucy’s temper—"

"Lucy’s temper is, I doubt not, all that you describe; but tempers are sadly tried by poverty, Edward. I have seen excellent tempers soured by continual privation. And you must remember that you have not only yourself to think of; you have a mother and two unmarried sisters, whose comforts must, in some degree, depend upon your exertions."

Edward was silenced, but not convinced. He had too much good sense not to feel the weight of his mother’s arguments, and too much warmth of feeling not to resent them. He turned again to his sermon, and Mrs. Ashworth resumed her work, with the quiet satisfaction of a woman who had said what she believed to be her duty, and was content to leave the event to time and reflection.

In this state of things, a letter was received from Sir Reginald Bouverie, of Delaford Park, a gentleman of large fortune and considerable interest in the county, who had been an old friend and patron of Mr. Ashworth’s father. The letter was couched in terms of great civility, and contained an offer which, while it flattered Mr. Ashworth’s vanity, agitated his feelings in no common degree. Sir Reginald proposed to present him to the living of Delaford St. Lawrence, a valuable benefice in his gift, worth upwards of seven hundred a year, and which would become vacant in a few weeks by the resignation of the present incumbent. But this offer was accompanied by a condition, which, though delicately hinted at, was sufficiently intelligible. Sir Reginalin observed, that he had always entertained a high respect for the Ashworth family; that he was desirous of providing for his niece, Miss Sophia Bouverie, a young lady of considerable expectations; and that he should be happy to see Mr. Ashworth settled at Delaford, and united to a woman who would do credit to his choice. The letter concluded with an invitation to Highmorton, that the young man might have an opportunity of becoming acquainted with Miss Bouverie.

Mr. Ashworth laid down the letter with emotions which may be more easily conceived than described. The advantages of the Delaford living were such as he had never dared to hope for; and the connection with Sir Reginald’s family would open a scene of life far superior to anything he had yet known. But against all this was to be set his engagement to Lucy Vane — an engagement entered into with the most perfect sincerity, and never for a moment regretted, till ambition and interest began to whisper that he might do better.

He carried the letter to his mother, who read it with undisguised satisfaction.

"This is a most fortunate circumstance, Edward," said she, "and I trust you will not hesitate to accept it. Sir Reginald’s offer is everything that could be wished. Miss Bouverie, I conclude, is a young woman of fortune; and though you say she is plain, a plain woman with a good temper and a handsome jointure is not to be despised."

"She is said to be extremely plain, madam," replied Edward, gravely; "and I am not sure that I could make her happy, or myself either, were I to sacrifice every feeling of the heart to considerations of advantage."

"Fiddlestick on feelings!" cried Mrs. Ashworth, with a spirit which her son’s imprudence had somewhat ruffled. "You are young, Edward, and full of romance; but you will find, when you have been a few years longer in the world, that a good income is a much better support for happiness than all the feelings in the universe. Pray, what is Miss Vane’s portion?"

"Her portion, madam, is her own amiable disposition, and the consciousness of having deserved my esteem."

"A very pretty portion, truly! And how do you propose to maintain her upon it? Your present living will scarce maintain yourself; and if you marry Lucy Vane, you may bid farewell to every prospect of advancement. Sir Reginald’s offer is a chance that may never occur again; and if you refuse it, you will have nobody to blame but yourself for the consequences."

Edward felt the force of this reasoning, and yet he could not bring himself to yield. He retired to his own room, and passed a night of great agitation. The living of Delaford, with all its advantages, presented itself to his imagination in the most flattering colours; but when he reflected on Lucy’s affection, on her confidence in his honour, and on the misery which a breach of his engagement must inflict upon her, he was ready to renounce every worldly consideration, and adhere to his first resolution.

The next morning, however, brought a visit from Lucy herself; and the sight of her, so gentle, so affectionate, so entirely unsuspicious of the conflict in his bosom, produced an effect upon him which she little calculated. He looked at her pale face, her modest demeanour, and her plain but neat dress, and compared her, in spite of himself, with the imaginary Miss Bouverie, surrounded by all the elegancies of Delaford Park. The comparison was not favourable to Lucy; and yet, when she spoke to him with her usual kindness, and expressed her hopes that his new living would afford him more leisure for his favourite studies, he was ready to reproach himself for his baseness.

"You are very good, my dear Lucy," said he, with an emotion he could not wholly conceal; "I fear I am not so deserving of your interest as you suppose."

Lucy, who had observed his altered manner, but attributed it to the pressure of his new duties, replied with a smile, that she knew no one who was better entitled to success in any station; and that whatever might be his lot, she should always feel a pride in his character.

This speech, so artless and so affectionate, went to Edward’s heart. He felt that he was unworthy of her; that he was meditating a treachery which nothing could justify; and he almost resolved to write to Sir Reginald that instant, and decline his offer. But when he sat down to his desk, and took up his pen, the recollection of his mother’s arguments, of his own narrow circumstances, and of the brilliant prospects which the Delaford living opened to his view, again recurred to his mind. He laid down his pen, walked to the window, and stood for some minutes in a state of indescribable agitation.

At length he returned to his seat, and began his letter to Sir Reginald. He thanked him for his generous offer, acknowledged the obligation, and hinted at an engagement which might possibly interfere with his acceptance of it. But here he stopped. The letter remained unfinished; and Edward Ashworth, with a mind distracted between inclination and duty, between ambition and affection, knew not which to choose.
