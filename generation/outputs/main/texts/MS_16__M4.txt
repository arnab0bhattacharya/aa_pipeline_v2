I have lived so long in the shadow of a great and secret sorrow that the world has come to seem to me but a pageant of shadows, and its voices but the distant murmur of a stream that flows past a dungeon wall. Yet there are hours—and this is one—when the past, which I had thought buried beneath the accumulated ruins of years, rises before me with a terrible and living distinctness, and demands, as with a trumpet’s voice, that I no longer keep silence. It is not for my own sake that I now take up the pen, for I have long since ceased to reckon my own peace of any value; it is for the sake of one whose image is enshrined in my heart, and for the sake of truth, which, however long deferred, must at last prevail.

My father, Sir Edmund Ashby, was a man of ancient lineage and of a temper so noble and so generous that the whole county of —— spoke his name with a blessing. He was, indeed, the very soul of honour; and it was this fatal and exquisite sensibility—this incapacity to endure the shadow of a stain upon his name—that hurried him to his doom. I was but a child of twelve summers when the calamity fell upon us; yet the scene is graven upon my memory with a minuteness that no lapse of time can efface. I remember the grey autumnal morning, the mist that hung like a funeral pall over the meadows, the hurried arrival of a stranger at our gates, the low and agitated conference in the library, and then the sound of my mother’s shriek, which seemed to rend the very heavens asunder. They brought him home at evening upon a bier, and the rain beat upon the coffin-lid as though nature herself wept for the ruin of so noble a heart.

The man who slew him was one Lionel Verney—a name which I have since learned to speak with a loathing that words cannot express. He was the son of a neighbouring gentleman, and the quarrel (as I afterwards learned from the papers which my father’s solicitor placed in my hands when I came of age) arose from a trifling difference of opinion at a convivial meeting, inflamed by the malignant insinuations of a third party, whose name I have never been able to discover. Verney, it is true, did not seek the encounter; but he accepted the challenge with a cold and deliberate ferocity that argued a heart dead to every generous impulse. He fired before the word was given—at least, so runs the testimony of the only witness whose deposition I possess—and my father fell without a groan, his pistol discharging itself harmlessly into the air.

For years I knew no more than this. My mother, broken by her grief, retired with me to a remote village in the north, where she devoted herself to my education and to the cultivation of those sentiments of piety and resignation which were the only solace of her widowed heart. She never spoke of the duel; and I, perceiving that the subject was a wound which bled afresh at every touch, forbore to question her. Thus I grew up in ignorance of the world, and of the world’s opinion concerning the man who had robbed me of my father.

It was not until my twenty-first year, when I came into possession of my inheritance, that I learned the truth. Among my father’s papers I found a sealed packet, directed to me in his own hand, and endorsed with these words: “To be opened by my daughter when she shall have attained her majority, if she should ever desire to know by whose hand her father fell.” With trembling fingers I broke the seal. The contents were brief but terrible: a full account of the quarrel, the name of the aggressor, and the deposition of the second, whose signature was attested by a magistrate. I read it with a species of stupor; and then, as the full import of the revelation burst upon me, I fell upon my knees and prayed—prayed, in the wildness of my agony, that I might be permitted to avenge my father’s death.

But Heaven had other designs. In the years that followed, I became acquainted with a young lady of rare endowments and of a disposition so gentle and so pure that she seemed to me a being of another sphere. Her name was Emily Wren; and she was the daughter of a clergyman in the neighbouring town, whose modest stipend was eked out by the instruction of a few pupils. Emily and I became friends—nay, more than friends; for I loved her with a devotion which I had thought no heart could feel, and I believed—I still believe—that my affection was returned. She was the confidante of all my thoughts, the sharer of all my hopes; and when, after a year of intercourse, I ventured to speak to her of love, she confessed, with a blush that made her lovelier than ever, that she had long regarded me with a tenderness surpassing that of mere friendship.

It was at this period—when my cup of happiness seemed full to the brim—that the blow fell which has darkened all my life. I had occasion to visit London upon business connected with my estate; and there, in the midst of the gay and glittering throng which frequents the metropolis during the season, I beheld—Lionel Verney. He was now a man of middle age, portly and commanding in his figure, with a countenance which, to the superficial observer, bore the stamp of benevolence and honour. He was a member of Parliament, and his speeches were quoted with admiration in the public journals. He moved through the brilliant circles of fashion with the ease of one accustomed to adulation; and I heard his name pronounced with respect by persons who would have shrunk with horror from the very thought of a duel.

The sight of him revived all my dormant anguish. I returned to my home in a state of feverish excitement; and I had scarcely entered the house when Emily, with a countenance radiant with joy, ran to meet me, and announced that she had that morning received an offer of marriage from Mr. Verney, who had been staying in the neighbourhood, and that her father, dazzled by the prospect of so splendid an alliance, had given his consent. I heard her words as one hears the thunder of an avalanche; and then I fell senseless at her feet.

When I recovered, I was alone. Emily had fled in terror; and I was left to the contemplation of my misery. What could I do? To speak the truth—to reveal the secret of my father’s death—would be to blast the happiness of the friend I loved, to cover her with shame, and to consign her to a life of wretchedness. To be silent was to permit the murderer of my father to enter the family of the woman I adored—to sit at her board, to clasp her hand, to call her wife. I was torn by a thousand contending emotions. I thought of my father’s dying groans, of my mother’s broken heart, of the long years of solitude and sorrow which I had endured; and then I thought of Emily—of her gentle nature, her innocence, her love for me—and my resolution faltered.

I resolved at last to speak. I would go to Emily’s father; I would place in his hands the proofs of Verney’s guilt; and I would leave it to his honour to decide whether such a man should be permitted to ally himself with his family. But when I reached the parsonage, my courage failed me. The good old man received me with his usual kindness; and Emily, who was present, looked at me with such an expression of entreaty in her eyes that my heart died within me. I could not speak. I murmured some incoherent words, and withdrew.

That night I wrote a letter—a long, passionate, agonised letter—to Emily herself. I told her all: the story of the duel, the name of the man who had slain my father, the proofs which I possessed. I implored her to pause—to consider—to save herself from a union which would be a perpetual source of misery to her and to me. I sent the letter by a trusty messenger; and I waited with an agony of suspense for the reply.

It came at last—but not from Emily. It was a brief note from her father, informing me that his daughter had that morning been married to Mr. Verney by special licence, and that they had departed for the Continent. The old man added that he trusted I would see the impropriety of troubling the family further with a matter which concerned only myself.

I read the note, and I laughed—laughed with a laughter that was more terrible than tears. Then I took the packet of my father’s papers, and I locked it in a secret drawer, and I swore a solemn oath that I would never speak of the duel to any living soul. And I have kept that oath. For five years I have kept it; and in that time I have seen Emily—for she returned to England after a year of absence—I have seen her the wife of Lionel Verney, moving in society, admired, caressed, and happy. I have seen her children—his children—clinging to his knees; and I have turned away with a sickness at my heart which I cannot describe.

And now, at last, the secret has become too heavy for me to bear. I have determined to speak—not publicly, not to the world, but to one person—to Emily herself. I shall go to her, and I shall tell her all. She may hate me; she may spurn me; she may refuse to believe me; but I shall have done my duty. And if, after all, she should still love him—if she should choose to remain in ignorance—then I shall know that the grave has closed over my last hope, and I shall retire for ever from the world, and seek in the solitude of nature that peace which I have sought in vain amid the haunts of men.

The night is far advanced. The wind howls mournfully without, and the rain beats against the windows. I have been writing for hours, and my hand trembles with fatigue and emotion. To-morrow I shall go to her. God grant me strength to perform my task!
