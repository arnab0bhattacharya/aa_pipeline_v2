Professor Aldworth Pellow, of the chair of Natural Philosophy at Marchmont College, was a man of fifty-five, with a bald dome of forehead, a ragged moustache, and the settled habit of talking to himself in a low, argumentative mutter whenever an experiment approached its crisis. On the Tuesday afternoon in question he stood in the garden of his house in Prince Regent’s Terrace, holding a small brass canister at arm’s length, and addressing it as though it were a somewhat backward pupil.

“You are, if I may say so, the most infernal nuisance in the whole of chemistry,” he observed. “Nine years of my life. Nine years, and Mrs. Pinnock still believes I am manufacturing a substitute for dripping.”

The canister contained some two ounces of a greyish powder, the product of a long series of experiments upon the hypothetical “gravific ether” of which he had written, with more caution than conviction, in the Marchmont Quarterly. The powder, when exposed to the air in sufficient quantity, appeared to interpose a species of insulation between any adjacent mass and the earth’s pull. He had demonstrated the fact already with a two-ounce weight, which had risen to the ceiling of his study and remained there, gently rotating, for the better part of an hour, until the effect wore off and it descended with a smart tap upon his cranium. He had since established, by careful measurement, that the effect was proportional to the surface exposed, and that a body of sufficient area—a plank, a table-top, a light wickerwork—might be lifted with a very trifling charge.

The garden chair was of wickerwork. It stood upon the little lawn, a modest affair of interlaced cane with a faded cushion, and it was Mrs. Pinnock’s particular pride, having been purchased at the Church Bazaar of the previous Whitsuntide for eleven and sixpence. Pellow had chosen it because it was light, expendable, and—though he would not have admitted the motive—because its loss would annoy his housekeeper in a manner he found, in his drier moments, faintly amusing.

“We shall now,” he said to the canister, “endeavour to discover whether the effect is distributed uniformly over an extended surface. Hold still, you brute.”

He sprinkled a portion of the powder upon the seat of the chair, stepping back as he did so. Nothing occurred for perhaps five seconds. Then the chair rose, with a curious deliberation, some three feet from the grass, and hung there, rocking gently, like a boat at anchor. Pellow clapped his hands.

“Ha! Uniform distribution, as I predicted. The centre of effort is directly above the centre of mass. Now if I add a trifle more—”

He was interrupted by the garden door, which opened to admit Mrs. Pinnock, a stout, red-faced, energetic woman of sixty, carrying a basket of washing and wearing the expression of one who has just discovered a fresh outrage.

“Professor, I’ll thank you to keep your nasty chemicals off the—merciful heavens!”

The chair had drifted, under the influence of a light easterly breeze, some six or seven feet to the left, and was now bobbing at the height of the drawing-room windows. Mrs. Pinnock set down her basket and stared.

“It’s the chair,” said Pellow, with the slightly fatuous satisfaction of a demonstrator. “My dear Mrs. Pinnock, you are witnessing a scientific event of the first magnitude. The chair is, in point of fact, no longer subject to gravity.”

“It’s my chair,” said Mrs. Pinnock, in a voice of dangerous calm. “It’s the bazaar chair. Eleven and sixpence. And it’s got my good cushion on it.”

“The cushion,” Pellow conceded, “is an error of judgment. I shall reimburse you. But observe the stability of the thing! It hangs as steady as a balloon. I believe that if I were to increase the charge and seat a person upon it—”

“You’ll do no such thing,” said Mrs. Pinnock, and made a grab for the chair as it drifted past. Her fingers closed upon the wicker arm. The chair, thus burdened, dipped perhaps a foot, then righted itself, and to Pellow’s unspeakable horror began to ascend.

“Let go!” he shouted. “My dear woman, let go!”

But Mrs. Pinnock, who had never in her life let go of anything that was hers, did not let go. She had grasped the chair with both hands, and now, as it rose, she was compelled to scramble, with a singular lack of dignity, until she sat full upon the cushion, her basket of washing scattered across the lawn, her skirts billowing, her mouth open in a soundless O of astonishment. The chair ascended with a steady, unhurried motion, clearing the hedge at the end of the garden, and passed over the roof of the Pellow residence at a height of perhaps forty feet.

Pellow ran into the road. A milkman’s cart stood at the corner, the horse cropping at a hedge. The milkman, a young man named Hedges, was staring upward with an expression of profound and simple wonder.

“She’m flyin’,” said Hedges.

“She is not flying,” said Pellow, with what dignity he could muster. “She is being conveyed. There is a difference. Get me a ladder. Get me a—get me a long pole, a clothes-line, anything!”

But the chair was already past the terrace and over the roof of the Marchmont Institute, a gaunt Victorian pile of red brick, and the breeze was carrying it steadily eastward over the town. Pellow, hatless, his coat-tails flying, ran along the High Street in pursuit, and behind him came Hedges and the milk-cart, and behind Hedges a growing rabble of small boys and idle men, all with their faces upturned.

The town of Marchmont, from above, is a compact and orderly place, arranged in a rough grid about the market square, with the college and its playing fields to the north and the river winding away to the east. Mrs. Pinnock, seated upon her cushion with her hands gripping the arms of the chair, saw it all spread out beneath her like a map—the roofs of the terraces, the green rectangle of the recreation ground, the glint of the river, the spire of St. Botolph’s. She was, by her own later account, too frightened to scream, and too angry to faint. She observed that the chair was drifting towards the gasworks, and had the presence of mind to lean her weight to the left, which caused it to veer, with a sluggishness that nearly cost her her nerve, towards the softer prospect of the water-meadows.

It was over the recreation ground that the descent began. Whether the powder had exhausted itself, or whether the moisture of the cushion had interfered with the action, Pellow never satisfactorily determined. The chair sank slowly, in a series of gentle lurches, and came to rest in the middle of a game of bowls then in progress between four elderly members of the Marchmont Working Men’s Club, who had watched its approach with the stolid fatalism of Englishmen at leisure. Mrs. Pinnock stepped off, smoothed her apron, and sat down abruptly upon the grass.

Pellow arrived three minutes later, breathless and dishevelled. The crowd had gathered in a ring about the chair, which now lay upon its side, entirely inert, its wickerwork somewhat crushed. Mrs. Pinnock regarded her employer with an eye of cold and level reproach.

“Professor,” she said, “I have been a good servant to you for eleven years.”

“My dear Mrs. Pinnock—”

“I have put up with your smells and your explosions and the time you set fire to the scullery.”

“It was an accident of no consequence—”

“And I will thank you,” she continued, rising and shaking out her skirts, “to give me the eleven and sixpence for the chair, and a shilling for the cushion, and to keep your nasty powders in future where they belong, which is at the bottom of the river.”

There was a murmur of approval from the crowd. Pellow, looking about him at the ring of faces, understood that the moment was not propitious for the exposition of the principle of the gravific insulation. He paid the money. He retrieved the canister from the lawn that evening, and the chair, which he kept in his study as a memento, though Mrs. Pinnock would never again enter the room without a certain darkling glance at the ceiling.

The paper he read before the Royal Society the following spring was received with polite incredulity, and the phenomenon was not repeated. But Hedges the milkman, to the end of his days, would tell the story in the public bar of the King’s Head, and it was his version, embellished by time and beer, that gave rise to the singular legend, still current in Marchmont, of the flying housekeeper of Prince Regent’s Terrace.
