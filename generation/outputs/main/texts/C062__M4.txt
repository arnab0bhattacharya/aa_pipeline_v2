The room was a monument to a world he had spent thirty years denouncing, and Silas Vane sat in the very centre of it, installed in a wing-backed chair of plum-coloured velvet as though it were a throne. The tea things had been cleared, leaving only a decanter of brandy and the faint, sweetish ghost of bergamot in the air. Across from him, on a low settee that seemed designed to make its occupant look small and penitent, sat Miss Cora Bellamy.

She was younger than he had expected—two-and-twenty, perhaps—and she had a plain, serious face that was not beautiful but was, he admitted, remarkably direct. Her dress was expensive but severe, the colour of wet slate, and she wore no jewellery save a small silver brooch at her throat. She had been speaking for some minutes, and Vane had been listening with the practised, receptive stillness that had served him well in a hundred such interviews.

“My father,” she was saying, “made his money in shipping. He was not a cruel man, Mr. Vane, but he was a thorough one. He believed in the ledger and the law and the rightness of his own position. When he died last spring, I found that I could not keep it. The house, the shares, the rents from the tenements in Wapping—it all seemed to me a kind of theft that had been dressed up in a will.”

Vane inclined his head. “And you have read my pamphlets.”

“I have read everything you have written. The first one—‘The Coming Dissolution’—I found in a bookshop in Bloomsbury when I was seventeen. I read it in a single night.” She paused, and for the first time her composure flickered, a faint flush rising in her pale cheeks. “It was like a window being opened in a stuffy room. I had never heard anyone say that the whole apparatus was rotten—not merely in need of reform, but rotten to its foundations. It was the first honest thing I had ever read.”

“You are very kind,” said Vane. He had said it a thousand times, and it came out as smoothly as ever.

“I am not kind,” she said. “I am determined. That is why I have come to you. I wish to give you my fortune. Not to a cause—to you. I have looked into the various societies and committees, and they all seem to me to be merely smaller versions of the thing they oppose. Men in offices, writing letters, quarrelling over minutes. But you have never asked for anything. You have lived as you preached—simply, without compromise. I want you to take the money and use it as you see fit. I trust you to know what to do with it.”

Vane lifted his brandy and drank. The glass was heavy crystal, and it caught the light from the fire, throwing a little constellation of reflections across the table. He thought, with a strange detachment, that he had been waiting for this moment for years without ever quite believing it would come. A wealthy young woman, newly orphaned, burdened by conscience, seeking a man of principle to absolve her. He had written about such people—had, in fact, described them with a certain acid contempt as the “sentimentalists of the drawing-room,” the well-meaning rich who salved their consciences by funding the destruction of their own class. He had never expected to be the recipient of their charity. But here she was, and the sum she had mentioned—she had mentioned it quite plainly, as though it were a weather report—was not inconsiderable. It would keep him in rooms like this, in brandy like this, for a very long time.

“You do me too much honour,” he said, and heard the falseness in his own voice. It was a small thing, a hairline crack, but he noticed it. He had been noticing it more and more lately. “But I must ask you to consider what you are proposing. I am an anarchist, Miss Bellamy. I do not merely wish to redistribute your father’s wealth—I wish to abolish the system that made it possible. If I take your money, I will use it to undermine the very order that protects you.”

“I know,” she said. “That is why I chose you.”

He looked at her. She was leaning forward slightly, her hands clasped in her lap, and her eyes were bright with a kind of fervour that he recognised. He had seen it in his own face, once, in the early days, when he had stood on a platform in a dingy hall in Soho and spoken of the coming revolution. He had believed it then—or he had thought he believed it, which was perhaps the same thing. The words had come easily, and the applause had come easily, and the subscriptions from wealthy sympathisers had come easily too. He had told himself that he was merely using their money against them, that the purity of his ends justified the compromise of his means. But somewhere along the way—he could not say precisely when—the ends had grown dim, and the means had become the whole of his life.

“You are very sure of me,” he said.

“I am sure of what you have written. I am sure of what you have lived.”

“And if I told you that I am not the man you think I am?”

She smiled. It was a small, grave smile, and it did not reach her eyes. “Then I would say that you are being modest, and that modesty is a virtue, but it is not a reason to refuse.”

Vane set down his glass. The fire had burned low, and the room was growing cold. He thought of his rooms in Lambeth—two small, damp rooms above a printer’s shop, furnished with a deal table and a horsehair sofa and a shelf of books that he had not opened in years. He thought of the pamphlets he had written, the speeches he had given, the long, slow accumulation of a reputation for integrity that he had never quite earned. He thought of the money he had taken from people like Cora Bellamy—not directly, never so openly as this, but in small sums, subscriptions, “donations to the cause”—and how he had spent it on rent and brandy and the occasional new coat, telling himself that a man of ideas needed to keep up appearances. He had never believed in anything. He saw that now, with a clarity that was almost peaceful. He had believed in the idea of himself as a man who believed, and that was a different thing entirely.

“Miss Bellamy,” he said, and his voice was quiet, “I cannot take your money.”

She frowned. “I don’t understand. Is it not enough? I can give more—there are properties in Kent that I did not mention—”

“It is not the amount. It is the principle.” He paused, and then he laughed—a short, ugly sound that surprised them both. “That is a fine phrase for a man in my position. The principle. I have lived my whole life on principle, you see. Other people’s principle. Other people’s money. And I have just now discovered that I have none of my own.”

She stared at him. “I don’t believe you.”

“You should. I am telling you the truth, and it is the first true thing I have said in years. I am not an anarchist. I am not anything. I am a man who has been paid to talk, and I have talked very well, and I have taken the money, and I have gone home to my rooms and drunk your health in brandy and told myself that I was doing important work.” He stood up. His legs felt unsteady, and he put a hand on the back of the chair to steady himself. “You should keep your fortune, Miss Bellamy. Give it to the tenements in Wapping, if you like. Or keep it and live comfortably and forget that you ever read my pamphlets. They were not honest. I was not honest. I am not honest now, except by accident.”

She rose too, and for a moment they stood facing each other across the low table, the firelight flickering between them. Her face was pale, and her hands were trembling, but she did not look away.

“You are wrong,” she said. “You are a better man than you think.”

“I am exactly the man I think,” said Vane. “That is the trouble.”

He bowed—a small, formal bow, the gesture of a man who had spent too many years in drawing rooms—and walked to the door. He did not look back. The corridor outside was dim and silent, and the stairs were carpeted so thickly that his footsteps made no sound at all.
