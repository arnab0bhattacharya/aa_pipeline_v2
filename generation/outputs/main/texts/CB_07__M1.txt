I was but three weeks in the parish of Swithens, and already I felt myself a man set upon two sides, with no firm ground for my feet. To the masters of the great mill that smoked and groaned by the river, I was a poor parson, a gentleman of no consequence, whose opinions were as threadbare as his black coat. They received me in their counting-houses with a condescending civility that stung more than any open insult, and dismissed my questions about the health of their workers with talk of production quotas and the price of raw cotton. To the workers themselves, I was the master’s man, a spy sent to soothe their grievances with scripture while their wages were cut and their children coughed their lives away in the dust-filled sheds. They would turn their faces from me in the narrow, soot-choked lanes, their silence a more terrible accusation than any curse.

It was in this state of frustrated and isolated pride that I sought out the parish school. The Rector, a gouty old gentleman who preferred his port at Brierly Hall to the damp air of the valley, had mentioned it as a matter of duty. “A widow, a Mrs. Ainsworth, keeps it,” he had said, waving a plump hand. “An earnest sort of woman. Sees to the little ones. You might call on her. Good for the parish record.”

The school was a single, low-ceilinged room attached to the old wool-drying shed, and the air within was thick with the smell of chalk, damp stone, and the packed bodies of children. And there she was. She was not what I had pictured. I had expected some faded, gentle creature, worn down by poverty and piety. Instead, I found a woman of perhaps thirty-five, with hair the colour of dark honey drawn back in a severe knot, and eyes of a startling, penetrating grey. She was teaching the children a hymn, her voice clear and firm, without a trace of sentimentality. She saw me and paused, her gaze resting on me for a long moment—a gaze that did not flatter, but assessed.

“Mr. Langton,” she said, her tone level. It was not a question. She knew who I was.

“Mrs. Ainsworth. I have come to see the school.”

“You see it.” She gestured to the rows of small, pale faces. “They are the children of the mill. They learn their letters in the morning, and by the afternoon, the older ones are in the shed. What would you see besides?”

Her candour disarmed me. I, who was so accustomed to the veiled animosity of the workers and the bland condescension of the masters, felt as though I had walked into a blast of fresh, cold wind.

“I wished to know if I could be of service,” I stammered, feeling the inadequacy of the words.

She gave a short, humourless laugh. “Service? The Rector sends a new curate, and he speaks of service. The mill-owner, Mr. Croft, speaks of his duty to provide employment. The men speak of their right to a living wage. Every man speaks of his part, and the children go hungry and the fever spreads in the courts. What is your service, Mr. Langton? To read the burial service when they are dead?”

Her words struck me like a physical blow. I felt a hot flush of anger and shame. “Madam,” I said, my voice harder than I intended, “you are quick to judge. I have been here but a short time. I am trying to understand—”

“Understanding is a luxury,” she cut in, her eyes flashing. “Action is a necessity. Did you preach last Sunday on the text, ‘Suffer the little children to come unto me’? I heard you did. It was a fine sermon, I am told. But did you then walk down to the River Court and see the little children who cannot come to you because they are too weak with the flux, or too busy tying threads for Mr. Croft’s profit? Words, Mr. Langton. The Bible is full of them. But it is also full of fire and judgment.”

We stood thus, locked in a silent battle of wills. The children watched us, their hymn forgotten. I saw that I could not match her with righteous indignation, for her indignation was purer, born of a closer acquaintance with suffering than my own. My anger began to recede, replaced by a profound and unsettling shame.

“I am not Croft’s man,” I said at last, the words torn from me. “And I am not the Rector’s puppet. But I confess I do not know what I am. I came here with a heart full of zeal and a head full of sermons, and I find them both useless.”

For the first time, her expression softened. It was not a smile, but a lessening of the severity in her face. “Zeal is a fire, Mr. Langton,” she said, her voice quieter now. “It can warm, or it can consume. It must be tempered with knowledge. You wish to know this parish? Then do not speak to Mr. Croft, or to his foreman. Speak to the women who stand for hours over the steaming dye-vats. Speak to the old man in the almshouse who lost his arm in the machinery and was given a shilling for his trouble. And when you have listened to them, then perhaps you will have a sermon worth the hearing.”

She turned back to the children, her duty clear and immediate. I was dismissed. I walked out of the schoolhouse and into the grey afternoon. The mill whistle shrieked, a long, mournful cry that echoed off the hills. Smoke poured from the tall chimney, a black pillar against the bruised-looking sky. I looked at the rows of grimy, narrow houses, at the figures trudging home from their labour, their shoulders bowed. I had seen it all before, but now, through Mrs. Ainsworth’s eyes, I saw it truly for the first time. It was not a picturesque scene of industry; it was a landscape of injustice.

That evening, I did not write my sermon. Instead, I pulled on my boots and walked down into the stinking labyrinth of the River Court. A bitter wind was rising, and the first drops of a cold rain began to fall. I had no plan, no words of comfort or wisdom. I had only her challenge burning in my mind. I knocked on a door, and when a haggard woman opened it, I did not offer a prayer. I said, simply, “I am the new curate. I have come to listen.”

I did not know where this path would lead, or what good I could do, or if I would be broken by the forces arrayed against me. But as I stood there in the wind and the rain, I felt the first stirring of a purpose that was my own—not borrowed from a book or a bishop, but forged in the fire of that woman’s honest scorn. The storm was gathering, both in the sky and in my own soul, and I knew I must learn to stand within it.
