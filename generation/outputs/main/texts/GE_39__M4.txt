The apothecary’s assistant, Mr. Abel Hartley, came each morning at half-past nine, and the sound of his knock—three strokes, the last two quick together, like a man who would not presume upon a fourth—had come to seem to Ellen Marrable the true beginning of the day, so that the hours before it were but a waiting, and the hours after it a species of aftermath in which the world resumed its common colour. Her mother’s sick-room at the back of the house in Coggles Lane looked out upon the river, and the light that came off the water in the early part of the morning had a tremulous quality, a restless shifting of pale gold and grey, which seemed to Ellen to partake of the uncertainty of all mortal things; and it was in this light that Mr. Hartley’s countenance, always pale and somewhat thin, took on a look of gentle gravity that was not quite melancholy, as though he had schooled himself to expect little and had found the discipline not unkindly.

He was not the doctor, but the doctor’s assistant, a young man of no fortune and of a family obscure—his father had been a schoolmaster in a neighbouring village and was dead—and there were those in Marthorpe who thought it a condescension in Dr. Nunn to employ him, though the doctor himself, a stout and easy man who loved his dinner and his whist, was heard to say that Hartley had more physic in his little finger than half the faculty in their whole bodies. However that might be, it was certain that Mrs. Marrable, who had been ailing since the turn of the year with a slow and wasting disorder of the lungs, found a comfort in his visits that she found in nothing else, and would have it that the very manner of his entering the room, with his quiet step and his careful avoidance of any loud or sudden sound, did her more good than the mixtures and the blistering. Ellen, who had nursed her with a devotion that was the more absolute for being unexamined, had at first regarded him with the suspicion which a careful housekeeper entertains for all members of a profession that must be paid; but by the time the spring had come and the willows along the river had put forth their grey-green catkins, she had ceased to think of him as a person who sent in a bill, and had begun to think of him—she hardly knew how or when—as a person whose coming she desired.

It is a subtle and gradual thing, this transfer of the heart’s allegiance from the abstract to the particular, and it is seldom accompanied by any conscious act of the will. Ellen Marrable was two-and-twenty, and had been for three years the object of a steady and respectable attachment on the part of Mr. Thomas Gedge, a corn-factor of Marthorpe, a man of five-and-thirty, with a red face, a loud laugh, and a comfortable house at the end of the High Street, whose suit was favoured by her father, a retired malster of a sanguine and practical turn, who could see no reason why a girl should not marry a man who could keep her in comfort, and who regarded the languors and refinements of sentiment as things which a well-regulated mind should put away with other childish toys. Mr. Gedge was not an unkind man; he was, indeed, generally esteemed a good fellow, and he had a way of slapping his thigh and crying “Well, Miss Ellen, and how’s the world using you?” which was meant to be hearty, and which Ellen had once thought so. But it is one of the ironies of our nature that the very qualities which commend a man to the world at large may be those which, in the closer air of a sick-room, betray their insufficiency; and Mr. Gedge, who could not enter a room without filling it, who brought with him an atmosphere of malt and merchandise and loud good-humour, and who would say, “Cheer up, ma’am, you’ll soon be about again,” in a voice that made the invalid wince, had come insensibly to seem to Ellen like a noise from the outer world which she was glad to have shut out.

Whereas Mr. Hartley, who spoke low, and who would sit by the window and read to her mother from the “Quarterly” or from a volume of Cowper, and who had a way of looking at the patient with a steady, considering gaze that seemed to take in not only her symptoms but her whole condition, had come to be associated in Ellen’s mind with all that was quiet and faithful and sustaining. She had begun to watch for the three strokes of his knock; she had begun to arrange the little matters of the room—the placing of the chair, the folding of the counterpane—with a view to his coming; and she had begun, though she would have been shocked to own it, to think of what he might be thinking of her, and to wonder whether the faint colour that came into his cheek when she spoke to him was the effect of the morning air or of something more personal.

The summer came on, and with it a change in Mrs. Marrable that was not for the better. Dr. Nunn shook his head and spoke of the necessity of keeping up the strength; Mr. Hartley came twice a day, and sometimes in the evening; and Ellen, who had slept for weeks in a chair by her mother’s bed, grew pale and thin, and yet found in her fatigue a strange exaltation, as though she were being admitted into a fellowship of suffering that had its own consolations. It was in these weeks that she and Mr. Hartley came to speak to one another of matters that had no direct relation to the case—of the river and its moods, of the old church and its brasses, of a book he had been reading on the natural history of the county, and of a certain disposition of mind which he confessed to have, and which he called “a habit of looking too far ahead,” and which she, with a smile that was almost tender, said she believed to be common to all careful people. And once, when her mother was sleeping and they sat together at the window, he said, with a hesitancy that was new in him, that he had often thought that the truest kindness a man could do himself was to expect little, since expectation was but another name for the bondage of the will to the future; and she answered that she supposed it depended upon what one expected, for that some things were worth the bondage. He looked at her then with a look that made her heart beat so that she was obliged to turn away, and there was a silence between them that was more eloquent than any speech.

But the world outside the sick-room had not been idle. Mr. Gedge, who had been patient for a long time, began to be importunate; he spoke to Mr. Marrable, and Mr. Marrable spoke to Ellen, with a bluntness that was not unkindly, but that left no room for the finer shades of feeling. “Gedge is a good man, and he’ll be wanting an answer,” said the old malster. “You can’t keep him dangling forever, and your mother—well, your mother may be taken from us, and then where will you be? A woman wants a home, Ellen.” Ellen listened, and said nothing, and went back to her mother’s room with a heart that was divided against itself. For she knew that her father spoke the truth as the world reckons truth; she knew that Mr. Gedge would give her a home and a position and a comfortable life; and she knew, too, with a knowledge that was like a cold hand upon her heart, that she had let herself feel for Mr. Hartley what she had no right to feel, and that the very secrecy of her feeling was a kind of treachery to the honest man who had waited for her so long. And yet, when the three strokes came at the door the next morning, and she heard the familiar step in the passage, she rose to meet him with a sense of relief that was like the breaking of a fever, and she knew that whatever the future might hold, the present moment was his, and that she could not, if she would, have had it otherwise.

The autumn brought a change. Mrs. Marrable, who had seemed to be sinking, rallied a little, and the doctor spoke of a mild winter and the possibility of a recovery that should be at least a prolongation of life. And with the rallying of the mother came a relaxation of the strain that had bound the daughter to the sick-room, and with that relaxation came a clearer vision of her own position. She saw herself, as it were, from a little distance, and the sight was not altogether pleasing. She had allowed a stranger—for so she must call him, since he was not of her family or her father’s choosing—to become the centre of her thoughts; she had fed a feeling that could lead to nothing but disappointment; and she had done this while a good man, who had a right to expect her fidelity, was waiting for her answer. It is a hard thing to be just to oneself in such a case, and Ellen Marrable was not one of those who can take refuge in the notion that feeling is its own justification. She was a woman of a scrupulous conscience, and she had been bred in a school that held duty to be the first of the virtues; and she began to think that the truest kindness she could do to Mr. Hartley was to see him less often, and to the memory of what had passed between them to consign it to that region of silent things which are not the less real for being unspoken.

But destiny, which, as the wiser among us have observed, stands by with our dramatis personae folded in her hand, had not done with Ellen Marrable. One evening in October, when the river was running high after the rains and the light in the west was a pale and troubled gold, Mr. Hartley came to the house at an hour when he was not expected, and asked to see her alone. She received him in the little parlour, where the fire was burning low and the clock ticked with a solemnity that seemed to her to be measuring out the last moments of her peace. He stood before her with his hat in his hand, and his face was paler than she had ever seen it, and his voice, when he spoke, was low and steady, as though he had considered his words for a long time and had resolved to utter them without faltering.

“Miss Marrable,” he said, “I have no right to speak to you as I am going to speak, and I have told myself so a hundred times; but I have a feeling that if I do not speak now I shall never speak at all, and that the silence would be a worse wrong than the speech. I am a poor man, and I have nothing to offer you but a life of care and a heart that is wholly yours. I know that there is another who has a better claim, and I know that I am putting myself in the way of a rebuke; but I could not let the year go by without telling you that I have thought of you by day and by night, and that the thought of you has been the one light in a life that has not many lights.”

Ellen stood before him with her hands clasped and her eyes cast down, and for a moment the room and the river and the ticking clock seemed to be far away, and there was nothing in the world but the sound of his voice and the beating of her own heart.
