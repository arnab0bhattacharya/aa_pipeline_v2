MR. AUGUSTUS POMEROY, a gentleman of no fortune and no particular prospects, had been manufactured, as it were, out of the raw material of a clever orphan boy, by the great and wealthy Sir Godfrey Blackwood. And a very excellent job of manufacturing it was; for Mr. Pomeroy was now, at the age of two-and-twenty, the finished article. He could converse in French upon the subject of the weather, in Latin upon the subject of the ancients, and in English upon the subject of anything and everything with a languid grace that was the envy of less carefully constructed young men. He could ride a horse, play upon the pianoforte, and carve a fowl, all with that air of effortless superiority which is the very hallmark of a good education and a full stomach.

The scene of this successful creation was Blackwood Manor, a great sprawling house in the county of Shropshire, which stood upon its lawn like a well-fed alderman, proud of its girth and certain of its consequence. The lawns were greener than any lawn had a right to be, the gravel was raked with mathematical precision, and the very roses, it seemed to Augustus, bloomed with an educated blush. Here, surrounded by every comfort, every luxury, every refinement that Sir Godfrey’s immense fortune could provide, Augustus Pomeroy had been raised from the age of seven, a grateful and devoted ward to his magnificent benefactor.

Sir Godfrey himself was a man of vast dimensions and vaster opinions. His voice was like the booming of a friendly cannon, and his laugh, which was frequent, could be heard in the stables. He was a great patron of the arts, a great friend to the government, and a great believer in the invincible might of British commerce. “Commerce, my boy!” he would thunder, clapping Augustus on the shoulder with a hand like a leg of mutton, “Commerce is the engine of the world! It is what separates us from the savages! It builds bridges, it forges iron, it carries civilization to the farthest corners of the globe! Never you forget that.”

And Augustus, who had been fed, clothed, and taught by the fruits of that very commerce, never did forget it. He looked upon Sir Godfrey with a devotion that was as warm and as unquestioning as a spaniel’s. He was to be Sir Godfrey’s private secretary, his companion, the future steward of his estates and his vast, mysterious investments. His life was a beautifully paved road, stretching out before him into a golden future.

The first crack in this perfect pavement appeared not in Shropshire, but in the roaring, rattling, smoky heart of London, where Augustus had been sent for a week to attend to some business of Sir Godfrey’s. The business was with a firm of solicitors, and it was in their dusty, paper-choked office that the world tilted on its axis. The senior partner, a Mr. Grubb, a man whose face seemed to have been dried and pressed between the pages of a legal tome, was explaining the intricacies of the Blackwood estate.

“A most robust portfolio, most robust,” Mr. Grubb had droned, his finger tracing a column of figures. “The sugar plantations in the West Indies, you see, form the very bedrock. The bedrock upon which the whole edifice is built. Yields have been most satisfactory, most satisfactory indeed, since the… ahem… since the legislative changes.”

Augustus, who had been thinking of a new waistcoat he wished to order, felt a sudden, unaccountable chill. “The plantations?” he inquired, his voice sounding thin and far away. “I was not aware Sir Godfrey held such interests.”

Mr. Grubb peered at him over his spectacles. “Held? My dear sir, he is the principal shareholder in the Blackwood & Sons Sugar Company. Why, his grandfather, the first Sir Godfrey, established the concern. It is the source, the fountainhead, of the entire family fortune. Everything—the Manor, the town-house in Grosvenor Square, the funds that have provided for your own education, Mr. Pomeroy—every shilling of it has its origin in the sweat of those plantations.”

The room seemed to contract. The ticking of a great clock on the mantelpiece grew loud and menacing, each tick a hammer-blow upon his conscience. The word ‘plantation’ was not an abstraction to Augustus; he had read the pamphlets, the fiery speeches, the grim accounts in the abolitionist papers. He saw not columns of figures, but rows of men, women, and children, bent double under a burning sun. He saw not ‘yields,’ but the lash, the chain, the branded flesh. And he saw that the very coat upon his back, the very ring upon his finger, was purchased with that agony.

He returned to Blackwood Manor a different man, or rather, a man with a terrible ghost living inside him. The polished floors seemed to groan with the weight of unseen captives. The silver on the sideboard glowed with a sinister, blood-warmed light. When Sir Godfrey clapped him on the back, Augustus flinched as if struck.

“You are pale, my boy,” Sir Godfrey boomed, his face a mask of jovial concern. “London has taken the colour from your cheeks! Too much dissipation, eh? A young man’s folly! We shall soon set you right with some of my excellent port.”

That evening, in the library, a room of leather-bound books and comfortable silence, Augustus could bear it no longer. The fire crackled, and Sir Godfrey sat in his great armchair, a picture of benevolent power.

“Sir Godfrey,” Augustus began, his hands trembling. “I have been speaking with Mr. Grubb.”

“Ah, Grubb! A dry old stick, but a sound one. Knows every penny I possess to the last farthing. What did he have to say?”

“He spoke of the sugar plantations,” Augustus said, the words tasting of ash.

Sir Godfrey’s smile did not falter, but his eyes, for a moment, grew hard as flint. “Indeed. A most profitable concern. The very foundation of all this,” he said, with a grand, sweeping gesture that took in the room, the house, the world. “The foundation of your world too, Augustus. Remember that.”

“But Sir Godfrey,” Augustus pressed on, desperation lending him a courage he did not know he possessed, “the conditions… the men and women who work them… it is a trade in human souls! How can we, how can *you*, live upon such a fortune?”

For a long moment, Sir Godfrey was silent. He rose from his chair and stood before the fire, his great shadow looming over Augustus. When he spoke, his voice was no longer a friendly boom, but the low, dangerous growl of a mastiff defending its bone.

“Souls? Do not speak to me of souls in the counting-house of commerce! I deal in sugar, and in rum, and in profit! It is a business, sir, a great and glorious British business! Do you think this house was built on sentiment? Do you think the roads of this country were paved with good intentions? No! They were paved with enterprise! With capital! With the sweat of those who are fitted by Providence for labour, and directed by men like me for the good of all! It is the natural order of things.”

He turned on Augustus, his face purple with indignation. “And you! You, who have eaten my bread, worn my cloth, and learned your Latin from my tutors! You, who are a product of this very enterprise, dare to stand in my house and question the source of your own fine breeding? It is the height of ingratitude! The most brazen hypocrisy!”

The tirade went on, a torrent of self-justification and fury. But Augustus did not hear it. He saw only the truth, stark and terrible. His life, his education, his very gentility, were not a gift of kindness, but the polished coins paid for a monstrous crime. He was a gentleman, but his gentlemanly existence was a lie.

The next morning, before the sun had fully risen, Augustus Pomeroy walked out of Blackwood Manor. He carried no bundle; the coat on his back and the money in his pocket were tainted, and he left them behind with the rest of his life. He walked down the long, perfect driveway, the gravel crunching under his worn-out boots. He did not look back at the great house, that monument to a fortune built on chains. He walked towards the rising sun, a young man with nothing but an education and a principle to his name, and for the first time in his life, he felt truly, terribly, and magnificently free.
