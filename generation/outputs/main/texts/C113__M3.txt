The fever took Mr. Locke on the third Sunday after his arrival. One moment he stood in the draughty pulpit of St. Aidan’s, exhorting the sparse congregation of Dunwich-in-the-Moor to vigilance against the devil’s snares; the next, he was gripping the oak lectern, his face the colour of skimmed milk, his breath coming in short, rattling gasps. By the time the parish clerk had helped him down the steps, the fever had fully claimed him, turning his skin to a furnace and his words to a delirious, Latin-strewn babble.

The vicarage was damp, half-furnished, and entirely unfit for a sick man. There was no housekeeper, as the parish was too poor to provide one. It was Thomas Marsden, a yeoman farmer whose pew was always the first on the left, who made the decision. He lifted the young curate into the back of his hay cart, wrapped him in a horse blanket, and drove him the two miles across the rutted moorland track to High Ridge Farm.

For the first week, Mr. Locke knew nothing of his surroundings. He drifted through a burning haze, aware only of the bitter taste of willow bark tea and the cool, calloused hand that pressed a damp cloth to his forehead. The typhus was upon him, a lingering visitor that respected neither rank nor education.

It was Thomas’s daughter, Martha, who became his primary nurse. At twenty-two, she had the sturdy build of a woman accustomed to lifting kettles and chasing wayward sheep across the heather. She had never left the valley of Dunwich. The furthest she had ever travelled was to the market town of Ashford, eight miles south, and that had been when she was twelve. Her world was bounded by the grey stone walls of the farm, the bleating of ewes, and the turning of the seasons. She could read, after a fashion, taught painstakingly by her late mother, but she possessed no books of her own.

When the fever broke, Mr. Locke awoke to a low-ceilinged room smelling of woodsmoke and dried lavender. He was terribly weak, his limbs feeling as though they belonged to someone else. He turned his head on the rough linen pillow and saw Martha sitting by the small, diamond-paned window. She was entirely absorbed in a book.

It was a volume of Cowper’s poetry, one of the few books Locke had brought with him in his trunk, which Thomas had fetched from the vicarage. Martha was tracing the words with a chapped index finger, her lips moving silently. The late afternoon sun caught the dust motes dancing around her, gilding her plain, homespun dress.

‘Miss Marsden,’ he croaked.

She jumped, dropping the book, which fell with a soft thud onto the flagged floor. A flush spread rapidly from her neck to her roots. ‘I beg your pardon, sir. I only meant to sit with you, as Father said. I did not mean to pry.’

‘It is no intrusion,’ Locke said, managing a weak smile. ‘Is it the Cowper? Do you like it?’

She retrieved the book, holding it awkwardly, as if it were a fragile bird. ‘I find it strange, sir. The words are familiar, but their arrangement is… heavy. I cannot always catch the meaning.’

‘Read it aloud,’ Locke suggested. ‘Poetry is meant for the ear, not just the eye.’

Hesitantly, she began to read. Her voice was unpractised, carrying the broad, flattened vowels of the Derbyshire hills. She stumbled over the metre, pausing in the wrong places. Yet, as she continued, a certain music emerged from her voice, a grounded, earthy rhythm that lent the verses an unexpected gravity. Locke lay back and listened, closing his eyes. He had read the poem a dozen times in the drawing rooms of Oxford, but he had never truly heard it until now.

Over the following weeks, as the autumn deepened and the bracken on the moors turned a violent, rusting orange, Locke slowly convalesced. He was confined to the farmhouse, too weak to walk further than the kitchen hearth. Martha brought him his meals—broths thick with barley, fresh rye bread, and sharp, crumbly cheese—but she also brought him his trunk.

Each evening, when the farm chores were done and Thomas had fallen asleep in his chair by the fire, Martha would sit with the curate. He began to lend her books. First, a simple history, then a collection of essays, and finally, a novel. 

Martha’s mind, starved of sustenance for so long, devoured them. She read with an intensity that startled Locke. She did not merely absorb the narratives; she interrogated them. When she read of the sea, she demanded he describe the sound of waves breaking, a concept entirely foreign to her landlocked existence. When she encountered philosophical arguments on the nature of the soul, she applied them to the harsh realities of life and death on the moors, where lambs froze in spring snows and farmers were broken by their labour.

‘You say, sir, that this author believes suffering ennobles the spirit,’ she said one evening, her dark eyes reflecting the firelight as she looked up from a battered copy of *The Spectator*. ‘But I have seen suffering, and it mostly just makes people tired and bitter. My father’s back is bent, and his spirit is worn down by the rent, not ennobled. Is it only the suffering of the wealthy that counts for something?’

Locke was taken aback by her directness, but also profoundly moved. ‘You may be right, Martha,’ he admitted. ‘Perhaps the essayist wrote from a position of comfort. You teach me to read him differently.’

She looked at him, a sudden, unguarded vulnerability in her expression. ‘I teach you? I am only a farmer’s daughter.’

‘And I am only a parish curate, indebted to a farmer’s daughter for my life,’ he replied softly.

The shift between them was gradual, unspoken, and dangerous. Locke found himself anticipating the evenings not for the relief of his physical pain, but for the sharpness of her mind. He watched her hands as she turned the pages—hands that could wring a goose’s neck and milk a cow, yet handled the paper with exquisite tenderness. He noticed the way a stray lock of dark hair always fell across her brow when she was concentrating, and how she impatiently tucked it back.

For Martha, the curate had ceased to be a man of the cloth, an alien creature dropped onto the moors from a world of colleges and cathedrals. He had become her window. Through him, the vast, terrifying, and beautiful world beyond the Derbyshire stone walls had rushed in. She began to see the moors not as a prison, but as a province of a much larger empire of thought. 

One night in late November, a fierce gale rattled the windowpanes, and the wind howled down the chimney like a trapped beast. Thomas had gone to bed, and the farm was quiet save for the storm. Martha sat reading *Paradise Lost*. She struggled with Milton’s dense syntax, her brow furrowed.

‘“Of Man’s first disobedience…”’ she read, her voice dropping into the natural, sombre cadence of the moorland wind. She stopped, looking at Locke, who was sitting up in a chair for the first time, wrapped in a heavy woollen coat. ‘Sir, why did God wish for them to remain ignorant? Would it not have been more merciful to let them see the world as it truly is, even if it brought them pain?’

Locke looked into the fire. He thought of his own comfortable, unexamined life before the fever. He thought of the rigid expectations of his bishop, the dull round of services he had left behind. He had come to Dunwich believing he was bringing light to the dark places. He had not expected to find a fire already burning in the hearth of High Ridge Farm.

‘I do not know, Martha,’ he said, his voice barely above a whisper. ‘Perhaps the knowledge was always meant to be sought, not granted. Perhaps the disobedience was the very thing that made them human.’

Martha closed the book, resting her hands on the leather cover. She looked at him, and in the quiet, smoky room, the distance between the Oxford scholar and the Derbyshire farmer’s daughter collapsed. He reached out, his hand still thin from illness, and placed it over hers. She did not pull away. The wind screamed against the stones of the farmhouse, but inside, by the fire, there was only the sound of shared breath, and the profound, terrifying realization that neither of them would ever be quite the same again.
