The announcement, which Mr. Hollingsworth had chosen to make upon a Sunday evening when the family party was assembled in the great drawing-room at Wrenfield Hall, was delivered with all the composure of a man who had long since settled the matter in his own mind, and who derived no small satisfaction from the contemplation of its effects upon others. He was a gentleman of seventy, of a vigorous constitution and an irascible temper, whose fortune, accumulated through three generations of prudent merchants and one of fortunate marriages, was generally understood to be considerable; and whose expectations, during the last fifteen years, had been the peculiar care of every relation within the circle of his acquaintance. He had, he said, observed with regret the assiduity of his nephews and nieces, and the frequency with which his health was enquired after by persons who had never before evinced the smallest solicitude concerning it; and he had therefore determined, while still in possession of his faculties, to bestow his property upon a charitable institution for the relief of decayed musicians, a class of men for whom he had always entertained a particular esteem, his own father having been a performer upon the violoncello.

The effect of this communication may be more easily imagined than described. Mrs. Cressingham, his eldest niece, a lady of a certain age and a very determined manner, was the first to recover herself sufficiently to observe that she trusted her dear uncle would not be precipitate; that the claims of relationship, however humble, were not to be lightly set aside; and that she was persuaded, when he came to reflect upon the matter, he would see the propriety of consulting the interests of those who were, after all, his natural heirs. Mr. Cressingham, her husband, a gentleman of small estate and large expectations, remarked with a forced smile that charity began at home, and that he had no doubt his uncle was merely pleased to exercise his wit at their expense. The younger nephews and nieces said nothing, but looked at one another with an expression which spoke volumes; and Miss Hollingsworth, a maiden lady of fifty, who had lived at the Hall for twenty years and considered herself indispensable to its comfort, was heard to declare, in a voice tremulous with emotion, that she had never expected to be treated so unkindly by a brother whom she had ever loved and honoured.

The only person in the room who received the intelligence with any appearance of composure was Miss Eleanor Vane, a young lady of nineteen, who had been left an orphan at an early age, and whose father, a clergyman of small preferment, had been the particular friend of Mr. Hollingsworth in his youth. She had been brought to Wrenfield at the age of twelve, and had resided there ever since, in a position which it would be difficult to define, being neither a daughter nor a servant, but something between the two; and she had long since learned to expect nothing from her guardian's fortune, and to be grateful for the shelter of his roof. She said nothing, therefore, but continued to ply her needle with an assiduity which might have been mistaken for indifference; and it was perhaps this very composure which first attracted the notice of Mr. Hollingsworth, who, having expected to see her as much dismayed as the rest, was pleased to observe that she alone had the good sense to accept his determination without a murmur.

The weeks which followed were not calculated to raise the family in the estimation of any impartial observer. Mrs. Cressingham, who had previously been in the habit of visiting her uncle twice a year, now came every fortnight, bringing with her such delicacies as were most likely to tempt the appetite of an invalid, and reading to him from the newspapers with an assiduity which was evidently the result of a determination to be useful. Mr. Cressingham, who had never been known to spend a morning in the house, now discovered a sudden taste for the conversation of his uncle, and would sit with him for hours, discussing the price of corn and the state of the roads, with an air of profound interest which deceived nobody. The younger nephews and nieces wrote letters of affectionate enquiry, which were read aloud at the breakfast-table with an emphasis that betrayed a hope of their being understood as sincere. Even Miss Hollingsworth, who had been in the habit of complaining of her brother's temper, now discovered that he was the best of men, and that she had always said so.

Mr. Hollingsworth received these attentions with a gravity which was not without its humour. He was not a man of much penetration, but he was not without a certain shrewdness in matters which concerned his own comfort; and he had long since learned to distinguish between the solicitude of interest and the solicitude of affection. He said nothing, however, but continued to receive his visitors with the same civility as before, and to listen to their conversation with an air of polite attention which might have been mistaken for approbation.

It was in the third week of this period that Miss Vane, who had been in the habit of walking to the village every morning for the purpose of visiting a sick woman, returned to the Hall one day in a state of some agitation. She had met with an accident, she said, upon the road; her ankle had been turned by a stone, and she had been obliged to sit down upon a stile until she could recover herself. She had not been there above ten minutes, when a carriage had stopped, and a gentleman had alighted, and had offered her his assistance. He had been very civil, she said, and had insisted upon conveying her home; but she had declined, not wishing to give trouble, and had walked back slowly, leaning upon her parasol.

Mr. Hollingsworth, who had listened to this account with more interest than he usually displayed in the affairs of his household, enquired the name of the gentleman; but Miss Vane could not inform him, having forgotten to ask, and having been too much occupied with her own situation to attend to the particulars of his appearance. She believed, however, that he had spoken of being on his way to Norwich, and that he had mentioned the name of a house in that neighbourhood which she had not before heard.

The circumstance, trifling as it was, appeared to make an impression upon Mr. Hollingsworth, who was pleased to observe that Miss Vane had conducted herself with propriety, and that he was glad to find she had not been forward to accept the civilities of a stranger. He said nothing more at the time; but it was remarked by the family that he was more than usually silent during the remainder of the day, and that he looked at Miss Vane once or twice with an expression which they could not interpret.

The following morning, Miss Vane received a note from her guardian, requesting her attendance in the library. She found him seated at his writing-table, with a newspaper in his hand, and a countenance which betrayed a greater degree of emotion than she had ever before observed in him. He desired her to sit down; and then, after a pause of some moments, he informed her that he had that morning received a letter from a gentleman of his acquaintance, Mr. John Ashworth, of Ashworth Court, in the county of Norfolk, who had been pleased to express himself in terms of the highest approbation of her conduct upon the occasion of the accident which had befallen her; and that he had further requested permission to be introduced to her, with a view to a more particular acquaintance.

Miss Vane, who was not without a proper sense of her own situation, and who had long since learned to distrust the professions of those who had no interest in her welfare, received this communication with a modesty which did her credit. She said that she was much obliged to Mr. Ashworth for his good opinion, but that she could not flatter herself that she had done anything to deserve it; and that she hoped her guardian would not think her ungrateful if she declined an acquaintance which could only be attended with inconvenience to all parties.

Mr. Hollingsworth, who had expected a very different reply, was pleased to find his ward possessed of a prudence which he had not looked for in one of her years. He said that he had not been in the habit of consulting her inclinations upon such matters, but that he was glad to find she was not inclined to be forward; and that he should inform Mr. Ashworth that she was sensible of his politeness, but that she was not at present disposed to enlarge the circle of her acquaintance.

The family, who had by some means or other become acquainted with the circumstance, were not a little disappointed at this result. Mrs. Cressingham observed that it was easy to see what Miss Vane was about; that she had no doubt the whole affair of the sprained ankle had been contrived for the purpose of attracting the notice of a gentleman of fortune; and that she was surprised her uncle should be so blind as not to perceive it. Mr. Cressingham said that he had always thought Miss Vane a designing young woman, and that he was sorry to find his opinion confirmed. Miss Hollingsworth declared that she had never liked the girl, and that she had always said she would come to no good.

Mr. Hollingsworth, who was not without a certain sense of justice, was pleased to observe that these remarks were made in his presence, and that they were not calculated to raise the speakers in his estimation. He said nothing, however, but continued to treat Miss Vane with the same civility as before; and when, upon the following Sunday, she enquired whether she might be permitted to visit her sick woman at the village, he gave his consent with a readiness which surprised the family, and desired her to take the carriage, as the roads were bad, and he did not wish her to fatigue herself.

The family, who had hoped to see her disgraced, were disappointed. They had not, however, long to wait for a more decisive proof of their uncle's intentions. Upon the following Wednesday, Mr. Hollingsworth desired the attendance of his relations in the library, and informed them, with a composure which was not without its effect, that he had determined to make a codicil to his will, by which he should leave the sum of five thousand pounds to Miss Vane, as a testimony of his approbation of her conduct, and the remainder of his fortune to the institution for decayed musicians, as he had before intended. He added that he had no doubt they would receive this intelligence with the resignation which became them, and that he should be glad to see them at dinner as usual.

The family received this communication with a consternation which they in vain endeavoured to conceal. Mrs. Cressingham was understood to say that she had never been so treated in her life; Mr. Cressingham observed that he had always expected something of the kind; and Miss Hollingsworth declared that she should never hold up her head again. They were, however, too much afraid of losing the five thousand pounds, which they still hoped might be diverted into their own channel, to offer any open resistance; and they accordingly sat down to dinner with an air of injured innocence which was not without its effect upon the company.

Miss Vane, who had received the intelligence with a surprise which she did not attempt to conceal, was the only person who ventured to express her sense of her guardian's goodness. She said that she was sensible of his kindness, but that she could not accept a sum which was so much needed by others; and that she hoped he would permit her to decline it. Mr. Hollingsworth, who was pleased to find his ward possessed of a disinterestedness which he had not looked for, desired her to say no more; and observed that he had never been in the habit of having his favours refused, and that he should be glad if she would permit him to dispose of his property as he thought proper.
