The heat of that infernal afternoon seemed to have entered the very substance of the courthouse, to have steeped the whitewashed walls and the dark, polished benches in a kind of moral lassitude, so that the air itself felt heavy with the accumulated weariness of ships and men and the endless, patient sea. Captain Elias Ffoulkes, his hands clasped loosely before him on the scarred rail of the witness table, watched the proceedings with a fixed and seemingly impassive countenance, though a slow, sick tremor, like the shudder of a ship’s frame when the ground-swell strikes her, ran through his whole body. He had given his evidence, had heard his own voice, dry and remote as a stranger’s, pronounce the words that condemned him, and now he waited, in a silence that roared in his ears, for the next act of this dreary, inexorable drama.

The room was full, for the loss of the *Semiramis* on the Tubbataha Reefs had been a nine days’ wonder in the port, a subject for much grave head-shaking in the offices of the Straits Steamship Company and for much shrill speculation among the floating population of the waterfront. He was aware of the faces turned towards him, some curious, some commiserating, and a few, he fancied, alight with a veiled and shameful satisfaction. He knew them all, these men of the Eastern trade, with their sun-peeled noses and their air of weary competence, and he knew that in their hearts they had already judged him, not for the error of judgment, not for the fatal confidence in a dead reckoning, but for the failure, the final and unforgivable failure, of a master to bring his ship to her haven. He had been a careful man, a sober man, a man of the old school, who held that a master’s place was on his bridge and his soul in his keeping; and now, in a single night of rain and murk, of a current miscalculated and a light misjudged, he was become a byword, a thing to be pointed at.

The President of the Court, a spare, grey, desiccated man with the eyes of a hawk and the voice of a tired schoolmaster, shuffled his papers with a dry, rustling sound that seemed to fill the vast, still room. He leaned forward, his gaze passing over Ffoulkes as though he were a piece of furniture, and spoke a name.

“Mr. Archibald Crane.”

Ffoulkes felt the name strike him like a physical blow, a dull, heavy impact in the region of the heart. He did not turn his head, but his eyes, fixed upon the far wall with its map of the Archipelago, seemed to see, with a terrible distinctness, the figure that now rose from the back of the court and made its way to the witness box. He heard the measured tread of the man’s boots on the bare boards, the subdued murmur that ran through the crowd, the sharp, indrawn breath of someone near him. And he knew, with a certainty that was like a cold hand laid upon his soul, that the next few moments would decide not his professional fate—for that, he was well aware, was already sealed—but something more precious, something more fragile, the truth of a man’s life, the loyalty of a friend.

Crane was a younger man, thirty perhaps, with a fair, open face and a habit of looking one straight in the eye, a quality which Ffoulkes had always valued in him and which now he dreaded with a sickening intensity. He had been the second mate of the *Semiramis*, a man of a different generation, educated in the new school of steam and iron, with his certificates and his theories and his brisk, impatient ways. They had not always agreed. Ffoulkes, a sailor of the old windjammer tradition, had found the young man’s reliance on the patent log and the lead-line a thing almost indecent, a substitution of machinery for the seaman’s God-given instinct; and Crane, he knew, had looked upon his captain’s caution as a kind of timorousness, his seamanship as a relic of a bygone age. Yet there had been between them a bond of mutual respect, a tacit acknowledgment of each other’s worth, and Ffoulkes, in the bitter hours since the wreck, had clung to the memory of that bond as a drowning man clings to a spar.

Crane took his place in the box, his hand raised, his voice firm and clear as he repeated the oath. He looked neither at Ffoulkes nor at the President, but straight before him, at the great, dusty window through which the afternoon light came slanting, thick and yellow, full of motes.

“You were on watch, Mr. Crane, on the night of the twenty-first?” the President began, in his dry, toneless voice.

“I was, sir.”

“And what was the weather?”

“Thick, sir. Rain, squalls, and a heavy sea running. The wind was variable, backing and veering, and the current, we afterward found, was setting us to the eastward more than was allowed for.”

Ffoulkes listened, his heart a stone. The young man’s voice was steady, unemotional, the voice of a man giving evidence upon a matter of mere technical interest. He described the log, the soundings, the appearance of the sky, the feel of the wind, with a precision that was almost brutal in its clarity. He spoke of the captain’s orders, of his own misgivings, of the moment when he had ventured to suggest to Mr. Ffoulkes that the ship was nearer the land than her position indicated. Ffoulkes, hearing the words, felt the scene re-enacted in his mind with a vividness that was like a physical pain: the dark, reeling bridge, the sting of the rain, the young mate’s voice, hesitant yet insistent, saying, “I don’t like the look of it, sir. The smell of the land is strong.” And his own voice, he remembered, had been curt, dismissive, the voice of a man who has borne the burden of command for thirty years and will not be taught his business by a boy.

“And what did Captain Ffoulkes say to that?” the President asked, his pen poised.

Crane was silent for a moment. The stillness in the court was absolute. Ffoulkes felt the blood drumming in his temples. He did not look at the mate, but he was aware of him, of the slight, almost imperceptible movement of his shoulders, of the way his hands, resting on the rail of the box, had clenched themselves into fists. He knew, with a sudden, sickening prescience, what was coming. The young man would tell the truth, the whole truth, and nothing but the truth, as he was sworn to do, and the truth would damn him utterly, would strip him of the last shred of dignity, would hold him up to the contempt of his fellows as a man who had been warned and would not listen.

“Captain Ffoulkes,” Crane said at last, and his voice was lower now, almost inaudible, “was of the opinion that the set of the current was different from what I supposed. He told me to go forward and keep a sharp lookout, and to call him if I saw anything.”

It was not a lie. It was, in its way, a kind of truth, but a truth so pared down, so delicately balanced, so shorn of all its damning context, that it was more damning still. It suggested a captain who had listened, who had considered, who had taken what he believed to be adequate precautions. It left out the impatience, the contempt, the fatal over-confidence. It was a loyal man’s answer, the answer of a man who, having the power to destroy, had chosen, for reasons of his own, to spare.

Ffoulkes felt the tension go out of him in a long, shuddering sigh. He closed his eyes for a moment, and in the darkness behind them he saw, not the wreck, not the court, but the face of his young mate as he had seen it that night on the bridge, white and strained in the gleam of the binnacle light, and he understood, with a clarity that was almost unbearable, the magnitude of what Crane had done. He had not lied. He had simply, in the face of a merciless inquiry, allowed the truth to be obscured by the mists of a generous and deliberate reticence. He had given him the benefit of the doubt, the shadow of a doubt, and in doing so he had placed his own integrity, his own honour, in jeopardy.

The President was writing, his pen scratching across the paper with a dry, inexorable sound. The court was stirring, the tension broken, the faces turning to one another with whispered comments. Ffoulkes opened his eyes and looked at Crane. The young man had stepped down from the box and was making his way back through the crowd, his head held high, his face pale but composed. As he passed the table where Ffoulkes sat, he paused for an instant, and their eyes met. There was no triumph in the young man’s gaze, no reproach, only a kind of grave and sorrowful acknowledgment, as of one who has performed a painful duty and is glad that it is done.

Ffoulkes did not speak. He could not. He merely inclined his head, a barely perceptible movement, and the young man passed on, out of the court, into the blinding, yellow glare of the afternoon. The captain remained where he was, staring at the empty witness box, while the President’s voice droned on, pronouncing the inevitable finding, the suspension, the ruin of a career. But the words fell upon him like rain upon a stone, for he was thinking of something else altogether, of a truth that had been spared and a loyalty that had been given, unasked and undeserved, and of the strange, unaccountable ways in which men, in the midst of their errors and their failings, may yet stumble upon a kind of grace.
