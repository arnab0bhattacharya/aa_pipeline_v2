The fever had taken hold of him somewhere in the slow, grinding ascent through the lower valleys, climbing into his blood with a stealth that was almost impeccable. He had meant to cross the great mountains like a bolt discharged from the dark, driven interiors of the Empire—sealed in a railway carriage, bearing forged papers in his breast pocket, bearing death in his soul. But the human body, that frail and treacherous mechanism of flesh, has a profound and obscure logic of its own, revolting against the abstract purposes of the mind. By the time the diligence had lurched into the deep snow of the final pass, he was shaking with a violent, icy rigor, his vision swimming with the white, blinding glare of the peaks. He retained only a fleeting, nightmarish impression of being lifted from the wooden seat by arms that smelled of damp wool and pine resin, of being carried into a sudden warmth that felt like the very antithesis of his mission—the antithesis of the dark, absolute zero that had resided in his heart since they had given him the weapon in that shadowed room in Petersburg.

He had expected to die in the snow. It would have been a fitting, unremarked conclusion; an insignificant fate swallowed by the colossal indifference of the mountains. He was a man who had been fashioned into a tool, an instrument of historical necessity, stripped of personal desires to the point where his own existence seemed to him a mere spectral illusion. But he did not die. The warmth of the low-ceilinged chalet closed over him like a heavy, suffocating blanket, and through the delirium that followed—days or weeks, he could not tell—he was conscious of a ministrative presence that baffled his ideological comprehension. It was not the calculated efficiency of a hospital, but a clumsy, unceasing, and profound devotion.

When at last the fever broke, leaving him hollow, transparent, and immeasurably weak, he opened his eyes to the dusk of the room. The air was thick with the smell of burning wood and boiled milk. Beside the broad, iron stove sat the old man, whose name was Balthasar, quietly mending a leather harness. His face, seamed and browned by the brutal alpine winters, was turned away, but his daughter, a heavy, silent woman with vast, patient hands, moved in the shadows. She brought him a bowl of clear broth. He looked at her face, which was placid and without any discernible depth of intellect, and he thought of the Minister he had been sent to kill—the man whose carriage was to be shattered by the explosive in his valise on the cobblestones of Geneva. He thought of the absolute, annihilating justice of the Cause. And then the woman’s hand, rough and clumsy, yet imbued with a solicitous tenderness that seemed to belong to another, simpler universe, adjusted the coarse blanket over his chest. It was an inconceivable gesture. He felt a sudden, overwhelming vertigo, as if the solid floor of the world had given way beneath him.

Balthasar, observing that his guest had returned to the world of the living, laid down his harness and his leather awl. He came to the edge of the wooden cot, his heavy boots creaking, and looked down with an expression of grave, unexcited satisfaction. He was a man of few words, and those he possessed seemed to be hewn from the very rock of the mountains, stark and unadorned.

“You are mended now, perhaps,” Balthasar said, his voice vibrating with a deep, slow resonance. “The fever goes out of a man like water out of the spring snow. It leaves the ground bare.”

He tried to speak, to frame some polite, meaningless phrase of gratitude, but his throat was parched, and the words refused to come. He merely nodded. The old man nodded back, a solemn compact established between them, and retreated again to his stove. The silence of the house resumed its sway—a silence not empty, but filled with the crackle of pine logs, the distant, muffled roaring of the glacier, the slow ticking of the wooden clock. It was a silence that seemed to him, in his enfeebled and morbidly sensitive state, to be charged with an immense, terrifying significance. It was the silence of lives lived in direct contact with the earth, without the feverish, destructive agitations of ideas.

In the days that followed, as his strength slowly returned, he would sit wrapped in a rough blanket by the window, looking out at the vast, white slopes. His mind, trained to a relentless, analytical sharpness, turned and re-turned upon the paradox of his situation. He had been sent to execute a judgment; he was an emissary of a suffering humanity, a man who had severed all ties of family and affection to serve the absolute. Yet here, in this obscure and backward hamlet, he had been saved not by the grand, abstract solidarity of the proletariat, but by the spontaneous, unreasoning compassion of these two simple peasants. They had not asked for his papers. They had not inquired into his politics. They had seen a fellow creature perishing in the snow, and they had carried him in, warmed him, fed him, with the unthinking naturalness of breathing. He observed them with a fearful, penetrating scrutiny, as if they were some newly discovered species of animal. Balthasar would go out into the blinding snow to tend his few goats, returning with frost on his white beard, his movements deliberate, uncomplaining, eternal. The daughter would scrub the wooden floor, her face impassive, her existence bounded by the round of domestic duties.

He found himself watching the muscles of her arms, the slow, rhythmic motion of her body, with a kind of fascination that bordered on the morbid. She was life—life in its most elementary, unreflecting, and indestructible form. And the Cause? The Cause was a negation, a destruction of this. It was a grand, sweeping theory that demanded the sacrifice of the individual for the sake of a future too remote to be conceived. He had believed in it with all the fiery conviction of a convert; he had looked forward to the explosion, the shattered glass, the cries of the dying, as a necessary, purifying storm. But now, sitting in the warm, pungent atmosphere of the chalet, watching the old man and the woman, he felt the ground of his convictions shifting, slipping away from him. He was a man clinging to a ledge, and the abyss of doubt was opening beneath his feet.

One evening, when the wind howled with peculiar fury around the eaves, shaking the wooden shutters, Balthasar sat down opposite him, filling a short, blackened pipe. The old man struck a match, and in the flaring light his eyes, small and shrewd, rested upon his guest with a sudden, disquieting penetration.

“You are not of the lowlands,” Balthasar said slowly, between puffs. “You are from the East. You carry a heavy shadow, Herr.”

He started, his hand clutching the arm of his chair. The instinct of the conspirator, dormant for so many days, surged up within him, cold and alert. He saw himself discovered, denounced, dragged down to the valley by the gendarmes. He forced a sickly smile.

“I am from far away,” he murmured. “I am a traveler.”

“Travelers carry no weight,” Balthasar said phlegmatically. “But you carry a weight in your breast. It is visible. It is a black thing. I have seen it before, in the eyes of the young men who go to the cities and do not come back.”

He stared at the old man, unable to speak. The simplicity of the observation was more terrible than any accusation. It was not a judgment, but a statement of fact, delivered with the same detached certainty with which Balthasar might have predicted the coming of the winter snow. He felt himself stripped bare, his secret, terrible purpose laid open to the quiet gaze of this peasant. He had an almost irresistible impulse to confess everything—to tell of the bomb, of the Minister, of the dark, mad justice of the revolution. But the words died on his lips. What would this man understand of the grinding tyranny, the hopeless misery, the vast conspiracy of oppression that justified the deed? He would understand nothing. And yet, in his lack of understanding, Balthasar possessed a moral stature, a profound, unshakeable decency, that made the revolutionary feel himself to be a monstrous, bloodstained thing.

The old man continued to smoke, gazing into the fire. “The mountains do not care for the shadows of men,” he said at last. “They stand. The snow falls. The sun melts it. A man who comes here with a shadow must leave it in the snow, or it will eat him up.”

He turned his head away, pressing his forehead against the cold glass of the window. Outside, the white slopes gleamed in the moonlight, cold, pure, and indifferent. He thought of the valise, hidden in the hollow of his bed, containing the engine of destruction. He saw, with a sudden, horrifying clarity, the nature of his mission. It was not a blow for freedom; it was an act of blind, senseless murder, an attempt to impose a human, theoretical pattern upon the vast, unheeding chaos of life. The simple, elemental goodness of these people had accomplished what no argument, no philosophical treatise, no appeal to humanity could have accomplished. It had undermined the very foundations of his fanaticism. He was a man who had set out to kill, and he was conquered—conquered by a bowl of broth, by a rough hand on a blanket, by the immense, unthinking patience of a life lived close to the earth.

When he was well enough to leave, he stood in the doorway, his valise in his hand, looking back at Balthasar and the woman. They stood side by side, their faces expressionless, their forms dark against the light of the stove. He wanted to speak, to express the tumult of his thoughts, the agony of his disillusionment, but the words would not come. He merely bowed his head, turned, and walked out into the snow. The valise seemed to weigh upon him with an unbearable, crushing weight—the weight of a dead idea, the weight of a murder he could no longer commit. He walked slowly down the mountain, leaving his shadow behind him in the snow, a hollow man, moving towards a future that was as blank and as desolate as the white slopes around him.
