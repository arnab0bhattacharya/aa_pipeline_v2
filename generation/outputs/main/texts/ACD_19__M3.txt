It was upon a raw, foggy afternoon in the latter part of November, 1894, that my friend, Mr. Martin Hewitt, turned from his desk and handed me a sheet of ruled music-paper. The yellow fog of Marylebone hung thick against the bottle-glass of our sitting-room windows, but the bright fire in the grate threw a ruddy light over the scattered papers and reference volumes that littered the table.

"You play the piano a little, Brett," said he, pushing his gold pince-nez more firmly on to the bridge of his sharp nose. "Cast your eye over that manuscript and tell me what you make of it."

I took the paper readily enough. The ink was fresh, the notation written in a hurried but educated hand. "It is a melody of some kind," I answered, after a moment's scrutiny. "Unusual, certainly. The intervals are very wide, and the time-signature shifts from common to six-eight in the second bar. It has a strange, haunting quality to the eye, though I should be at a loss to hum it."

"Exactly," said Hewitt, tapping his thin, agile fingers upon the arm of his chair. "A singular composition, you will allow. Now, I should be greatly obliged if you would ring the bell. Our visitor, who left that paper upon the table half an hour ago, will have returned by now."

I rang, and a moment later the maid showed in a young woman of perhaps thirty years of age. She was plainly but neatly dressed in a dark serge costume, with a small bonnet trimmed with velvet. Her gloves were neatly mended at the fingertips, and she carried a weather-beaten leather satchel. I placed her at once as a music teacher, dependent upon her daily round of pupils.

"Pray take a seat, Miss Vance," said Hewitt, with that brisk, reassuring courtesy which he knew so well how to employ. "I have been looking into the matter of your manuscript. Mr. Brett, here, is my trusted friend and associate, and you may speak as freely before him as before myself."

"Thank you, sir," said Miss Vance, settling herself nervously upon the edge of the chair. "I am sure I do not know where else to turn. The whole affair is so trivial, and yet so unaccountable, that I fear you will think me a foolish woman come to waste your valuable time."

"I am the judge of what wastes my time," said Hewitt, with a smile. "Let us have the facts from the beginning, if you please. You are a music teacher?"

"Yes, sir. I have a connexion with several families in the Marylebone and St. John's Wood districts. Among my pupils is a young lady of seventeen, Miss Edith Vane. She is an orphan, and resides with her guardian, a Mr. Silas Lockwood, at his private residence, No. 14 Lyndhurst Terrace."

"And what is Mr. Lockwood's position?"

"He is a retired merchant, sir, of considerable wealth. He is a large, stern man, very precise in his habits, and I believe he has mining interests in South America. Miss Vane is his ward."

"Excellent. And the nature of your difficulty?"

"It concerns the young lady's practising, sir. For the last six weeks I have attended Lyndhurst Terrace twice a week to give Miss Vane her lesson. She is a gifted girl, with a remarkable ear, but latterly her attention has wandered in a most distressing fashion. Worse, she will persist in playing tunes which are not in her curriculum—tunes of a wild, formless character, which she says she cannot read from any book."

"Such as the one upon that paper?" asked Hewitt, nodding toward the manuscript.

"Exactly that, sir. I transcribed it from memory, for she played it three times running at our last lesson on Tuesday, entirely disregarding my attempts to correct her. When I asked her where she had heard it, she told me, with a very white face, that she had not heard it at all, but that she dreamed it."

"Dreamed it?"

"That was her first explanation, sir. But upon pressing her—for I could see she was holding something back—she confessed that she believed it was hummed to her. She said that for the last fortnight, shortly after dusk, she has heard a voice beneath her window in the garden, humming these very airs. The window of her music-room looks out upon a small private garden at the rear of the house, and she avers that the stranger's voice carries distinctly in the quiet of the evening."

Hewitt leaned forward, his eyes brightening. "She has seen the person?"

"No, sir. The garden is thick with evergreens, and the voice is low. It is always a man's voice, she says, humming a different tune each time. And—and this is the remarkable part, Mr. Hewitt—Miss Vane declares that the tunes get into her head, and that she cannot rest until she has played them upon the piano."

"A form of musical obsession, perhaps," I suggested. "An overwrought imagination in a nervous girl."

Miss Vance shook her head. "I should have thought so too, Dr. Brett. But there is more. When I called at Lyndhurst Terrace yesterday afternoon, I was met at the door by Mr. Lockwood himself. He is a man who usually leaves all household matters to his housekeeper, and I was surprised to find him in the hall. He told me, very bluntly, that my services would no longer be required. I asked the reason. He said that Miss Vane's health was delicate, and that she must give up her music for the present."

"Did he seem disturbed?"

"He seemed very much alarmed, sir. His face was flushed, and I observed that his left hand, which rested upon the newel-post, was trembling violently. I ventured to say that I hoped Miss Vane was not seriously ill. He replied that she was not ill at all, but that she had been practicing too hard, and that he would not have her worried. He then closed the door upon me with considerable haste."

Hewitt sat silent for a few moments, his fingertips pressed together. "You have the young lady's window, you say, at the back of the house, overlooking the garden?"

"Yes, sir."

"And the garden itself? Is it accessible from the lane?"

"There is a side-entrance, I believe, opening upon Lyndhurst Mews. But it is always kept locked."

Hewitt rose and crossed to the window, gazing out into the fog. "Miss Vance," said he, turning sharply, "you have done wisely in coming to me. There is more in this than a nervous girl and a humming stranger. Tell me, do you happen to know whether Mr. Lockwood has any near relatives?"

"I believe he has a nephew, sir—a Mr. Arthur Lockwood. I have never seen him, but Miss Vane once mentioned him. She said he had been in South America, and that there had been some disagreement between him and his uncle."

"Ah, South America! And the mining interests," murmured Hewitt, his eyes glinting. "Miss Vance, I shall trouble you to leave that manuscript with me. I also require the exact direction of Lyndhurst Terrace, and the hour at which the humming is usually heard. I propose to call at No. 14 this very evening."

"But Mr. Lockwood will not admit you, sir!"

"I have no intention of calling upon Mr. Lockwood. Brett, put on your overcoat. We are going gardening."

It was close upon seven o'clock when we found ourselves in the damp, leaf-strewn alley of Lyndhurst Mews. The fog had thickened, and the gas-lamps at the corners threw out dim, nebulous halos. Hewitt, who carried a small lantern, picked his way along the brick wall until he stopped before a low iron gate.

"You see, Brett," he whispered, drawing me into the shadow of the laurels, "the thing is perfectly simple when one has the clue. A wealthy man, a ward of seventeen, and a disinherited nephew in South America. The marriage of the ward would mean the transfer of a very considerable fortune. But the guardian is vigilant. Direct access to the young lady is impossible. What then? Why, the music-room window, and a series of melodies which she cannot resist playing."

"But what can be the object of the tunes?"

"Listen!"

From somewhere above us, through the half-open sash of an upper window, there came the clear, faint sound of a piano. A girl's hands were running over the keys, playing a strange, wandering melody with a desperate, mechanical accuracy. Even as I listened, a figure detached itself from the shadows beneath the window. A man stood there, bareheaded in the fog, and I could see the glow of a cigar. He began to hum, softly but distinctly, a new phrase. Instantly, from the window above, the piano changed. The girl's hands followed the voice, echoing the air with an uncanny precision.

"The power of association," whispered Hewitt. "A trained ear, a lonely girl, and a voice she has been taught to obey. Now, Brett—"

He stepped forward into the open. The man by the window started violently, dropped his cigar, and turned to flee. But Hewitt was too quick for him. With a spring that belied his slender frame, he caught the fellow by the collar and pinned him against the wall.

"Steady, sir, steady!" said Hewitt, cheerfully. "A little explanation, if you please. You are Mr. Arthur Lockwood, I presume?"

The man was young, dark, and sallow, with the sun-tanned skin of one who has lived much under southern skies. He struggled for a moment, then desisted.

"Who the devil are you?" he snarled.

"A private inquiry agent," said Hewitt. "And I think I may say that your little scheme is at an end. You have been using a series of native melodies—learned, no doubt, in the mining camps of Peru—to establish an influence over your cousin. A clever device, sir, but a dangerous one."

The man's face went pale. "I meant her no harm," he muttered. "I only wished to speak with her."

"That may be, but you will have the goodness to walk round to the front door with me, and lay the matter before Mr. Silas Lockwood. I fancy the interview will be somewhat stormy, but it will at least be honest. And as for you, Miss Vance," added Hewitt, turning to me with a quiet smile, "I think we may promise you that your pupil's music lessons will be resumed on Tuesday next."
