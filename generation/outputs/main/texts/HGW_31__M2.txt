The wind at the station of Cape Vane did not howl; it screamed, a high, thin shriek that seemed to drill into the very marrow of one’s bones. I had been on the ice for three days, stranded by a boiler failure in our tender, and the cold had long since ceased to be a sensation and become a state of being. I was Dr. Arthur Penrose, a physician by training and a skeptic by temperament, and I found myself standing in the freezing spray of a blubber-rendering shed, looking at a shape that defied the known taxonomy of the animal kingdom.

The station manager, a man named Halloway who possessed the moral flexibility of wet sand, had been pointing at the object with a grease-stained finger, his breath pluming in the dim light of the kerosene lamps. It lay on a trestle of driftwood, half-buried in a block of blue ice that had calved from the glacier shelf only the previous morning. It was not a whale, though it was of similar bulk. It was not a seal, though it had flippers. It was a thing of wrong angles and pale, translucent skin, folded upon itself like a discarded glove.

"It's a freak," Halloway said, his voice cracking from the chill. "A natural curiosity. The museums in London will pay a fortune for it. I can get it to the next port for a pittance. You stay here, Penrose. You’re a doctor; you can keep the crew healthy. I’ll send the ship for you when the ice breaks."

I did not answer immediately. I was staring at the creature’s head, or what served for a head. The eyes were closed, but the lids were thin, and beneath them I could see the dark, wet gleam of something moving. The skin was not hairless, but covered in a fine, downy fuzz that was already matting with frost. There was a smell about it, too—a faint, cloying sweetness, like overripe fruit left in a hot house, which was incongruous in this land of death and iron.

"It is not a curiosity," I said, my voice sounding foreign to my own ears. "It is an organism. And it is dying."

Halloway spat into a bucket of rendered blubber. "Everything dies, doctor. The question is, will it die before I can sell it? That is the commercial reality."

I moved closer, ignoring the man’s sputtering objection. I was a scientist, and the unknown demands observation. I pulled a handkerchief from my pocket, dipped it in a bottle of carbolic acid I carried for the prevention of gangrene, and wiped a smear of ice from the creature’s flank. As the ice melted, the skin beneath shivered. It was not the shiver of a corpse. It was the tremor of muscle tension.

"Halloway," I said, turning to face him. "Look at the limbs."

The creature had four limbs, but they were not the paddles of a marine mammal. They were jointed, complex, ending in long, dexterous fingers that were curled inward, clawing at the wood of the trestle. The bones were visible through the skin, long and slender, suggesting a creature built for dexterity rather than brute force. And then, the eyes opened.

They were not eyes in the human sense. They were large, black, and devoid of a white sclera, possessing a depth that seemed to swallow the light of the lamps. They did not focus on me, nor on Halloway. They seemed to look through us, at some distant, cold horizon.

Halloway stepped back, his face draining of color. "What is that thing?"

"I do not know," I said, and I was telling the truth. "But it is alive. And it is waking up."

The cold of the ice block was receding. The heat of the shed, generated by the rendering vats and the stoves, was beginning to penetrate the frozen mass. I could see the creature’s chest rise and fall, a slow, rhythmic expansion that was terrifying in its regularity. It was breathing.

"We must keep it frozen," I said, the urgency of the moment overriding my usual caution. "If it thaws fully, it may become dangerous. Or it may simply die, and we shall have a mystery for the coroner. But if it wakes up in this heat, who knows what it will do?"

Halloway’s fear was quickly replaced by avarice. He looked at the creature, then at the door, then back at the creature. "It’s valuable, Penrose. Alive or dead, it’s valuable. I’m not going to let a bit of warmth ruin a fifty-pound investment."

"You are a fool," I said, and I saw the anger flare in his eyes. "This is not a commodity. It is a potential threat to human sanity. Do you see those hands? Those are hands, Halloway. They are made for holding things. For tool use. What does it hold? What has it been holding in the ice for a century?"

The creature made a sound. It was not a roar, nor a cry. It was a low, resonant hum, a vibration that I felt in my teeth more than I heard with my ears. The hum grew louder, and the air in the shed seemed to vibrate with it. The kerosene lamps flickered, their flames bending away from the creature as if repelled by an invisible force.

Halloway reached for the axe he kept by the blubber vats, his face twisted with a mixture of greed and terror. "I’m cutting it free," he muttered. "I’m cutting it free before it eats us."

"No!" I shouted, stepping between him and the trestle. "If you cut the ice, you accelerate the thaw. You will kill it, or worse, you will wake it fully."

The hum intensified. The creature’s head lifted. The long, pale neck stretched, and the black eyes locked onto mine. In that gaze, I saw something that chilled me more than the Antarctic winter. I saw intelligence. Not the brute intelligence of an ape, nor the cunning of a fox, but a cold, calculating awareness. It looked at me as a man looks at a beetle under a glass.

Halloway swung the axe. The blade struck the ice block with a sharp crack. Shards of blue ice flew into the air, glittering like diamonds in the lamplight. The creature flinched, a sudden, violent jerk of its entire body. Its fingers, those long, pale claws, raked across the wood of the trestle, leaving deep gouges.

It was not dead. It was not a freak. It was a competitor. And it was waking up.

I realized then that my medical training was of no use here. This was not a patient to be cured, nor a disease to be treated. It was an invasion. The ice had been its prison, and the warmth of the shed was the key. I looked at Halloway, who was now swinging the axe with a frenzy, his eyes wide and wild. He did not understand what he was doing. He was not selling a curiosity; he was releasing a monster.

The creature’s mouth opened. There were no teeth, only a smooth, wet gullet that seemed to pulse with a dark, rhythmic light. The hum changed pitch, rising to a shriek that shattered the glass of a nearby window. The wind rushed in, howling its high, thin scream, and the lamps went out, one by one, until we were in darkness.

In the dark, I could hear the sound of ice cracking, the snap of wood splintering, and the wet, slapping sound of the creature moving. It was sliding off the trestle. It was coming for us.

I turned and ran into the night. The cold hit me like a physical blow, but it was a comfort compared to the heat of the shed. I ran toward the ship, toward the safety of the engine room, toward the known world. Behind me, I heard Halloway’s scream, short and final, and then the silence of the ice, broken only by the wind and the sound of something sliding over the snow, heavy and purposeful, heading toward the sea.

I reached the ship and locked the door. I sat in the dark, my hands shaking, and I waited for the dawn. I knew that when the sun rose, the world would not be the same. The ice was thinning. And what had slept in the deep was waking up.
