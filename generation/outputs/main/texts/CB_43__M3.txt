The bell of Bethesda Chapel had scarce ceased its tolling when I reached the schoolroom. The day was heavy and the air was thick with the breath of the mills, a smothering heat that seemed to emanate from the very bowels of the earth. I had spent the morning hours teaching the children of the weavers, and my spirit was wearied by the squalor of their garments, the hollow pallor of their cheeks, and the cruel, stunted crookedness of their limbs. Yet, amidst this congregation of suffering, there was one child—Silas Hoyt—who had ever possessed a peculiar claim on my regard. He was a boy of quick intellect and a solemn, searching eye, who learned his letters with a hunger that was painful to witness.

That Sunday, Silas was absent. I inquired of his sister, a wan girl of nine, whose little hands were already knotted with the toil of the spinning-frame. She looked up at me with a terror I had seen in a beaten cur, and murmured, with trembling lips, that Silas was laid up at home, for Mr. Grimsby had beaten him on the Saturday eve.

I knew Grimsby. He was an overlooker at Marsh Bank Mill, a man of Herculean frame and a heart of millstone. I demanded to know the cause. The child knew not, or would not say; but the terror in her eye spoke volumes. When the schoolroom was dismissed, I did not return to my boarding-house. A fire was kindled in my breast that burned hotter than the summer sun. I directed my steps towards the low, straggling rows where the mill-hands dwelt, and found the Hoyt’s lodging—a damp, foul-smelling cellar in a yard that seemed a very sink of human misery.

Silas was there, stretched on a pallet of dirty straw. His back was bared, and I saw the marks of the lash—great, red wheals that had drawn blood, stripes laid on with a savage, indiscriminate fury that made my blood boil and my hands clench. The boy looked up at me with those dark, solemn eyes, and I saw in them not the tears of a child, but the dumb despair of a tortured animal. He had dropped a bobbin, it seemed, and Grimsby had taken him to the engine-room, and with a heavy strap had beaten him till he fell, and beaten him again as he lay on the stone floor.

I spoke to the mother—a broken, listless woman, who seemed crushed beneath a weight of sorrow and toil—and I left the cellar with a resolve that burned like a furnace. I would go to the mill. I would see the master. I would not rest until this wrong was righted.

Marsh Bank Mill stood by the river, a great, dark pile of brick, with a tall chimney that vomited forth a ceaseless cloud of black smoke. It was Sunday, and the great wheel was still, the hands silent; but I knew that in the counting-house, the owner, Mr. Joshua Thornton, or his son, Mr. Edgar Thornton, might be found. I had heard that the elder Thornton was a man of iron, but the son was said to be of a softer temper. To him, therefore, I determined to appeal.

I crossed the yard, my footsteps echoing on the flags. The counting-house door was open, and I entered without ceremony. The room was plainly furnished, with a large desk, some ledgers, and a map of the district on the wall. At the desk sat a young man, elegant in dress and languid in manner, with a pale, clear-cut face and eyes of a cold, light blue. He was reading a newspaper, and looked up with an air of faint surprise as I stood before him.

“I wish to speak with Mr. Edgar Thornton,” I said.

“I am he,” he replied, laying down his paper and leaning back in his chair. “What can I do for you?”

“You can do justice,” I answered, and my voice, though I strove to govern it, had a tremor of indignation. “I am the Sunday-school teacher at Bethesda Chapel. I have this day visited a child of your mill—a boy named Silas Hoyt—and I have seen on his body the marks of a brutal whipping, inflicted by your overlooker, Mr. Grimsby. I have come to demand that this man be punished.”

He raised his eyebrows slightly, and a faint smile, which I thought ill-timed and offensive, played about his lips. “Indeed?” he said. “And pray, what had the boy done to merit correction?”

“Correction!” I echoed, my voice rising. “He dropped a bobbin. For this trifling fault, he was taken to the engine-room and flogged with a strap till he fell to the ground, and the lash was laid on again as he lay there. Is this your English justice, Mr. Thornton? Is this the way you train up the poor children who toil in your mill?”

He was silent a moment, and his blue eyes rested on me with a calm, scanning gaze that I found insufferable. “My good Miss,” he said at length, “you are excited. You have seen the boy, and your feelings have been worked upon. It is natural. But you must remember that the management of a mill is no light matter. We have hundreds of hands to control—some of them idle, some mutinous, many careless. Discipline must be maintained. If a boy neglects his work, he must be corrected, or the whole frame of the establishment falls into disorder.”

“Discipline!” I retorted. “Call it rather tyranny. Call it cruelty. Call it by the name which God, who sees these things, will give it in the day of His judgment. You speak of disorder; what of the disorder of a child’s body, bruised and bleeding? What of the disorder of a soul, terror-stricken and crushed? You talk of the frame of your establishment; I tell you that the frame of that boy is broken, and his spirit is well-nigh broken too. And for what? Because he dropped a bobbin—a bobbin, Mr. Thornton! Is a bobbin of more value than a child?”

He smiled again—that same faint, supercilious smile. “You are eloquent,” he said, “and you are unjust. I am not cruel. I have no wish to see children ill-used. But I cannot dismiss an overlooker on the complaint of a Sunday-school teacher, however zealous. Grimsby is a rough man, I grant you, but he is efficient. He keeps the hands to their work. If I were to interfere in every case of correction, I should have no authority left.”

“Authority!” I cried, and now my passion broke through all restraint. “What authority has a man over the body and soul of another, given him by God only that he may use it for the good of all? You have authority over these hands, Mr. Thornton, but you have also responsibility. And I tell you that the blood of this child will be required at your hands. You may smile, sir, but there is a Judge who sees not as man sees. He will not ask whether your mill was profitable, but whether you were just. He will not ask whether Grimsby was efficient, but whether he was merciful. And as for me, I shall not rest. I shall tell this tale in every house in the town. I shall rouse the people. I shall—”

“You will do nothing of the kind,” he interrupted, and now his voice had a sharper note. “You will go home, Miss, and cool your temper. You are a woman of education, I perceive, and I respect your motives, but you do not understand the world. The mill must go on. The hands must work. The overlooker must do his duty. If the boy was hurt beyond what was needful, I will speak to Grimsby. But I shall do no more.”

He rose, and bowed. I saw that it was useless to contend further. I turned and left the counting-house, my heart swelling with a grief and anger that I could not express.

The sun was setting as I crossed the yard, and its red light fell upon the great chimney and the blackened walls of the mill. It seemed to me like the glare of a furnace, and I thought of the words of the prophet: “The day cometh that shall burn as an oven; and all the proud, yea, and all that do wickedly, shall be stubble.” I prayed that it might be so. I prayed that the day of retribution would come, and that the cries of the oppressed would be heard. But as I walked homeward through the narrow, noisome streets, and saw the weary, sullen faces of the people, and felt the heavy weight of their wrongs pressing on my heart, I knew that the day was yet far off, and that the night of suffering was long.
