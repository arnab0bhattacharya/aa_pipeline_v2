The assembly rooms at Weymouth, though scarcely approaching the magnificence which the inhabitants of that rising watering-place delighted to anticipate, were yet sufficiently brilliant to overwhelm the senses of two young women who had never before danced anywhere but in the parlour of their father’s country parsonage. The cardroom, the orchestra, the flutter of coloured silks, and the steady, imposing gaze of fashionably dressed strangers, combined to form a spectacle that was as nearly dreadful as it was delightful. Miss Clara Whitfield and her sister Maria had been a full week in the town under the care of their aunt, Mrs. Gibson, a widow of competent fortune and insatiable curiosity, whose chief purpose in quitting the neighbourhood of Everleigh had been to secure for her nieces a glimpse of the wider world. She had not, she assured them, brought them all the way to the coast to sit over their needlework in a rented lodging-house.

Clara, who was nineteen and possessed of a very decided notion of her own consequence, was resolved to enjoy the evening to the utmost. She was handsome, animated, and wholly without that diffidence which sometimes renders beauty useless to its possessor. Maria, two years younger, was less striking, and infinitely less assured. She had a quiet countenance, a great deal of good sense, and a very moderate opinion of her own claims to notice. It was generally agreed among their acquaintance in Everleigh that Clara would make a brilliant match, and that Maria would make an excellent one, or none at all.

The sisters had been scarcely half an hour in the room before Clara was engaged for the first two dances by a Mr. Bryce, a young man of pleasing address and a red coat, whose acquaintance they had made that very morning on the esplanade. From that moment, her card filled with a rapidity that left no room for disappointment. She danced with a succession of partners whose names she could scarcely recollect by the end of the evening, and whose faces she had not particularly observed. She was too much engrossed by the present sensation to trouble herself with remembrance.

Maria, meanwhile, sat beside her aunt. She had been introduced to one or two young ladies, with whom she had exchanged the commonplaces of the evening, and she had been asked to stand up once, by a gentleman of middle age and uncertain countenance, whom she had respectfully declined, upon the reasonable suspicion that he had mistaken her for someone else. This was the sum of her conquests. She watched the dancing with a composure that Mrs. Gibson, who was herself engaged in a vigorous survey of the company, found quite inexplicable.

“You are not to sit there the whole evening, Maria,” said that lady, in a tone of undisguised impatience. “It is the very thing to be remarked upon. Pray, cannot you rouse yourself? Clara is dancing with her third partner already, and you have not moved from your chair.”

“I am very well situated, Aunt,” Maria replied, with a mildness which she knew would irritate, and which therefore she could not wholly forbear. “I am quite entertained by watching the room. And I do not think I shall die of sitting still for one evening.”

Mrs. Gibson was not satisfied, but she was diverted, at that moment, by the entrance of a party of strangers whose appearance promised something of interest. A lady of elegant appearance, accompanied by two gentlemen, had taken up a station near the door, and was surveying the assembly with an air of tranquil superiority. One of the gentlemen was tall, plain-featured, and wholly unremarkable; the other was rather younger, of a thoughtful aspect, and possessed of that quiet assurance of manner which seldom fails to attract notice, even in a crowded room. Mrs. Gibson immediately applied herself to the task of discovering who they were, and Maria was left to her own reflections.

She observed, without difficulty, that the younger gentleman, after a brief survey of the room, looked frequently in the direction of their own party. She attributed this at first to the more natural supposition that he was admiring Clara, who was at that moment dancing with a degree of animation which could hardly fail to draw the eye. Upon closer observation, however, she was obliged to confess to herself, that his gaze rested more often upon the chairs than upon the floor, and more particularly upon that corner of the room where she herself was seated. This was an impression which she did not suffer to take deep root. She had been brought up to distrust her own powers of pleasing, and she concluded that she was merely the accidental object of a wandering attention.

The evening wore on. Clara danced every dance, with an unflagging spirit that was the admiration of her partners and the despair of her aunt, who could not but observe that she had reserved nothing for the later part of the night, when the most desirable company was generally to be found. Maria continued to sit, to observe, and to be entirely overlooked, or so she believed. She saw the stranger party depart before the close of the evening, and thought no more of them.

The next morning, Mrs. Gibson was seated at her breakfast, engaged in a detailed recital of the preceding night’s events, when the landlady entered with a card. Maria, who was buttering a piece of toast with the quiet contentment of a person who has no engagement to interrupt, scarcely looked up. Clara, who was still in high spirits from the triumphs of the evening, seized the card with an eagerness which the occasion scarcely warranted, and pronounced the name aloud.

“Mr. Elliot. Aunt, who is Mr. Elliot? I am sure I never heard the name before. Can it be one of my partners of last evening? There were so many, I cannot pretend to remember them all.”

Mrs. Gibson took the card, examined both sides of it with the critical attention of a connoisseur, and declared herself equally at a loss. It was not, she was certain, the name of any of the gentlemen to whom Clara had been introduced. She desired the landlady to send the gentleman up, and the three ladies applied themselves, with some solicitude, to the task of appearing suitably prepared for an unknown visitor.

Mr. Elliot was presently announced. He was the younger of the two gentlemen who had entered the assembly rooms with the elegant lady on the preceding evening. Maria recognised him immediately, and with a sensation of surprise which she found it difficult to conceal. He was a man of five-and-twenty, of a grave but pleasing countenance, and a manner which, though not immediately easy, was free from any of that forwardness which so often passes for confidence in the ballroom. He enquired, with great civility, after Mrs. Gibson’s health, expressed his pleasure at finding the young ladies at home, and begged to know whether Miss Whitfield and Miss Maria had enjoyed their first Weymouth assembly.

Clara replied with her usual readiness, and a great deal of spirit, that she had never spent a more delightful evening, that the rooms were charming, the music excellent, and the company, she believed, everything that could be wished. She spoke for some minutes with an eloquence that left little for anyone else to say, and which might have satisfied a visitor who came only for the purpose of general conversation. Mr. Elliot listened with attention, bowed, smiled, and then, with a slight but perceptible change of direction, addressed himself to Maria.

“I hope, Miss Maria,” said he, “that you were not wholly without amusement. I observed that you did not dance.”

Maria, startled to find herself so particularly noticed, could only reply, with some confusion, that she had been very well entertained by watching the others.

“That,” said Mr. Elliot, “is a proof of philosophy which I can admire, but cannot imitate. I found the spectacle of so much animation so far from satisfying, that it only made me wish to participate in it.”

He said this with a look which Maria did not know how to interpret. Clara, who had been listening with a degree of surprise which she could not entirely suppress, interposed with a lively remark upon the hazards of dancing with strangers, and the conversation became general. But Mr. Elliot’s attention, throughout the remainder of his visit, returned again and again, with a steadiness that was remarkable, to the sister who had not danced at all.

When he had taken his leave, Mrs. Gibson sat for some moments in profound silence, looking from one niece to the other with an expression of considerable perplexity. Clara was the first to speak.

“Well,” said she, with a laugh which had in it something of mortification, “I suppose I must conclude that it is better to sit still than to dance. I had not thought that idleness was the way to be admired, but I dare say I am no judge of what is fashionable.”

Maria made no reply. She was employed in arranging the tea things with an assiduity which she had no occasion for, and which concealed, if it did not entirely disguise, a very natural curiosity as to what Mr. Elliot’s visit might portend. Mrs. Gibson, recovering at length from her astonishment, declared that she had always known there was more in Maria than met the eye, and that she had only been waiting for an opportunity to prove it. This was not, strictly speaking, accurate; but it was the kind of assertion which that lady found it convenient to make, and which nobody present was in a humour to dispute.
