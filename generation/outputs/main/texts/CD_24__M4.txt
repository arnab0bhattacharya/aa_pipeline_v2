The establishment of Mr. Obadiah Grubb, dealer in rags, bones, and the flotsam of a hundred forgotten households, stood at the nether end of a crooked and miry lane in Clerkenwell, where the houses leaned together like conspirators, and the smoke of a thousand chimneys hung low and heavy, as if the very atmosphere were a blanket that had never been shaken. It was a place of perpetual twilight, wherein the sun, if he visited at all, came with a sickly and apologetic countenance, and the mud in the kennel was of a richness and tenacity that spoke of ages of accumulation. Here, behind a high wall of crumbling brick, topped with a bristling chevaux-de-frise of broken bottles, Mr. Grubb had for forty years presided over his singular domain; a domain of which the chief features were a great shapeless mound of miscellaneous refuse, a weighing-machine of ancient and uncertain temper, a shed whose roof sagged like the back of an overburdened porter, and a multitude of sacks and heaps, each in its own state of decomposition, and each, according to the common report, harbouring a treasure.

That Mr. Grubb was a miser, no man in Clerkenwell doubted. His coat, which had once been blue, was now a garment of philosophical neutrality, so patched and repatched that its original colour was a matter of tradition rather than of observation; his hat was a battered cylinder of felt, from beneath which his sharp grey eyes peered out like two rats from a dustbin; and his hands, which were never idle, were of that peculiar hue which is the result of a lifelong intimacy with the more respectable kinds of filth. He spoke but little, and that little in a voice like the creaking of a rusty hinge; and when he laughed, which was seldom, it was a dry, rattling sound, as though a bag of bones were being shaken. The boys of the neighbourhood believed that he slept upon a bed of sovereigns, and that he ate his supper off a plate of gold; while the more sober-minded matrons, who sent their children to him with the household rags, held that he was a poor, harmless creature enough, whose only sin was a love of money that had grown upon him as the moss grows upon a wall.

It was upon a raw November morning, when the fog lay thick upon the lane and the gas-lamps burned yellow all day, that Mr. Grubb was found lying at the foot of his great mound, with his face turned upward to the sky and his old hat rolled into the kennel. The physician, who was summoned from a neighbouring street, declared that the heart had failed; and the parish authorities, having viewed the body with that professional cheerfulness which is the peculiar attribute of parish authorities, gave orders for its removal, and thought no more of the matter. But the neighbours thought a great deal of it. For no sooner was the rumour abroad that Grubb was dead, than there arose from every doorway and every cellar a swarm of those whom the prospect of gain draws forth, as the sun draws flies; and the yard, which had been for forty years a place of silence and of secrecy, became of a sudden the scene of a tumultuous and eager inquisition.

First upon the ground was Mrs. Peg Flannagan, a woman of vast circumference and a voice of brass, who kept a little shop for the sale of greens and firewood, and who had long regarded the yard with the eye of a creditor. She was accompanied by her son, a lanky youth of sixteen, whose principal occupation was to whistle, and who now stood with his hands in his pockets, surveying the scene with an air of profound indifference. Close behind her came Mr. Silas Tottle, a small man in a snuff-coloured coat, who was understood to be in the coal trade, and who carried a hammer, as though he expected to find the treasure embedded in the masonry. Then came the two Misses Crumm, elderly sisters, who kept a milliner’s shop in the next street, and who had brought with them a basket, a pair of scissors, and a firm determination to examine every rag in the establishment. And after them, a score of others, of both sexes and all ages, each with some implement of search, and each with a countenance in which curiosity, cupidity, and a certain grudging respect for the dead were strangely mingled.

“Now, then, Mrs. Flannagan,” cried Mr. Tottle, who had already mounted the mound and was prodding it with his hammer, “if you’ll be so good as to keep your family off my heels, I’ll thank you. There’s room enough for all, and no call for shoving.”

“Shove yourself, Silas Tottle,” returned the lady, with spirit. “It’s little you know about rags, I’ll be bound, and less about bones. It’s my opinion the old man’s money is in the shed, and I’m going to look for it.”

“And I say it’s in the heap,” said Mr. Tottle. “For didn’t I see him myself, no later than last Tuesday was a week, a-putting something into a sack at the top of it, and looking round him as sly as a cat?”

At this, there was a general rush towards the mound; and the two Misses Crumm, who had been examining a heap of old boots with great minuteness, abandoned that pursuit and flew to the sack which Mr. Tottle had indicated. It contained nothing but a quantity of broken crockery and a dead cat; and a murmur of disappointment ran through the assembly.

It was at this juncture that there appeared upon the scene a young girl of some fifteen years, in a clean though much-mended frock, and with a shawl wrapped about her head and shoulders. Her name was Lucy Drayton, and she had been for two years the servant of Mr. Grubb; the only creature, it was said, for whom the old man had ever shown the least regard. She had nursed him in his last illness, and had closed his eyes; and she now stood in the doorway of the shed, with her hands clasped before her, and her pale face looking out upon the crowd with a strange expression of pity and of fear.

“And what does Miss Lucy say?” cried Mrs. Flannagan, catching sight of her. “She ought to know, if anyone does. Come, my dear, speak up. Where did the old man keep his money?”

Lucy hesitated. “He kept it,” she said at last, in a low voice, “in a great many places. There was some in the shed, behind the loose brick; and some in the old boot that hangs upon the nail; and some in the sack that has the red mark on it. But the most of it—the most of it is in the yard, under the little pear-tree that stands by the wall.”

A cry of astonishment and delight burst from the assembly. The pear-tree was a stunted and melancholy shrub, which had never borne fruit, and which stood in a corner of the yard where the rubbish was deepest and the mud was thickest. In an instant, the whole crowd was in motion towards it; and Mr. Tottle, who had the hammer, was the first to reach the spot.

“Stand back!” he cried, “and let me dig. I’ll have it up in a jiffy.”

He dug; and the crowd pressed round him, breathless. He dug deeper; and still there was nothing. He dug until the perspiration stood upon his forehead, and his snuff-coloured coat was speckled with mud; and still there was nothing. A murmur of doubt arose; and Mrs. Flannagan was heard to observe that she had never thought much of Lucy Drayton, and that it was a pity a poor girl should have learned to tell stories.

But at that moment the hammer struck something hard. There was a sound of metal; and Mr. Tottle, with a cry of triumph, drew forth a small iron box, rusted and heavy, which, being forced open with some difficulty, disclosed a sight that made every eye to start and every mouth to water. For there, packed close and tight, were sovereigns and guineas, and little parcels of bank-notes, and a few old-fashioned rings and trinkets, the whole amounting, as was afterwards computed, to no less a sum than eleven hundred pounds.

A great shout went up from the crowd. Mr. Tottle clutched the box to his breast; Mrs. Flannagan made a grab at it; the Misses Crumm screamed; and the lanky youth, who had hitherto preserved his indifference, was suddenly seized with a violent desire to whistle. In the midst of the confusion, Lucy Drayton stepped forward, and laid her hand upon the box.

“It is not yours,” she said, in a voice that trembled, but was clear. “It is not yours, any of you. He told me, the night before he died, that if anything happened to him, this was to go to his sister’s child, that is in service at Norwich, and that he had never seen but once. He made me write it down, and he signed it with his mark; and here it is.”

She drew from her bosom a folded paper, soiled and crumpled, and held it up before them. The crowd fell back a little, and looked at her in silence. There was a moment of hesitation; and then Mr. Tottle, with a shrug of his shoulders, returned the box to her hands.

“Well,” said he, “if that’s the way of it, I’m sure I don’t want to rob an orphan. But it’s a queer thing, Lucy Drayton, that you should have known where it was all the time, and never said a word.”

“I did not know,” said Lucy, simply, “until he told me. And I would not have told you now, but that I was afraid you would pull the place to pieces, and destroy the little that is left.”

And so the crowd dispersed, grumbling and wondering; and the yard of Mr. Obadiah Grubb was left to the fog and the silence, and to the little pear-tree that had never borne fruit, but which had, after all, been the means of bringing forth a treasure. And Lucy Drayton, with the box in her arms and the paper in her bosom, went her way through the miry lane, and was seen no more in Clerkenwell; but it was reported, long afterwards, that a certain young woman in Norwich, who had been a servant in a gentleman’s family, came suddenly into a fortune, and that she never forgot the poor old rag-and-bone man who had loved her mother, and who had died, as he had lived, in the midst of his heaps.
