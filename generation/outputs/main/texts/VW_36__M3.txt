The light fell in long, pale blades across the dining-room floor, cutting the shadows into angular pieces, and there it lay, the dust motes dancing in the shafts, rising and falling as if they, too, were breathing the thin, sharp air that had been prescribed, rigorously prescribed, for the mending of lungs. Oliver sat by the window. He had been sitting there for an hour, perhaps two; time, which in London had chased him with a whip, here merely seeped through the floorboards, slow and heavy with the scent of pine and old woodsmoke. His hands, which had been so terribly thin, so frighteningly translucent in the hospital bed that he had been forced to hide them beneath the blankets, were resting on his knees. He looked at them now, half expecting them to belong to someone else; they were tanned, or at least browned, by the mountain sun, and the veins were no longer a violet map of his frailty but merely the cords of a living body. The doctors had said he would live. They had said it with such stern conviction, as if living were a task to be assigned, like a sum in arithmetic, and he had been sent here, to the Hotel Belvédère, to work out the equation.

The dining room was empty, save for the matronly woman in the corner who coughed with a regularity that made it sound less like a symptom and more like a social grace, a punctuation to the silence. Oliver watched the snow on the peaks. It was so white, so aggressively, brilliantly white that it hurt his eyes, or rather, it hurt the memory of his eyes, which still expected the gray, fog-choked horizons of his sickroom. He thought he might sleep, or perhaps he would just sit, letting the morning pass through him, when the door opened, not with a creak but with a sigh, and she walked in.

He had seen her before; he had watched her from the balcony yesterday as she stood by the stone balustrade, throwing a piece of bread to the birds. She was not ill. One could tell that immediately, not merely by the color in her cheeks—that might have been the wind, or the altitude—but by the way she moved. She did not move cautiously, as if the floor might give way beneath her, or as if the air itself were a fragile thing to be handled with care. She walked with a kind of defiant grace, her shoulders back, her chin tilted upward, and she carried a book, not tucked under her arm as invalids carry books, like small shields against conversation, but held loosely in her hand, her finger marking a page, as if she had been interrupted in the middle of a sentence and intended to return to it the moment the world ceased to bore her. She was dressed in a dark green coat that made her look, against the snow, like a fir tree, rooted and entirely self-sufficient.

She sat down at the table across from him, by the other window, and did not look at him. She looked at the mountains. She seemed to be listening to them, as if they were speaking to her in a language she had long been trying to learn. Oliver felt a sudden, sharp pang—not of attraction, not yet, but of recognition. He recognized something in her posture, in the way she held her head, that said, *I am here because I have been sent away, or because I have run, and either way I am a prisoner, though my prison has no walls, only peaks.*

The morning passed, or rather, it accumulated, piling up moments like snowdrifts. She ordered coffee, and when it came, she wrapped her hands around the cup, not because she was cold, Oliver thought, but because she wanted to hold something, to grip something solid in a world that had become unbearably fluid. He watched her. He could not help it. The nurses had told him, in their brisk, cheerful voices, that he must not think too much; thinking consumed energy, and energy was what the lungs needed to rebuild themselves. But he thought anyway. He thought about how the light caught the edge of her cup, and how her eyes, which were gray, or perhaps green, moved across the window, not restlessly, but with a slow, deliberate attention, as if she were reading the landscape the way one reads a difficult text, searching for meaning in the grammar of rock and ice.

"Do you find," she said, suddenly, still looking at the window, as if the question had been addressed to the mountain and he had merely overheard it, "that the snow makes one feel more alone, or less?"

He was startled, not by the question, but by her voice, which was lower than he had expected, with a slight huskiness that might have been the altitude, or might have been sorrow, or might have been nothing at all but the natural register of her throat.

"More," he said. "I think more." He paused. "Though I was alone before I came. In a different way."

She turned then, and looked at him, and he felt the full weight of her attention, which was not warm, exactly, but it was real, it was unsoftened by the pity that everyone else here seemed to wear over their faces like gauze.

"Yes," she said. "That is the distinction, isn't it? There is the loneliness of being ill, which is a kind of tunnel, and there is the loneliness of being well in a world of illness, which is more like standing in a field. One sees everything. There is nothing to obstruct the view."

He nodded. He understood her, or he thought he did. She was not ill, but she was wounded; he could see it now, in the way she held her book, gripping it a little too tightly, the way one grips the edge of a table when the floor lurches. She had come here to escape something—not a disease of the body, but something else, something that had its own fevers and its own chills, its own long nights of sweating through the sheets.

"I'm Oliver," he said. It seemed absurd, this exchange of names, as if a name could bridge the vast, cold distance between two people sitting in a room full of light and dust motes and the coughing of strangers. But he said it anyway, because there was nothing else to say, and because the silence, which had been comfortable a moment ago, had suddenly become a kind of vacuum that threatened to swallow them both.

"Eva," she said. She did not offer her surname. She did not say, *Pleased to meet you.* She simply said the word, as if placing a small, precise stone on a cairn, and then she turned back to the window, and he turned back to his, and they sat there, in their separate silences, which were no longer entirely separate but had begun, at the edges, to bleed into one another, like watercolors on wet paper.

Outside, the snow went on being white. The peaks went on being high. And somewhere in the hotel, a door closed, and footsteps receded down a corridor, and the matronly woman coughed, and the sound was absorbed by the vast, indifferent silence of the mountains, which had stood there before either of them had been born and would stand there long after they had gone, and this thought, which might have been melancholy, was not; it was, instead, a kind of comfort, the only comfort available to people who had been broken, in one way or another, and who were trying, in this thin, bright air, to put themselves back together.
