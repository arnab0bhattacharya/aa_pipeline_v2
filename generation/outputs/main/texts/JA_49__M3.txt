It was a truth universally acknowledged in the village of Pemberleigh that Mrs. Vance was a woman of exceedingly frail constitution; a circumstance she dwelt upon with such regularity, and so little abatement, as to leave her acquaintance in no doubt of its severity, even if they occasionally harboured private doubts of its existence. Her ailments, however, were not of the common sort that yield to physic, but rather of a delicate and complicated nature, demanding the perpetual solicitude of her family and the complete subjugation of their own inclinations. 

It was therefore with a sense of accustomed resignation, rather than any violent alarm, that her eldest daughter, Mrs. Elliot, received a hastily scrawled missive one Tuesday morning. The letter was penned by her mother’s maidservant, but bore the emphatic dictation of Mrs. Vance herself. It conjured a scene of immediate and unparalleled suffering, of a sudden seizure in the chest, a faintness, and a creeping numbness that could only be the precursors to a rapid decline. Mrs. Vance could not answer for the consequences; she must see her dear Eleanor before she breathed her last, and desired she might be brought instantly, leaving all trivial worldly engagements behind.

Eleanor, though well acquainted with her mother’s penchant for the dramatic, could not wholly disregard so melancholy a prognostication. She imparted the intelligence to her husband, Mr. Elliot, a gentleman of very amiable temper and a comfortable estate in the neighbouring county, with the agitation natural to a daughter who, despite everything, retained a strong filial affection. 

Mr. Elliot heard her with the utmost composure. “If Mrs. Vance believes herself to be in danger, my dear, there is certainly nothing to be done but to obey the summons,” he replied, closing the book he was reading with a quiet and unvarying patience. “I will order the carriage immediately. I presume you wish me to accompany you?”

Eleanor thanked him, though she gently intimated that he need not trouble himself; that her mother’s house in Somerset was, at the best of times, but ill-provided with entertainment for a gentleman, and that in the present atmosphere of sickness, his own comfort would be entirely sacrificed. Mr. Elliot, however, would not hear of letting her travel alone; he professed himself perfectly happy to endure a little want of amusement, and in less than an hour they were seated in the carriage, rolling towards Pemberleigh.

They arrived at Beechcroft late in the afternoon, prepared to find the house in a state of suspended mourning. They were ushered into the drawing-room, where Mrs. Vance was discovered reclining upon a sofa, enveloped in a vast quantity of shawls, and supported by pillows of every dimension. A small fire burnt in the grate, though the day was uncommonly warm, and the curtains were half-drawn, casting an air of gloom over the apartment.

At the sight of them, Mrs. Vance uttered a faint moan, and extended a hand, which Eleanor pressed with sympathetic tenderness. 

“My dearest mother,” cried she, “how do you do? You are worse than I feared. What has happened? What has the apothecary said?”

“The apothecary!” repeated Mrs. Vance, in a voice that, though intended to be a whisper, carried the full strength of her indignation. “He is a fool, Eleanor. He knows nothing of my constitution. He has the presumption to tell me it is but a slight cold, brought on by sitting in a draught. A cold! A chill of the vital spirits, a settling of matter upon the lungs, he calls a cold! But I know better. I feel my end approaching. I have not slept these three nights, save in short and unrefreshing snatches.”

Here she was seized with a small cough, which she executed with great precision, looking around to ensure its full effect was registered by her audience.

Mr. Elliot stepped forward with a countenance of the deepest concern. He inquired after the nature of the draught, the precise situation of the window, and the exact hour at which the fatal chill had been contracted. He listened to her circumstantial account with a degree of attention that Eleanor, who had heard a hundred such histories before, could not but admire. He expressed his sorrow that she should have been so neglected, hoped she would take some warm negus, and declared that he would himself inspect the fastenings of every window in the house, that no such mischief might recur.

Mrs. Vance regarded him with an eye of growing complacency. “You are very kind, Mr. Elliot,” said she. “You are all attention. I wish my own son were half as considerate. But sons, I suppose, are too much occupied with their own concerns to attend to a dying mother.”

Mr. Elliot protested that he was sure no son could be more devoted, and added that, for his own part, he should be quite miserable if he did not devote his entire stay to her service. Eleanor, observing this, felt a slight surprise. Mr. Elliot was generally a man of few words, and rather apt to withdraw into his own thoughts when in company; but here he was all animation, all solicitude, pressing Mrs. Vance for further particulars of her sufferings with an earnestness that seemed perfectly sincere.

The following days served only to confirm Eleanor in her astonishment. The expected crisis did not arrive. The “settling of matter upon the lungs” resolved itself into a trifling cough, and the numbness vanished entirely upon the application of a warm brick to the feet. Mrs. Vance, however, did not immediately recover her spirits; she complained of lassitude, of a general weakness that required her to remain upon the sofa, and of a nervous irritability that made the slightest noise insupportable.

Mr. Elliot, far from exhibiting any disappointment at the fraudulence of the alarm, seemed rather to redouble his attentions. He read to her for hours together, selecting passages from works of moral philosophy, which Mrs. Vance pronounced “very improving,” though Eleanor rather suspected she did not understand them. He adjusted her cushions with a dexterity that would have done credit to a lady’s-maid, carried her little negus, walked out in the rain to procure a particular sort of lozenge she affected, and listened to her account of her wrongs with an unwearying patience. He never contradicted her, never suggested she was getting better, and never appeared to wish himself elsewhere.

Eleanor, who had expected her husband to be bored to distraction, beheld this transformation with a mixture of amusement and perplexity. She had known him to be impatient of trifles in his own home, to be eager for his sport, and fond of his library; yet here he was, a willing captive to a valetudinarian, and apparently well content.

She began to suspect that there was some hidden spring of enjoyment in the situation which she had not at first perceived. She watched him more narrowly, and at length discovered, or fancied she discovered, the secret of his contentment. Mr. Elliot, it appeared, took a quiet, philosophical satisfaction in the study of human folly. Mrs. Vance, with her imaginary ailments, her insatiable craving for sympathy, and her absolute unconsciousness of anything beyond her own sensations, was to him a subject of endless, though perfectly good-natured, speculation. He viewed her as a curious specimen of human nature, a living illustration of the power of self-deception, and he found in her absurdities a source of amusement that was altogether innocent, if somewhat ironic.

One morning, as Eleanor sat at her work-table, she observed Mr. Elliot standing by the sofa, listening with an air of grave attention while Mrs. Vance related a long and intricate history of a former illness, in which she had been given over by three physicians, and only restored to life by the singular efficacy of a certain herb tea.

“And do you really believe a word of it?” whispered Eleanor, as they met at the window a few minutes afterwards.

Mr. Elliot smiled. “My dear Eleanor,” he replied, “I believe that your mother believes it, which is all that signifies. If her faith in the herb tea supports her spirits, I should be the last to shake it. There is a great deal to be said for the power of imagination, and your mother is a living monument to it. I confess I find her conversation more entertaining than half the wits in London. One always knows exactly what she will say, and yet one is always surprised that she should say it. It is a great gift, in its way.”

Eleanor laughed, and acknowledged that he was right. She had been vexed at her mother’s selfishness, and alarmed at her own secret impatience; but Mr. Elliot’s serene good-humour and quiet perception of the comic side of things reconciled her to the situation. She saw that he was not deceived, but that he was too kind-hearted to be contemptuous, and too wise to be fretful. 

Mrs. Vance, in the meantime, continued to improve, though she would not own it. She declared Mr. Elliot to be the most attentive son-in-law in the kingdom, and lamented that Eleanor had not half his sense. She prolonged her stay upon the sofa for a full week longer than was necessary, and when at length she rose from it, she did so with the air of one performing a miracle of fortitude. 

When the carriage was announced to carry them away, she embraced Mr. Elliot with tears in her eyes, and pronounced him her preserver. He received her praises with his usual modesty, handed her into the carriage, and as they drove off, turned to Eleanor with a smile.

“Well, my dear,” said he, “I think we may pronounce the crisis past. Your mother will certainly live to summon us again, and I shall be quite ready to come. I have rarely passed a pleasanter week. I have learned more of the art of nursing, and more of the nature of a cold, than I ever hoped to know. And I have had the satisfaction of observing that a good temper is the best specific in the world, both for the patient and the physician.”
