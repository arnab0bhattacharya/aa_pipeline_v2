If any romantic young lady of tender years and a highly susceptible imagination had been induced to take up her permanent abode upon the first floor of Mrs. Grumby’s select suburban villa in Cloudesley Square, Islington, she might have been excused for supposing that the institution was a vast, elaborate, and unwholesome museum, expressly dedicated to the preservation of a single pair of lovers in a state of agonizing suspended animation. For it was in the back-parlour of this very villa that Miss Charlotte Pettle and Mr. Adolphus Sucker had been engaged to be married for seven long, lingering, devastatingly uneventful years.

Seven years! A period of time sufficient to wear out a whole dynasty of London pavements, to exhaust the patience of a whole dynasty of Job-like grandmothers, and to render a young lady’s complexion as pale as the uncooked dough of a Chelsea bun! Mr. Sucker was a young man of that peculiarly elastic species who are perpetually looking towards a golden tomorrow, and perpetually partaking of a very thin and watery today. He was a clerk in the General Office of the Weigh-House, with a forehead that retreated like a modest tide, and a pair of whiskers that seemed to have been pasted on by a friend in a hurry. His prospects, he was accustomed to inform Miss Pettle, were "ripening." They were always ripening. They had been ripening so long that they must, by now, have been entirely rotted away to nothing, had they been actual fruit. But as they were merely figurative fruit, they continued to ripen with a vitality that defied horticulture.

Miss Charlotte Pettle, on the other hand, was a young woman of a firm and rounded figure, a bright and expressive eye, and a spirit that was decidedly beginning to curdle. She had waited, and waited, and waited, until her capacity for waiting was entirely exhausted, and she felt much like a tightly-corked bottle of London porter that has been violently shaken and is only awaiting the slightest hand to pop the wire and send the froth flying to the ceiling.

On a particularly damp and dreary Tuesday evening in November, the back-parlour fire was burning its usual small, economical, and highly deceptive quantity of coals, casting a reddish glow over the faded hair-cloth sofa, the green papier-mâché tray, and the two persons seated upon it. Mr. Sucker was holding Miss Pettle’s hand in a damp, lingering grasp, and looking into her face with an expression of melancholy devotion.

‘My Charlotte,’ murmured Mr. Sucker, giving his head a sentimental little shake, as if to dislodge a tear that had not yet formed, ‘my Charlotte, the time approaches. I feel it in my bones. My prospects are advancing, my love is advancing, everything is advancing!’

‘Everything but the wedding-day, Adolphus,’ said Charlotte, plucking her hand away with a jerk that nearly dislocated his wrist. ‘I am twenty-seven years of age. When you first began to ripen, I was twenty. If I had been a plum, I should have been stewed and eaten long ago!’

‘But, my dearest—’

‘Deest me no dears, Adolphus Sucker!’ cried Charlotte, firing up. ‘I have been “deared” by you until I am perfectly sick of the sound. I tell you what it is, sir; I have made up my mind. Either you name the day before this month is out, or I release you from your engagements, and I go and take a situation as a daily governess!’

‘A daily governess!’ gasped Mr. Sucker, turning pale. ‘My angel!’

‘No angel at all,’ retorted Charlotte, ‘but a woman of flesh and blood, and aching feet, and a determination equal to your uncle’s. Speaking of your uncle, Adolphus, if you cannot marry me without him, marry me when he dies. But marry me you shall, or I am gone!’

‘When my uncle dies!’ repeated Adolphus, catching at the phrase like a drowning man catching at a straw. ‘Ah, Charlotte, if my uncle were to die—’

‘If my uncle were to die!’ repeated a cracked, harsh, and terrible voice from the other side of the room. ‘Ah! If I were to die, hey?’

The young couple started as if they had been shot. They turned their heads, and there, in a large horsehair arm-chair, which had been supposed to be entirely unoccupied, sat Mr. Sucker’s uncle, Mr. Gideon Grunch.

Mr. Grunch was a gentleman of seventy-five, with a face like a cold boiled potato, and a pair of small, red, deeply-set eyes, that twinkled with a fiendish delight in the miseries of others. He was wrapped in a shawl of enormous dimensions, and wore a velvet cap drawn down over his ears. He had come in softly an hour before, while they were at tea, had taken his usual seat, and had fallen into a gentle slumber, from which Charlotte’s spirited declaration had now aroused him.

‘If my uncle were to die!’ repeated Mr. Grunch, with a grin that displayed a set of artificial teeth, which looked as if they had been borrowed from a larger and more formidable animal. ‘A pretty pair of vultures! A pretty pair of grave-watchers! So you are waiting for the old man to pop off his perch, are you, Adolphus? And you are helping him to count the hours, are you, Miss Pettle?’

‘I—I—really, uncle,’ stammered Adolphus, trembling violently, ‘I—I didn’t know you were there.’

‘No, I dare say not,’ said Mr. Grunch. ‘You were too busy measuring me for my coffin. But I heard! Oh, yes, I heard! “When my uncle dies!” says Adolphus. “Marry me when he dies!” says Charlotte. Ha! ha! ha!’

The old gentleman laughed until he was fairly purple in the face, and had to beat himself on the chest with a shrivelled fist to recover his breath.

‘It is very unkind of you, uncle,’ said poor Charlotte, with tears in her eyes. ‘I am sure I have never wished you anything but good.’

‘Haven’t you, my dear?’ said Mr. Grunch. ‘Haven’t you? Well, we shall see. We shall see what a little wholesome exercise of the will can do. I have made up my mind, my dears. I have made up my mind!’

‘Upon what, uncle?’ asked Adolphus, faintly.

‘Upon living!’ roared Mr. Grunch, striking his stick upon the floor with a force that made the green papier-mâché tray ring again. ‘Upon living! Upon living to a green and vigorous old age! Upon living until I am a hundred! Upon living until I am a hundred and twenty! Upon living until I have outlived every ripening prospect in the British Empire, and every daily governess in Islington! I shall live, sir, out of pure, concentrated, and unadulterated Spite!’

Charlotte gazed at him in mute dismay. She saw that he was in earnest. There was a grim, uncompromising determination in his eye that spoke volumes.

‘I shall live,’ continued Mr. Grunch, warming with his subject, ‘I shall live to see you both withered and grey. I shall live to see your whiskers drop off, Adolphus, and your complexion fade, Miss Pettle. I shall live to see the very hair-cloth sofa worn smooth by your impatient waiting. I shall eat my dinners, and drink my port, and take my drops, and live, live, live! I shall become a proverb in Islington. They shall point me out in the street, and say, “There goes Grunch! He is living out of spite!” And when you are both in your graves, I shall dance upon them, and then go home and eat a mutton-chop!’

‘Uncle!’ cried Adolphus, in an agony.

‘Don’t uncle me!’ said Mr. Grunch. ‘I am not your uncle. I am your Immortal Enemy! I am a Fixture! I am an Institution! I am the oldest and the toughest and the most unmanageable Grunch that ever defied destiny and his doctor! And now, sir, as you seem to be quite impatient for my property, you may go and earn some of your own. Good night, Adolphus! Good night, Miss Pettle! I am going to live for ever. Ha! ha! ha!’

He wrapped his shawl more closely around him, leaned back in his chair, and closed his eyes, with a smile of fiendish satisfaction playing upon his thin, cold lips. He looked like a statue erected to the genius of Spite, a monument of unyielding malice, a sardonic mummy that had determined to cheat the sexton of his dues.

And so he remained. And so, I am afraid, he remains to this day. He has taken to eating his mutton-chops with a greater relish than ever. He has discarded his drops, and taken to cold bathing. He has purchased a new almanac, and pinned it up in his bedroom, with all the years from now to nineteen hundred marked in red ink. He has become a phenomenon, a puzzle, a terror to the neighborhood. The very boys in Cloudesley Square point him out to each other as he passes, and whisper, "There goes the old buffer that won't die!" And Miss Charlotte Pettle sits by the fire, and darns Adolphus's stockings, and looks at the coals, and thinks, with a heavy sigh, that if Mr. Grunch's determination is equal to her own, she may wait for her wedding-day until the day after the end of the world.
