It is a curious thing, the determined stupidity of the average man in the face of anything that interferes with his routine. I had thought, in my innocence, that a working model of the Chrono-Optic Apparatus would be quite sufficient to shatter the complacency of the British public. I was quite wrong. I had imagined that the spectacle of tomorrow, brought into the daylight of today, would rouse the government to immediate and frantic action. The event proved me to be a very poor psychologist.

The thing had begun, as most extraordinary things do, in a remarkably dull manner. I had been working in my laboratory in Chelsea—a damp, badly-lit place off the King’s Road, smelling of varnish and ozone—trying to perfect a device for the transmission of visual images over wires. I had hit upon a method of projecting a beam of polarized light through a selenium cell arrangement, with the aim of capturing distant events and throwing them onto a screen. I had expected to see, at best, the traffic on Westminster Bridge or the smoke rising from the Battersea power station. Instead, I saw the end of the world.

It was on a Tuesday afternoon, if I remember rightly, that I first got the machine to function properly. I had left the apparatus running while I went to boil a kettle for tea. When I returned, the screen was no longer blank. It was showing a view of London, but a London that was entirely unfamiliar. The sky was a flat, oppressive sheet of violet-grey, and the Thames was not water at all, but a sluggish, black expanse of what appeared to be boiling tar. I assumed at first that there was some fault in the optics. I adjusted the focus, and the image sharpened with that peculiar, disagreeable clarity that is the chief characteristic of my invention.

Then I saw the Houses of Parliament. They were not merely damaged; they were erased. A great, scooped-out crater lay where the clock tower should have been, and a peculiar, reddish dust lay in drifts against the surviving walls of the abbey. I watched, spellbound, as a flight of enormous, dark shapes moved across the sky with a slow, mechanical rhythm. They were not birds. They were machines, but of a type that no engineer of my acquaintance would have credited. They moved with a silent, fish-like motion, and from their bellies dropped what I can only describe as lines of liquid fire.

I sat down on my stool and stared at the screen for the better part of an hour. The vision did not waver. I saw the streets of what I took to be Croydon laid waste, and a strange, creeping fog advancing over the ruins of Waterloo. I saw people, too—or the shapes of people—moving with a listless, terrified gait through the wreckage. They wore garments of a cut I did not recognize, and their faces had a drawn, livid look, as if they were perpetually on the verge of starvation.

It was three days before I understood that I was not looking at the present. The clue came from a newspaper. On the Friday morning, the *Daily Chronicle* carried a report of a new building being erected on the Chelsea Embankment—a block of flats in a rather garish French Renaissance style, with a great deal of ornamental ironwork. I had seen that building on my screen the previous Tuesday. It was complete, and it was on fire.

I tested the matter very carefully. I noted down a dozen small details from the screen—a peculiar accident at a railway junction in Yorkshire, a speech by the Chancellor of the Exchequer on a tax upon imported grain, an unusually severe thunderstorm over the southern counties. Every one of them, within the space of a fortnight, came to pass with the exactness of a mathematical proof. I was looking into the future, and the future was a catastrophe.

I went first to my friend Bunting. He was a civil engineer of some repute, a man with a comfortable paunch and a mind that ran entirely on rails. I found him in his office in Victoria Street, eating a sandwich and reading a trade catalogue.

“Bunting,” I said, “I have made a discovery.”

“Have you?” he said, with that maddening politeness of his. “Sit down. Have a sandwich?”

“No, thank you. Bunting, I have invented a machine that shows the future.”

He looked at me over his sandwich with a sort of patient, bovine sympathy. “Indeed?” he said. “And what does the future hold, then?”

“War,” I said. “A war such as we have never seen. London will be destroyed. The country will be laid waste. I have seen it.”

Bunting chewed thoughtfully. “My dear fellow,” he said, “you’ve been working too hard. These electrical experiments of yours are very telling on the nerves. I read an article only the other day in the *Lancet* about it. You want a holiday. Take my advice and go to Bournemouth.”

“But I have seen it!” I cried. “I have the machine! Come and look at it!”

He came, because he was a good-natured man, and because he did not believe a word of what I said. He stood in my laboratory with his hat on and his hands in his pockets, and watched the screen with an expression of polite bewilderment.

“What’s that, then?” he asked, pointing to the burning flats on the Embankment.

“That is a building that will not be finished for another six months. I saw it on the screen a week ago. It was completed then.”

“Coincidence,” said Bunting. “Some old fire, I expect. The machine is showing you a photograph, or something of the sort. Very clever, I’m sure, but it’s a trick.”

I showed him the other things. I showed him the strange machines in the sky, the ruined Parliament, the violet sky. He looked at them all with the same mild, uncomprehending stare.

“Pictures,” he said. “Very good pictures. But you can’t expect me to believe they’re the future. It’s against all reason. You’re a scientist, my dear chap. You know as well as I do that the future hasn’t happened yet. You can’t photograph a thing that hasn’t happened.”

I tried to argue with him. I talked about the nature of time, about the possibility that the future was as fixed and as observable as the past, about the implications of a four-dimensional geometry. He waved it all away with a sandwich-scented gesture.

“It won’t do,” he said. “It’s very ingenious, and I dare say you’ll make a lot of money out of it as a toy, but it’s not the future. It can’t be.”

He went away, and I was left alone with my machine and my knowledge.

I went to others. I went to the authorities. I wrote letters to the newspapers. I even, in a fit of desperation, went to a member of Parliament, a man who had once been to dinner at my club and who I thought might have some influence. He was a tall, bland man with a gift for saying nothing at great length. He listened to me with the same air of patient sympathy that Bunting had shown, and said that he was sure the matter would receive every consideration, and that he would bring it to the attention of the proper department. He did nothing of the sort, of course.

The extraordinary thing was that not one of them would look at the evidence. They would not come to the laboratory. They would not examine the machine. They would not even read my notes. They had decided, without inquiry, that the thing was impossible, and no amount of demonstration would shake them. They were like men who, told that the house is on fire, refuse to leave the dinner table because they have never known a fire in the house before.

I tried to rouse them with the sheer horror of what I had seen. I told them about the machines in the sky, about the liquid fire, about the slow, creeping fog that seemed to eat into the very stones of the city. I told them that the country was going to be destroyed, that everything they knew and valued was going to be swept away in a few hours of madness. They heard me out, and then they went back to their sandwiches and their trade catalogues.

I have given up trying to convince them. I sit in my laboratory now, and I watch the screen. The future does not change. The violet sky grows darker, the machines move across it in their slow, terrible procession, and the fires spread across the face of London. I have seen it so often now that it has lost its power to astonish me. It is simply a fact, like the law of gravity or the boiling point of water.

What puzzles me still, in my quieter moments, is not the future itself, but the blank, impenetrable stupidity of the present. Here am I, with a machine that shows the end of everything they know, and they will not look at it. They will not even turn their heads. It is as if the whole world had been anaesthetized, and I alone am left awake to watch the disaster coming.
