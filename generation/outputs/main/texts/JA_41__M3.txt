The shrubbery at Ashcroft Park, though extensive and artfully arranged by a former possessor who had understood the value of a winding path and a thick yew, could not entirely shield its visitors from the prying eyes of those who traversed its gravelled walks with no other intent than to discover them. It was in this retreat, therefore, that Mr. Heywood and Miss Clara Wynne had contrived to meet for the third time in a fortnight—a frequency which, considering the shortness of their acquaintance and the strictness of Miss Wynne’s circumstances, might have appeared to some as the height of imprudence, and to others as the natural consequence of an attachment which had nothing else to recommend it.

Mr. Heywood was the possessor of eight thousand a year and a settled estate in the adjoining county. Miss Wynne was the possessor of nothing more remarkable than a pair of very fine dark eyes, a limited wardrobe, and that species of genteel poverty which, while it preserves the outward forms of respectability, renders them peculiarly painful to those who are compelled to maintain them. She resided at Ashcroft Park under the protection and at the expense of her aunt, Mrs. Pennington, a widow of good family, narrow fortune, and an unbounded conviction that she understood the conduct of human affairs better than any person in England.

The conversation of the young couple, at the moment of interruption, had reached that interesting but delicate stage where the protestations of the gentleman had begun to outstrip the calculations of the lady. Mr. Heywood had been declaring, with an ardour which his situation rendered natural and his character rendered sincere, that he valued independence of fortune far below the society of a woman of true understanding; and Miss Wynne, whose understanding was sufficiently clear to comprehend the value of eight thousand a year, had been endeavouring to reconcile the dictates of her heart with the suggestions of her prudence, when the gravel beneath their feet gave notice of an approaching step, and Mrs. Pennington appeared round the corner of the espalier.

To have looked guilty would have been to confirm every suspicion; to have looked wholly at ease would have been to defy it. Miss Wynne chose the middle course, which is open to few but the young and the bewildered—she coloured deeply, dropped her companion’s arm, and said nothing. Mr. Heywood, who had more presence of mind and less knowledge of the world, bowed with great readiness and expressed his hope that Mrs. Pennington was well. Mrs. Pennington, who was extremely well, and rather pleased than otherwise to find herself so situated, received the address with a degree of composure that surprised them both.

“I see,” said she, fixing her eye upon her niece with the gravity of one who has penetrated a design of national importance, “that I have interrupted a conversation of some moment. I shall be very sorry to be an obstacle to anything that is proper; but I must beg, Mr. Heywood, that you will allow me to judge what is proper, for I have known Clara from a child, and am, I believe, the best judge of what is for her happiness.”

Mr. Heywood, who had not hitherto suspected Mrs. Pennington of any particular interest in his affairs, was a good deal astonished; but he had the good sense to perceive that silence was the only prudent reply to a speech so compounded of authority and condescension. He therefore only bowed again, and Mrs. Pennington, having fixed the time and place of a future interview with the precision of a general arranging a campaign, suffered them to part.

It was on the following morning that Mrs. Pennington commenced her operations, and it soon became evident that she had undertaken the management of the affair with a zeal which, if it had been directed by any knowledge of the parties concerned, might have been useful, but which, as it was founded entirely upon her own preconceived notions of what a courtship ought to be, could not fail to be embarrassing.

Her first measure was to insist that Mr. Heywood should formally apply to her for permission to address her niece, as though she stood in the place of a parent and Clara were the heiress of a considerable fortune. Mr. Heywood, who was perfectly willing to conduct the matter with all proper respect, waited upon her accordingly, and was received with a solemnity which would have better suited the negotiation of a treaty than the proposal of a marriage.

Mrs. Pennington begged him to be seated, and then entered into a discourse upon the subject of family connexions, settlements, and the duties of married life, which lasted for upwards of half an hour and contained very little that was applicable to the case in hand. She spoke of the necessity of providing for the younger branches of a family, though she had no branches of her own; she dilated upon the importance of a proper establishment, though she had never kept one; and she concluded by observing that, as Clara had nothing, it would be necessary for Mr. Heywood to settle a very considerable sum upon her, not so much for her own comfort as for the credit of the family into which she was to marry.

Mr. Heywood, who had always intended to act with the utmost liberality, heard this part of her discourse with more patience than pleasure. He was not unwilling to be generous, but he was unwilling to be dictated to; and Mrs. Pennington’s manner of prescribing the exact sum, the precise mode of settlement, and the particular house in which they were to reside, gave him a sensation which he could not but feel was very unlike the sentiment with which he had entered the room.

When he was at length permitted to see Miss Wynne, he found that lady in a state of considerable agitation, which was by no means diminished by her aunt’s determination to be present during the whole of the visit. Mrs. Pennington seated herself between them with an air of cheerful vigilance, and addressed herself to the conversation with so much industry that the young people could scarcely exchange a sentence without her intervention. She took upon herself to answer every question that was addressed to Clara, to propose every subject that was started, and to conclude every remark with an observation of her own, which, if it did not always advance the discourse, at least effectually prevented it from becoming too particular.

Mr. Heywood bore this restraint for three visits, with a patience which did him credit, or at least did credit to the strength of his attachment; but on the fourth, finding that Mrs. Pennington had added to her other precautions that of leaving the door open and placing a maid-servant in the antechamber, he took an opportunity, while the good lady was expatiating upon the merits of a particular kind of wedding-cake, of leaning towards Miss Wynne and murmuring, in a tone of considerable feeling, that he began to think her aunt’s management was likely to prove more effectual than any opposition could have been.

Miss Wynne, whose distress had been increasing with every visit, could only reply by a look of such genuine vexation as convinced him that she, at least, was no party to these arrangements. It was fortunate for them both that Mr. Heywood’s affection was of a kind to be strengthened rather than diminished by opposition; and he left Ashcroft that evening with a resolution which Mrs. Pennington’s interference had very nearly rendered desperate, but which it had ultimately served to confirm.

The crisis was brought on by Mrs. Pennington’s deciding, with her accustomed promptitude, that the wedding should take place from Ashcroft Park, that the breakfast should be conducted upon a scale of peculiar magnificence, and that she herself should preside over the whole arrangement. She wrote to Mr. Heywood to communicate these decisions, and received, in reply, a letter which, though perfectly civil in its language, contained the information that he and Miss Wynne had been married that morning at the parish church, by special licence, and in the presence of only two witnesses.

Mrs. Pennington’s indignation was, for some hours, too great for utterance; and when it at last found vent, it was directed not so much against the parties concerned as against the perversity of human affairs in general, which could never be persuaded to conduct themselves according to the plans of those who were best qualified to direct them.
