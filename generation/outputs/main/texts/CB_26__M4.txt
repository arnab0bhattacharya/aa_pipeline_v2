The letter lay upon the table in my little chamber, its black seal broken, its folds already softened by much handling. I had read it at least a dozen times since the servant had brought it up at dusk, and still the words refused to settle into sense. *We buried him on Thursday last, in the rain.* My brother—my only brother, Edmund—was dead. The rain had fallen upon his coffin, and I had been here, three hundred miles away, teaching the daughters of strangers the difference between the indicative and the subjunctive mood.

I sat upon the edge of my bed, still in my teaching gown, and watched the candle gutter in its socket. The house was quiet now; the last bell had rung an hour since, and the girls were abed, and Mrs. Hartwell, our headmistress, had retired to her own apartments with her customary punctuality. No one had knocked at my door. No one had asked if I were unwell, though I had descended to supper with a face, I doubt not, as white as the napkin at my plate. I was a useful teacher, a competent teacher; I was not a woman whom anyone thought to inquire after.

I did not weep. I sat quite still, and the grief that filled me was not the soft, dissolving grief of tears, but a hard and burning thing that lodged beneath my ribs and would not be dislodged. I thought of Edmund as a boy, running across the frost-bound fields behind our father's house, his breath steaming in the cold air, his hand outstretched to catch mine. I thought of him as a man, stooped over his ledgers in the counting-house, his face grown thin and gray with the labour of keeping us both from the poorhouse. He had sent me the money for my passage to this foreign city; he had written, *You must not fret about me; I am quite well.* And now he was dead, and I was here, and the candle was guttering.

I rose at last and put the letter in the drawer of my little escritoire, beneath my Bible, and I undressed myself with the mechanical care of one who has performed the same motions a thousand times. I did not sleep. The hours passed over me like slow water, and I lay staring at the dark ceiling, listening to the wind as it moaned in the chimneys and rattled the casement. Toward dawn, I rose again and washed my face in cold water, and looked at myself in the glass. My eyes were dry and bright; my cheeks were pale, but not, I thought, alarmingly so. I dressed myself with particular attention, choosing my plainest collar and my most sober ribbon, and I descended to the schoolroom to face the day.

The morning was raw and misty, and the schoolroom, when I entered it, was cold. The fire had not yet been lit, and the girls stood in clusters about the windows, their breath visible in the chill air, their voices subdued. They fell silent when they saw me. I went to my desk and laid out my books, and I heard, behind me, the whisper of their glances. They knew nothing, of course; they could not know. But they perceived, with the unerring instinct of the young, that something was amiss, and they watched me as they might watch a stranger.

I called the first form to order. We were to construe a passage of Cicero that morning—a passage upon the duties of friendship, which I had chosen a fortnight since in my innocence. I opened my book, and the Latin swam before my eyes. I blinked, and the words resolved themselves, and I began to hear the girls recite. *Quid enim?* said the first, a tall girl with a heavy German accent. *What then?* I corrected her pronunciation, and she began again. My voice, I observed with a kind of distant wonder, was quite steady. It issued from my throat as though it belonged to another woman.

But when the second girl stumbled over a subjunctive, and I rose to explain the construction, I felt something rise within me—a pressure in my chest, a swelling in my throat—and I was obliged to pause. I turned to the window and looked out at the mist, and I said, *Continue, if you please*, and the girl continued, and I heard her voice as though from a great distance.

*Are you well, Miss Ashby?* asked the girl beside her, in a low tone.

*I am quite well*, I said. *Attend to your lesson.*

The morning wore on. I heard recitations; I corrected exercises; I set a theme upon the death of Socrates, and then, recollecting myself, I changed it to the death of Cato, which seemed to me less perilous. The girls wrote, their pens scratching upon the paper, and I sat at my desk and looked at the letter in my drawer—for I had brought it down with me, I know not why, as though it were a talisman or a wound that I must keep open. I did not open the drawer. I sat with my hands folded upon the desk, and I thought of Edmund in the rain, and I thought of the words I had written to him last, *I am quite well*, and I thought of the words he had written to me, *I am quite well*, and I wondered if he had known, when he wrote them, that they were a lie.

At noon, Mrs. Hartwell sent for me. She was a stout, florid woman, with a face like a full moon and a manner of brisk condescension, and she received me in her parlour with her knitting in her hands.

*Miss Ashby*, said she, *I am told you are not yourself today. The girls have remarked it. I trust there is nothing in your circumstances which unfits you for your duties.*

*There is nothing, madam*, I replied. *I am quite well.*

*You are pale*, she said, *and you were late to the schoolroom this morning.*

*I was not late*, I said. *I was punctual to the minute.*

*You were not yourself*, she repeated. *I must remind you, Miss Ashby, that the reputation of this establishment depends upon the composure and regularity of its teachers. The girls are sensitive to any disorder. If there is anything which troubles you, I must request that you leave it at the door of the schoolroom.*

I looked at her—at her full moon face, at her knitting needles, at the comfortable fire that burned in her grate—and I felt the hard thing beneath my ribs stir and kindle.

*Madam*, I said, *my brother is dead.*

She set down her knitting. Her face did not change, but her eyes, for a moment, flickered.

*I am sorry to hear it*, she said. *When did this happen?*

*He was buried on Thursday last*, I said. *In the rain.*

*You have my sympathy*, she said. *But you will understand that I cannot permit the school to be disrupted. You may have the afternoon to yourself, if you wish it. But tomorrow you must be as you were.*

*As I was*, I repeated.

*As you were*, she said. *The girls must not be disturbed.*

I inclined my head and withdrew. I went back to my chamber, and I sat upon the bed, and I looked at the letter in my drawer, and I did not weep. I thought of Edmund in the rain, and I thought of the girls in the schoolroom, and I thought of Mrs. Hartwell's full moon face, and I felt a strange, cold clarity descend upon me. I was alone. No one here knew me; no one here would mourn with me; no one here would permit me to mourn. I must be as I was. I must be composed, and regular, and punctual; I must leave my grief at the door of the schoolroom, as though it were a muddy umbrella.

I rose and went to the window. The mist had lifted, and the city lay below me, gray and strange, its spires and roofs glistening in a pale, watery sun. I thought of Edmund, and I thought of the fields behind our father's house, and I thought of the rain upon his coffin. And I said to myself, in a low voice, as though he could hear me: *I am quite well, Edmund. I am quite well.*

And then I turned back to my desk, and I took out my books, and I prepared my lessons for the morrow.
