The mist had risen from the river, leaving the streets wiped clean and glistening, so that the very cobblestones seemed to breathe a silver, uncertain light. Henry Bracken stood upon the bridge, watching the brown water swirl beneath him in thick, sluggish coils, and thought how odd it was that he should be here, in the very heart of London, and yet feel entirely disembodied, as if he had left his actual self behind in the cramped parlour at Selwick, among the faded antimacassars and his mother’s anxious, unblinking gaze. But no—he was here; he had the stiff leather of his case beneath his fingers, and in his breast pocket the letter from Mr. Aldous, of the firm of Aldous and Quigley, Publishers, stating that they would be pleased to see him between the hours of ten and twelve. It was now half-past eleven. He must walk swiftly. Yet he lingered, for the city opened out before him with such strange, terrifying magnificence—the spires and chimneys dissolving into the soft October haze, and everywhere, everywhere, the multitude surging past him, each face shut up in its own private compartment of thought, each pair of legs driven by some imperative he could not guess at. Where were they all going? Not to Aldous and Quigley, certainly; not to enquire after rooms in dingy streets where the paint peeled from the window-frames and landladies stood in doorways wiping their hands on greasy aprons. No, they were going to offices, to warehouses, to love-affairs, to quarrels, to cups of tea poured out in quiet back-kitchens—and the whole vast, intricate machinery of their going was as incomprehensible to him as the workings of some immense, indifferent animal.

He turned at last and walked along the Embankment, past the book-sellers whose stalls displayed sodden, yellowed volumes that had seen better days, and so into the narrow streets where the publishing houses stood, respectable and shuttered, with their brass plates polished to a dull, discreet gleam. Mr. Aldous had an office on the second floor; the stairs were carpeted in a thin, threadbare strip of red that muffled his footsteps and gave him the curious sensation of ascending into some solemn, ecclesiastical silence. He was shown into a room where the light fell slantwise through a high window, illuminating the dust that hung perpetually in the air, and there, behind a desk piled with manuscripts, sat Mr. Aldous himself—a small, pale man with spectacles and an air of infinite, weary patience, who listened to Henry’s stumbling, over-rehearsed sentences with a faint, kindly smile that was worse than any frown.

“You write, I think you said?” Mr. Aldous murmured, turning a paper-weight over and over in his fingers—a small glass globe containing a miniature landscape of snow. “Fiction? Yes. Well, we are always glad to see new work. But the market, you understand, is exceedingly difficult just now. Exceedingly difficult. I should advise you—” and here he paused, removing his spectacles and rubbing the bridge of his nose as if the very act of speaking had fatigued him beyond endurance—“I should advise you to try the magazines first. Build up a reputation, however modest, and then—well, then one never knows.”

Henry had emerged into the street again, the interview over, the letter in his pocket now seeming to weigh upon him like a stone. He had not shown them anything; he had not even spoken of what he wished to write, of the great, formless thing that pressed against the inside of his skull and would not take shape, that tormented him in the small hours and vanished like smoke when he sat down at the desk in his bedroom at Selwick, staring at the blank white expanse of paper. What was it? He could not say. It had something to do with the quality of light on wet pavements; something to do with the expression in a stranger’s eyes, caught for an instant in a passing omnibus and gone before one could read it; something to do with the sense, which had struck him just now on the bridge, of being at once utterly alone and yet implicated in the lives of all these thousands, as if each passerby were a thread in some vast, invisible tapestry whose pattern he was forbidden to see.

He walked, and walked, and the day wore on. He looked at rooms. There was one in a street off the Gray’s Inn Road, a narrow, tall house with a green door and a knocker shaped like a lion’s head, brass and tarnished. The landlady, a Mrs. Pettigrew, led him up three flights of stairs, her breath coming in short, laboured gasps, and showed him a room that looked out over a soot-blackened courtyard where a single, stunted tree pushed its bare branches against the sky. The room contained a bed, a washstand, a chair, and a small, cracked mirror in which his face appeared pale and somewhat distorted, as if the glass itself had grown weary of reflecting and wished to be left in peace. The rent was twelve and six a week. He stood in the centre of the room and tried to imagine himself writing there—tried to see himself seated at the rickety table by the window, the gas-fire hissing in its grate, the words flowing from his pen in that easy, abundant stream he had so often envisioned. But the room resisted him; it had its own life, its own accumulated silence of previous tenants, of nights spent in sleeplessness and anxious thought, and he could not force himself into it.

“It’s a very nice room,” he said, “very quiet,” and Mrs. Pettigrew, who had been watching him with the shrewd, calculating gaze of one who has learned to read the financial standing of a young man from the cut of his coat, nodded slowly and said that it was, and that she had had gentlemen staying there for years, and never a complaint.

By the late afternoon he was footsore and bewildered, and had fallen into that state of mind in which the external world ceases to be altogether real and begins to resemble a panorama seen through glass—the buses, the hawkers, the shop-windows full of goods he could not buy, the women hurrying past with shopping baskets, the clerks streaming homeward in their dark, respectable suits—all of it moving past him like figures on a stage, silent and two-dimensional. He had eaten a bun in a tea-shop, sitting at a small marble-topped table while the waitress, a tired girl with red hands, wiped crumbs from the surface with a damp cloth, and he had thought, looking at her, that here was a life as obscure and as irremediable as his own, and that she, too, perhaps, carried within her some unspoken, inarticulate thing, some dim awareness of beauty or sorrow that would never find its expression.

And now it was evening, and the lamps were being lit, and the city, which had seemed so harsh and glaring by day, softened into a haze of gold and amber, the shop-fronts glowing with a warm, seductive light, the windows of the houses above revealing, here and there, a glimpse of a curtained interior—flowers on a table, a woman bending over a fire, the blue flicker of a gas-mantle—and all of it charged, now, with a significance so piercing, so intolerably poignant, that he felt his throat tighten and his eyes sting as he walked. He had found no room; he had secured no position; he had written nothing. And yet he had seen—what? The mist on the river; the dust in Mr. Aldous’s office; the stunted tree in the courtyard; the red hands of the waitress; the lion’s-head knocker, tarnished, on the green door. Fragments, all of them; disconnected, meaningless, perhaps. And yet, as he walked back across the bridge in the gathering dusk, with the river below him reflecting the scattered lights of the city in long, trembling ribbons of gold and crimson, he felt, with a certainty that defied analysis, that somewhere within the chaos of the day, within the very heart of his failure, there lay concealed the thing he had come to find—not a room, not a position, but something subtler, more elusive, something that could not be named or seized, but only, perhaps, at some distant hour, written.
