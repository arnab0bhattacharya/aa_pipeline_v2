The house stood at the corner of a narrow street whose name I never learned to pronounce, in a quarter of the town where the gables leaned toward one another like old women exchanging confidences, and where the cobbles were so worn by the passage of centuries that they held the rain in shallow, glimmering cups. It was a tall, narrow dwelling, brick upon brick, with windows that caught the western light and threw it back in sullen flashes, and a door of black oak studded with iron, upon which I knocked three times before my courage failed me and I nearly turned away.

I had come to be housekeeper to Doctor Émile Vandenberghe, physician to the poor of the parish of Saint-Rombaut, a man of middle years whose fame for charity had reached even the lodging-house in Dover where I had spent the winter in a species of living death, counting my coins and my regrets in equal measure. The letter he had written in answer to my application was brief and businesslike, and I had read it so many times that the creases had worn through at the folds. He required a woman of method and discretion, he wrote, to manage his household and to assist in the keeping of his accounts; the work was hard, the pay modest, the situation solitary. I had replied that solitude was no terror to me, and that I had a constitution of iron and a will of the same material. I had not told him why I had left England. I had not told him that I had left a grave behind me, and a name that I wished to bury with it.

The door was opened by a woman of perhaps forty years, whose face was the colour of tallow and whose eyes were black and quick and suspicious. She was dressed in mourning, though for whom I could not guess, and she held a candle in her hand as though it were a weapon.

“You are the Englishwoman,” she said, in French that was thick with the accent of the Low Countries. “You are late.”

“The packet was delayed by fog,” I answered. “I came as soon as I might.”

She looked at me for a long moment, taking in my plain black gown, my shawl, my travelling-bag, my face—which I knew was pale and thin and not at all the face of a woman who had come to bring comfort into a house. Then she stepped aside, but only just, so that I was obliged to brush against her as I entered.

“I am Joséphine Vandenberghe,” she said. “The doctor’s sister. He is not here. He is with a patient—there is always a patient. I keep the house when he is gone, and I have kept it these twenty years. I did not ask for an English housekeeper.”

“No,” I said. “I imagine you did not.”

She led me up a narrow stair to a room at the back of the house, which was clean and bare and looked out upon a small courtyard where a single pear-tree stood, black and crooked against the grey sky. There was a bed, a chair, a table, a crucifix upon the wall. She set the candle down upon the table and turned to me with her hands folded at her waist.

“You are a Protestant,” she said. It was not a question.

“I am.”

“And you have come here, to a Catholic house, in a Catholic town, to keep the house of a man who gives his life to the poor of the Church. You will forgive me if I ask why.”

“I came because I needed work,” I said, “and because your brother offered it. I have no other reason.”

“No other reason,” she repeated, and her lips curved in something that was not a smile. “You English are very good at having no other reason. You come with your Bibles and your cold faces and you tell us that we are idolaters, and then you wonder why we do not love you.”

“I have not come to tell you anything,” I said. “I have come to keep your brother’s accounts and to see that his linen is mended and his meals are hot. If I can do that without preaching, I think we shall do very well.”

She looked at me again, and I saw something flicker in her black eyes—not warmth, precisely, but the beginning of a question that she did not know how to ask. Then she went out and closed the door behind her, and I sat down upon the bed and pressed my hands together and told myself that I had endured worse than this, and that I would endure this too.

The doctor came home at dusk. I heard him in the hall below, his voice low and kind, and the voice of a woman weeping, and the sound of coins counted out upon a table. Then the door of the parlour opened and he came in, a tall man with grey at his temples and a face that was worn thin by other people’s sorrows, and he took my hand in both of his and said, “Mistress Hale, you are welcome, you are truly welcome,” and I felt the tears start in my eyes, though I could not have told you why.

His sister stood in the doorway behind him, watching. She saw the tears. She saw me dash them away with the back of my hand as though they were a weakness I could not afford. And I saw her see it, and I knew that she had marked it down in the ledger of her suspicions, and that she would not forget.

The days that followed were days of hard work and little speech. I rose before dawn and set the fire and made the bread and boiled the linen, and I balanced the doctor’s books at the parlour table while the light of the candle guttered and the wind came off the canal and rattled the shutters. The patients came and went—the lame, the blind, the consumptive, the women with too many children and too little bread—and I learned to know them by their knock, and to have a cup of broth ready for the worst of them before the doctor had even opened the door. Joséphine watched me. She watched me fold the sheets and count the spoons and sweep the hearth. She watched me when I thought she was not looking, and when I looked up she did not look away.

One evening, when the rain beat against the windows and the doctor was gone to a confinement in the next street, she came into the parlour and sat down opposite me at the table, and folded her hands, and said, “You do not go to Mass.”

“No.”

“You do not pray with us in the evening.”

“No.”

“And yet you work like a woman who is trying to earn something she cannot pay for.” She leaned forward. “What is it, Mistress Hale? What have you done?”

I set down my pen. The fire cracked and settled. I thought of the grave in the churchyard at home, and the name upon the stone, and the thing I had done that had driven me from England—not a crime, but a shame, which is worse; not a sin that could be confessed, but a secret that could only be carried. I thought of telling her. I thought of the words forming on my tongue, and of how they would sound in that cold room, and of how she would look at me afterward.

“I have done nothing,” I said, “that I am obliged to tell you.”

She rose. Her face was hard, but her voice, when she spoke, was not. “You are proud,” she said. “That is the English disease. You think your silence is a virtue. But I have lived long enough to know that silence is only a virtue when it protects the innocent. When it protects the guilty, it is a kind of lie.”

She went out. I sat alone with the fire and the rain and the books I had been balancing, and I found that my hands were shaking, and that I could not for the life of me remember what I had been writing.

The next morning I rose earlier than usual and went down to the kitchen, and I made the bread and set the fire and swept the hearth, and when Joséphine came in I said to her, without turning round, “I was married to a man who was not kind to me. He died. I left England because I could not bear to stay in the house where he had lived, or to hear his name spoken by people who did not know what he was. I have not told your brother because I did not wish to be a burden, or an object of pity, or a scandal. That is all. You may believe it or not, as you please.”

She was silent for a long moment. Then she came and stood beside me at the table, and she took the loaf I had made from my hands and set it down, and she said, “I believe it.”

I looked at her. Her black eyes were still sharp, but the suspicion in them had softened into something else—something that was not quite trust, but was perhaps the ground in which trust might grow.

“You will tell my brother,” she said. “Not today. But soon. He is a good man, and he does not like secrets in his house. And I—” she hesitated. “I will not speak of it until you do.”

She turned away and began to set the table for the doctor’s breakfast, and I stood there with the flour still on my hands and the fire warm on my face, and I thought that perhaps I had come to a place where I might stay.
