The cloister of the Immaculate Heart stood stark and square against the low, grey skies of Vaelkeren, its brick walls holding the damp of the Scheldt like a sponge holds water. I had been engaged there to instruct the French and English tongues, and to oversee the evening hours of the boarders. It was a post of little emolument and much labour, yet I did not repine. I had sought employment, and I had found it; I had bread, and a roof, and for these blessings I was willing to render a strict and faithful account. But Providence, it seemed, was not yet done with my trials. A peculiar burden was laid upon me—a charge that was to prove a crucible for my temper and my principles.

Madame Vaelkens, the directress, a woman of formidable brow and unyielding will, had a daughter. Clara was fifteen, sharp-featured, and possessed of a dark, searching eye that seemed to calculate the weakness of every soul it encountered. She was a day-pupil, yet she lingered in my charge through the long, dim afternoons, exempt by her mother’s decree from the menial tasks allotted to the others. Her cleverness was indisputable; her deceit, equally so. She was a creature of masks, adopting whatever mien would best serve her immediate design, and her chief sport was to set snares for the teachers, reporting every misstep, real or imagined, to her mother.

On a particularly bleak afternoon, with the rain lashing the leaded windows and the wind howling through the quadrangle, I sat at the high desk to superintend the sewing. The room was cold, the fire in the stove niggardly fed by the frugal housekeeper. The girls stitched in silence, their fingers numb, their breath misting the air. Clara, seated at the front, had abandoned her work. She was gazing at me with a fixed, sardonic stare, her eyes reflecting the dull, red glow of the iron stove.

"Mademoiselle," she said, her French clipped and precise, "is it true that in England you beat children for misdemeanours?"

A flush mounted to my cheek. I knew the question was a snare. If I spoke leniently, she would report me as weak; if I spoke harshly, she would carry tales of my tyranny. "In England," I replied, keeping my voice even, "we govern by reason, not by the rod. The rod is for the stubborn and the dull."

"And for the deceitful?" she pressed, a faint smile hovering about her lips.

"For the deceitful," I said, meeting her gaze squarely, "we reserve a sterner punishment. We expose them to themselves. We hold up their falsehoods to the light, and let them see how ugly a thing is a lie."

Her smile vanished. She picked up her needle, but I could see the malice working behind her eyes. She would not forget, nor would she forgive. The afternoon dragged on. The light faded; the rain turned to sleet, rattling against the panes like shot. When the bell rang for dismissal, the girls rose and filed out. Clara lingered, adjusting her cloak with elaborate care.

"Mademoiselle," she said, "my mother desires that I remain until the carriage comes. She says I am to use your room for my preparation."

"It is not my room," I said. "It is the schoolroom. You may remain, provided you observe the rules of the house."

"Rules," she murmured, fastening the clasp at her throat. "They are such fragile things, are they not? Like glass, they shatter at a touch."

"They shatter only when struck with intent to break," I replied. "To the honest hand, they hold firm."

She departed then, leaving a chill that was not of the weather. I gathered my books and followed, my heart heavy with a sense of impending conflict.

That conflict was not long delayed. Two days later, I was summoned to Madame Vaelkens’ parlour. The room was dark, hung with heavy tapestries that seemed to absorb the meagre light. Madame sat behind a vast oak table, her hands folded, her face set in its usual mask of severe authority. Clara stood beside her, demure, her eyes cast down, the very picture of injured innocence.

"Mademoiselle Hale," Madame began, her voice flat and cold as the flags of a dungeon, "my daughter informs me that you have been speaking to her of punishment—of beatings, and of exposures. Is this the instruction my money purchases? Is this the sort of moral philosophy you bring from England to corrupt the minds of our youth?"

My heart beat hard, but I would not tremble. I knew myself innocent of any intent to harm, yet I knew also that innocence is not always a shield. I stood straight, my hands clasped behind me, and met her gaze.

"Madame," I said, "I spoke no word that was not prompted by your daughter’s question. She asked me of the English mode of discipline. I answered her truthfully, and in such terms as I thought fitting for a young woman of her understanding. I spoke of reason and of truth, not of cruelty."

"She says you called her deceitful."

"I said that deceit is a sterner fault than dullness, and that it merits a sterner reckoning. I did not apply the word to her person. But if the cap fits—"

"Mademoiselle!" Madame’s voice rose, sharp with indignation. Clara’s eyes flickered, a flash of triumph in their depths. She had set her trap, and I had walked into it. But I would not be caught. I had spoken no lie, and I would not retract the truth to soothe a mother’s vanity.

"Madame," I continued, my voice dropping to a quieter register, a register that I have found carries further than a shout, "I am here to teach, and to govern. I cannot do either if my every word is to be twisted and carried back to you as evidence of malice. Your daughter is clever—far cleverer than most of her age. She knows it, and she uses it. She baits the other girls; she tries my patience; she seeks to set my authority at naught. I have not been harsh. I have not been cruel. But I have been firm, and I will continue to be firm. If you wish me to be otherwise, you must find another teacher."

The silence that followed was profound. The wind moaned in the passage outside. The fire in the grate hissed and spat. Madame Vaelkens looked at me with something like surprise—not anger, but a reassessment. She was a woman who respected strength, even when it opposed her. Clara’s demure expression had cracked; a sullen flush crept over her face. She had not expected resistance. She had expected capitulation, or at least a stammering defence. She had found neither.

"You are very bold, Mademoiselle," Madame said at last.

"I am very honest, Madame. There is a difference."

Another silence. Then, slowly, Madame nodded. "You will continue as before. Clara, go to your room."

Clara turned and left, her step slow, her back rigid with resentment. When the door closed, Madame Vaelkens leaned forward. "She is my only child," she said, and for a moment the mask slipped, and I saw beneath it a mother’s fear—a mother who knew her daughter’s nature and dreaded its fruit. "She is not easy to govern."

"No child is," I said gently. "But she is not beyond governance. She needs an adversary worthy of her wit—not a tyrant, but a mind that can match hers and hold the line. Give me your leave to be that adversary, and I will do what I can."

Madame looked at me for a long moment, then nodded again. "You have it."

I left the parlour with a lighter step. The sleet had turned to rain again, and the gutters ran dark. I crossed the quadrangle, the wet cobblestones gleaming under the single lamp. The wind struck me, cold and keen, but I did not mind it. I felt a fire within that no wind could extinguish—a fierce, quiet resolve. I had not surrendered, and I had not been cruel. I had held the line, and the line had held.
