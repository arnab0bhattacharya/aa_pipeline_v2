It was in the bleakest November of my life that I sought the solitary cottage upon the rugged cliffs of Blackmore, a spot where the wild Devonshire seas beat with a ceaseless, unrelenting fury against the dark, jagged rocks below. I had been a wife, cherished and exalted, for scarcely eleven fleeting months when the treacherous waters swallowed up the vessel that bore my Edmund homeward, tearing him from my embrace, and leaving me a lone and desolate widow upon the earth. The world had become a hollow, mocking theatre to me; the faces of men, with their unmeaning smiles and their professed sympathies, were but so many daggers pointed at my bleeding heart. I shunned the habitations of the distant village; I dismissed the kindly importunities of the few neighbors who sought to offer their consolations; and I bound myself, in the sternness of my despair, to the society of my own grief, resolving to hold no communion with any living soul, but to weave my dark destiny with the howling winds and the mournful billows that had bereaved me.

The cottage I had chosen was a lowly, humble dwelling, situated so perilously near the edge of the precipice that, in the tempestuous hours of the night, the spray of the ocean was dashed against my latticed window, as if the elements conspired to remind me of the abyss into which my happiness had been hurled. Here, in this wild and sublime seclusion, I passed my days and nights. I would sit for hours by the expiring embers of my fire, listening to the roar of the deep, which seemed to echo the horrid shriek with which my soul had parted from its joy. The sea, that mighty and remorseless monster, was ever present to my view, a vast and moving tomb wherein lay entombed all my earthly hopes. I gazed upon its black and heaving surface with a sentiment akin to horror, yet I could not withdraw my eyes; for beneath those sullen waves reposed the form I had so dearly loved, and I felt, at times, a frantic, agonizing wish that the waters might rise to their utmost limit, overwhelm my frail tenement, and bury me in the same cold embrace that held him.

It was in the deepest depth of winter, when the tempests raged with most unmitigated fury, and the very air seemed laden with the breath of desolation, that a circumstance occurred which, though trifling in itself, powerfully affected my imagination, and awoke within my bosom emotions long strangers to it. One morning, after a night of more than ordinary turbulence, when I opened my door to breathe the keen, frosty air, I perceived, upon the threshold, a small bunch of wild flowers, pale and frost-touched, yet bearing the unmistakable mark of having been freshly gathered and laid there by a human hand. I started, as if I had trodden upon a serpent. The sight of these simple offerings, so delicately placed, so indicative of a timid, shrinking sympathy, thrilled me with a sensation I could not define. It was not anger, for my heart was too heavy for resentment; nor was it gratitude, for I had vowed to receive no kindness; but it was a strange, bewildering mixture of surprise and a soft, involuntary melting of the icy rigour of my grief. I took the flowers in my hand, and as I gazed upon their drooping petals, the tears, which for many weeks had been denied me, gushed forth in a copious tide, and I wept—Oh, how bitterly!—over the frail memorials of a benevolence I had not sought.

I knew not from whom this silent tribute proceeded, nor did I seek to know; but day after day, as the winter wore heavily on, the same offering was renewed. Sometimes it was a sprig of ling, gathered from the desolate moors; sometimes a few sea-pinks, culled from the crevices of the rocks; and once, a single, half-blown primrose, that spoke of the approaching spring. They were always placed at my door in the early dawn, before the sun had risen above the eastern hills, and I never caught a glimpse of my unseen benefactor. Yet I felt, by a mysterious intuition, that some gentle and sorrowing spirit was hovering near me, drawn towards me by the consciousness of kindred suffering.

It was not until the opening of March, when the winds had somewhat abated their fury, and the earth began to put on the first faint tints of reviving verdure, that I at length discovered the author of these mute attestations of sympathy. I had risen earlier than usual, and, moved by a curiosity I could no longer repress, I looked through my narrow lattice. The dawn was just breaking over the sea, casting a cold, grey light upon the waters, when I beheld, at a little distance from my door, a female figure, slight and youthful, who was in the act of depositing a small wreath of ivy and snowdrops upon the stone. She was poorly, yet neatly clad; and as she turned to withdraw, I caught a glimpse of her countenance, and recognized the features of a young girl, whom I had sometimes observed from a distance, wandering, solitary and pensive, upon the cliffs. She could not have numbered more than fifteen years; but upon her young face was stamped a gravity and melancholy that belonged to riper years, and which spoke, even more eloquently than words, of the secret sorrows that preyed upon her heart.

I opened the door, and the sound of my footsteps startled her. She paused, and, turning towards me, fixed upon me her large, dark eyes, swimming in an expression of fear and deprecation. I was moved. "Child," I said, "why do you leave these offerings at my door? I have desired no companionship; I have sought to be alone with my grief."

She trembled, and the colour mounted to her cheeks; but she answered, with a simplicity that went directly to my heart, "I knew that you were sorrowful, and I, too, am sorrowful. My father was a fisherman, and the sea, which has taken your beloved, took also my father, three years since. I have no one now; and I thought that if I brought you flowers, you might, perhaps, know that there was one near you who understood your grief, and who wept with you, though she dared not speak."

Her voice was low and tremulous, but it penetrated the deepest recesses of my soul. I looked upon her, and saw, as in a mirror, the reflection of my own desolation. Here was a being, young, and gentle, and innocent, upon whom the same heavy hand of affliction had fallen, and who, amidst her own orphan loneliness, had sought to minister to mine. I had said that I would receive no sympathy; that I would bind up my wounds in sullen and impenetrable silence. But the voice of this child, so simple, so touching, so full of unfeigned fellow-feeling, broke down all the barriers I had raised. I opened my arms, and she flew into them; and we mingled our tears together, and felt, in that communion of sorrow, a balm such as neither had dared to hope.

From that hour, the blackness of my despair began to be chequered by faint, tremulous gleams of consolation. I no longer shunned the society of this gentle being. She came to me at dawn and at twilight, and we sat together by the shore, watching the ebb and flow of the tide, and speaking, in low and broken accents, of those we had lost. The sea no longer presented to me a spectacle of unmingled horror; for I had found, upon its desolate borders, a companion in affliction, and my heart, which had been frozen by the icy breath of solitude, thawed beneath the influence of a sympathy that was pure, disinterested, and sublime.
