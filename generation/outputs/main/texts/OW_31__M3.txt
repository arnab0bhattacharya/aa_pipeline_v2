Lady Gwendolen Fairfax was a young woman of such unimpeachable moral geometry that she had never once been tempted to commit a kindness. She moved through the drawing rooms of London with the serene assurance of a Greek statue, perfectly cold and infinitely admired. Her husband, Lord Arthur Fairfax, was a man of the world, which is to say he was a man who had succeeded in keeping his sins entirely separate from his virtues. 

It was, therefore, with a profound sense of shock that Gwendolen discovered, by the sheerest accident of a misplaced banker’s ledger, that Arthur had settled a staggering sum of debts belonging to Mrs. Vane. Mrs. Vane was a widow of such dazzling notoriety that her mere entrance into a room was sufficient to lower the tone of the furniture. She possessed a history that was far more public than her income, and a complexion that was rumoured to be as expensive as her conscience. 

Gwendolen confronted her husband in the ruby-draped library of their Grosvenor Square house. She did not raise her voice, for a well-bred woman never raises her voice; she merely sharpened it until it could cut glass.

"I have seen the ledger, Arthur," she said, her eyes gleaming with the cold fire of righteous indignation. "You have paid the debts of Mrs. Vane. I suppose I must ask if you are her lover, though the question is terribly middle-class. One really should not have to ask."

Arthur looked at her with an expression of profound weariness. He was holding a porcelain cup of tea, which he set down with a delicate clatter. "My dear Gwendolen," he replied, "there are only two kinds of people in London: those who talk about their morals, and those who pay for their pleasures. I have merely paid a bill. As for explanations, I am afraid I have none to offer. To explain is to apologise, and to apologise is to admit that one’s actions require the approval of society. I do not."

"I see," said Gwendolen, turning a shade of white that was rather becoming. "Then you leave me no alternative. I shall go to her."

"Go to whom?" asked Arthur, momentarily startled.

"To Mrs. Vane. She is giving a ball tomorrow night. I shall attend it, and I shall confront her in her own house."

Arthur smiled, a slow, languid smile that held more sorrow than amusement. "You will find it a very garish affair, Gwendolen. She has a terrible fondness for orchids and hypocrisy."

"I am not afraid of orchids," said Gwendolen, sweeping from the room with the majestic finality of a tragedy queen.

The following evening, Gwendolen descended upon Mrs. Vane’s residence in Belgravia. The house was a masterpiece of aesthetic excess. The corridors were lit by shaded lamps that cast a glow like the inside of a pomegranate, and the air was thick with the scent of tuberose and cigarette smoke. The guests moved through the rooms with the practiced ease of people who have long since forgotten why they came, but are determined to stay until they remember.

Gwendolen, dressed in a gown of severe black velvet that set off her pallor like a cameo, pushed through the throng. She found Mrs. Vane in the conservatory, standing beside a blooming Cypripedium. Mrs. Vane was a woman of extraordinary beauty, the kind of beauty that seems to improve with scandal, as a fine wine improves with age. She had eyes like dark jewels and a mouth that looked as though it had been made exclusively for telling lies.

"Mrs. Vane," said Gwendolen, advancing with the air of an avenging angel, albeit an angel dressed by Worth.

Mrs. Vane turned, raising her lorgnette to her eye with a languid grace. "Ah," she said, her voice a low, musical purr. "Lady Fairfax. How perfectly charming of you to come. I was just saying to Lord Pembroke that society is becoming terribly moral, which is always a sign that it is becoming terribly dull. But you are not dull, are you, Lady Fairfax?"

"I am not here for your wit, Mrs. Vane," Gwendolen replied, her voice trembling with the effort of remaining composed. "I am here to ask you why my husband has paid your debts."

A ripple of something—was it surprise? was it amusement?—passed over Mrs. Vane’s face. She plucked a petal from the orchid and let it fall. "How very like Arthur," she murmured. "He always did prefer to pay in secret. It is so much more expensive that way."

"Then you admit it?" cried Gwendolen, losing her grip on her composure for a fatal second. "You admit that he is your lover?"

Mrs. Vane laughed, a soft, silvery laugh that seemed to mock the very concept of gravity. "My dear Lady Fairfax, in London, a lover is merely a man who has not yet been found out. But Arthur is not my lover. He is my brother."

The words hung in the air like a suspended chord. Gwendolen stared at her, the colour rushing to her cheeks. "Your brother? But your name is Vane. Your history—"

"Is entirely my own invention," interrupted Mrs. Vane, adjusting a diamond bracelet that caught the light with a savage flash. "A woman without a past is a woman without a future. I invented Mrs. Vane because society demanded a scandal, and I have always found it is better to give people what they expect. Arthur, poor dear, has always been terribly conventional. When he married you, he wished to protect you from the taint of a sister who had chosen to be interesting. So he paid my debts, which were considerable, and I, in return, kept his secret. It was a perfectly commercial arrangement. You see, my dear, morality is merely a question of geography. In Belgravia, I am a scandal. In Paris, I am a necessity."

Gwendolen stood in silence, the intricate architecture of her indignation collapsing around her. She had come prepared to confront a rival, and had found instead a relative. The irony was almost too perfect to be borne.

"You mean," she said slowly, "that Arthur has been deceiving me for the sake of my own respectability?"

"Deception is the highest form of devotion, Lady Fairfax," said Mrs. Vane, taking a glass of champagne from a passing footman. "Only the very dull tell the truth. The rest of us understand that life is far too important to be taken seriously. Arthur loves you, I suppose, in his own cumbersome way. But he loves his reputation more, and as his sister, I am a threat to it. So he pays my bills, and I keep silent. It is the English way."

Gwendolen looked at the orchid, its bizarre, beautiful petals curling like a painted sin. She thought of Arthur’s silence, his refusal to explain, and for the first time, she understood it. He had not been unfaithful; he had merely been discreet. And in the world they inhabited, discretion was the only virtue that survived the season.

"I see," she said at last, a faint, enigmatic smile touching her lips. "Then I must thank you, Mrs. Vane, for a most illuminating evening. I had come expecting a tragedy, and I find I am merely in a farce."

"My dear," said Mrs. Vane, raising her glass, "in London, there is no difference. The only thing that separates a tragedy from a farce is the price of the tickets."

Gwendolen turned and made her way back through the scented, glittering rooms. As she stepped into her carriage, she caught a glimpse of her reflection in the glass. She looked older, and infinitely wiser. She had learned, as all women eventually learn, that the only way to deal with a man’s secrets is to ignore them. For a secret, once understood, is merely a fact, and facts are the most vulgar things in the world.
