# The Thirteenth Suitor

Lady Penelope Fawcett had broken twelve engagements, and all of London society had been counting. There had been Lord Ashcombe in her first season, who wept publicly outside Gunter's tea shop. There had been the Honourable Thomas Pellew, who fled to Ceylon and took up rubber cultivation rather than face his club again. There had been a duke's heir, a baronet, a promising young diplomat, and, memorably, a Scottish laird whose mother arrived in London with a claymore she was not entirely joking about. Penelope broke them all with the same serene, regretful smile, as though each engagement were an egg she had discovered to be rotten, and she the reluctant cook who had no choice but to discard it.

Now, in the summer of 1893, she was engaged for the thirteenth time, to Mr. Edmund Calloway, and the betting books at White's had never been so full.

"What I mean to do," said Edmund, adjusting his cuffs in the mirror of his Albany chambers, "is marry her. Actually marry her. At the altar. With the ring and the vicar and the whole machinery of it."

His friend, Mr. Philip Vane, who had been lounging on the sofa with a glass of brandy and the expression of a man watching another man step cheerfully toward a cliff edge, raised his eyebrows. "You are aware of the count."

"I am."

"Twelve."

"I can count, Philip."

"Eleven men of title, one of significant fortune, and a Scotsman whose family still speaks of a curse. She left the Scotsman at the church, Edmund. At the church. He was standing at the altar in full Highland dress, and she sent a note."

"A very polite note, I'm told."

"It was a polite note. That is not the point."

Edmund turned from the mirror. He was not a handsome man in the fashionable sense—his jaw was too square, his brow too heavy, his eyes the colour of strong tea rather than the preferred azure—but there was about him a solidity, a quality of masonry, that suggested he had been built to withstand weather. "The others," he said, "treated the engagement as a kind of performance. They posed and preened and assumed she would simply arrive at the church because that was what young ladies did. None of them asked why she kept leaving."

"And you have asked?"

"I have. She told me."

Philip sat up slightly. "And?"

"And I am not at liberty to say. But I assure you, the reasons are sound, and they do not apply to me."

"Every man thinks his reasons are different."

"Yes. And twelve of them were wrong."

---

Across Berkeley Square, in the pale green drawing room of the Fawcett townhouse, a rather different conversation was under way.

"You are not to break this one," said Lady Marguerite Fawcett, standing before the fireplace with the rigidity of a woman who had corseted her opinions as firmly as her figure. "I do not say this as a mother who wishes to see her daughter settled, though I am that. I say it as a woman of sense. Thirteen is a cursed number, Penelope. You break this engagement, and you will have broken thirteen, and you will carry that number with you for the rest of your life. No one will marry you. No one will receive you. You will be the woman who broke thirteen engagements, and that will be your epitaph."

Penelope, who was seated by the window with a piece of embroidery she had no intention of finishing, did not look up. "It is only unlucky if one is superstitious, Mama."

"Everyone is superstitious. The ones who say they aren't are the most superstitious of all, because they're superstitious about admitting it."

"That is a circular argument."

"It is a mother's argument, which is to say it is correct."

Penelope drew her needle through the linen. She was twenty-four, dark-haired, and possessed of a face that men found not so much beautiful as inescapable—there was something about the directness of her gaze that made one feel observed with unusual clarity. She had broken eleven engagements before she fully understood why. The twelfth had taught her. The twelfth had been the Scotsman, Alasdair Munro, and she had liked him genuinely, had been prepared to marry him, until she overheard him tell his brother, the evening before the wedding, that he intended to sell the Edinburgh house she loved and move them to a shooting estate in Perthshire where, he said, she would "learn to be a proper wife and stop reading all that nonsense."

She had sent the note the next morning. Polite. Regretful. Final.

"The difficulty, Mama," Penelope said now, "is that men propose to an idea of me. They propose to a woman who will arrange their houses, bear their children, and reflect their consequence. They do not propose to me. They do not ask what I read, or what I think, or what I want. They assume there is nothing to ask."

"Every woman in London could say the same."

"Then every woman in London should send the same note."

Lady Marguerite made a sound that was not quite a laugh. "You will be ruined."

"Twelve engagements have not ruined me yet."

"Thirteen will. The number itself will do what the twelve could not. People will say you are cursed. They will say no man can hold you, that you are faithless by nature, that—"

"That I refuse to be owned by a man who does not see me." Penelope set down her embroidery. "Mr. Calloway sees me, Mama. He asked me what I wanted. No one has ever asked me that before. Not once in twelve engagements."

Lady Marguerite's mouth opened, then closed. She studied her daughter with an expression that contained, beneath its formidable architecture of disapproval, something softer. "He asked you what you wanted."

"He sat across from me in the conservatory and said, 'I should like to know what you want, because I should very much like to be it, and I cannot be it if I do not know.'"

"And what did you tell him?"

"That I wanted to be asked. That I wanted to read what I chose. That I wanted a room of my own and a say in the arrangement of our life. That I wanted to be a wife, but not a possession."

"And he said?"

"He said, 'I believe I can manage that. Shall we try?'"

Lady Marguerite was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the London season churned on—carriages rattling past the square, the distant clip of hooves, a flower seller calling his wares. The drawing room smelled of beeswax and the early roses on the mantelpiece.

"Thirteen," she said, almost to herself. "It is a terrible number to risk."

"It is a terrible number to fear," said Penelope.

---

The wedding took place on a Saturday in June at St. Cuthbert's, Mayfair. The church was full. The betting books at White's were closed, the wagers settled with varying degrees of grace. Edmund Calloway stood at the altar in a coat of dark grey, looking not nervous but attentive, as though he were waiting for a conversation to begin.

Penelope arrived on time. She walked the aisle with her mother, who gripped her arm with a strength that was not entirely steadiness and not entirely pride but some compound of both. The congregation held its breath with the practised discipline of people who had attended twelve previous attempts and had brought, in several cases, reading material for a long wait.

Penelope reached the altar. She looked at Edmund. He looked at her.

"Ready?" he said quietly.

"Ready," she said.

The vicar began. No note arrived. No one fled. The ring was placed, the vows were spoken, and when it was done, when the church doors opened onto the June sunlight and the Fawcett townhouse bells began to ring across Berkeley Square, Lady Marguerite, standing in the porch with tears she would later attribute to the wind, whispered to no one in particular, "Thirteen. Thank God for thirteen."
