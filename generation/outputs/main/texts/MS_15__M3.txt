It was in the melancholy autumn of the year two thousand and ninety-eight that I, Clara Vane, set forth alone upon the ruined thoroughfares of England, driven by a hope that was little better than despair, to seek my brother Lionel. A whole year had passed since the last trembling transmission from the western settlements had reached me—a year of silence so profound that it seemed the very earth had drawn a final, suffocating breath over the graves of its inhabitants. The Great Decay, that pestilential tide which had swept the vigor from our race, had left our country a desolate monument to vanished multitudes; the cities were become charnel-houses of silent glass and rusted iron, and the fields, untilled and untrodden, were reverting to the wild dominion of nature. I had been bred in the deep solitude of the northern valleys, among the survivors who clung to one another with a fierce, melancholy affection; but when the silence regarding Lionel stretched beyond endurance, I could no longer remain. I armed myself with such meager provisions as my adopted kindred could spare, and committing my destiny to the Providence that watches over the forlorn, I turned my face toward the south.

The roads, once thronged with the ceaseless march of human enterprise, were now but stony ribbons winding through a wilderness of encroaching briar and collapsing masonry. To walk for days without encountering a living soul was to taste the bitterness of a universal grave; the silence was not merely the absence of sound, but a heavy, palpable entity, pressing upon the heart with a weight that no tears could relieve. Yet the landscape, in its sublime desolation, awoke feelings akin to exaltation. The vast canopy of the heavens, swept by tempestuous winds, seemed to look down in solemn pity upon the ruined works of man; and the sun, breaking through the rifted clouds, cast a glory upon the decayed palaces of the ancients that filled my soul with a mournful rapture. What was the pride of that extinct generation, who had reared these towering structures of steel and stone, compared to the enduring majesty of the mountains and the sea? They had aspired to immortality, and their monuments were but the nests of wild birds and the refuge of the storm-beaten wanderer.

It was upon the fourth evening of my journey, as I descended the southern slopes of the Malvern hills, that the tempest burst forth with unexampled fury. The lightning played incessantly among the crags, illuminating the black and heaving waters of the Severn, which had burst its ancient bounds and spread a wide and desolate lake over the submerged valleys. I was weary, my strength exhausted by the toil of the day, and my spirit sank within me as I beheld the devastation of the waters. I sought shelter in the ruin of a great bridge, whose broken arches, springing from the turbid flood, offered a precarious refuge from the deluge. As I crouched beneath the massive stones, shivering in the blast, I became aware that I was not alone. A figure, emerging from the deeper shadows of the ruin, approached me with a slow and hesitating step. It was an old man, his hair white as the driven snow, his face furrowed by the hand of time and sorrow, but his eyes, though dimmed by tears, retained a fire that bespoke a spirit unsubdued by calamity.

He spoke to me with a gentle, melancholy courtesy, and drawing his tattered cloak around us both, he strove to cheer me with words of compassionate encouragement. "We are the children of the storm, my daughter," he said, "and the tempest without is but a type of the desolation that has swept over our race. But do not let your heart fail. I have seen the day when these valleys were filled with the sounds of life and joy; I have seen the harvests gathered, and the children playing in the meadows; and though all is gone, though I am left alone, a leafless trunk in a forest of the dead, I do not despair. There is a power that rules the storm, and that power is love. For the sake of those I have loved and lost, I endure." I looked upon him with wonder, for in his words I recognized the echo of my own feelings; and as the night wore on, and the storm raged without, we sat together in the darkness, and he told me the tale of his wanderings—of his wife, who had perished in the first onset of the pestilence, of his children, scattered and lost, and of his long, fruitless search for them through the length and breadth of the land. His grief was a monument to the past, and yet it was hallowed by a patience that seemed more than human. When the morning broke, and the clouds rolled away, he bade me farewell, blessing me with a fervor that brought tears to my eyes, and turning his steps toward the east, he was soon lost to sight among the misty uplands.

I pursued my journey with a heart fortified by this encounter, yet the days that followed brought only a deeper sense of the universal woe. The roads were marked by the tokens of former habitation—ruined farmhouses, gardens overgrown with noxious weeds, and the melancholy remains of vehicles, half buried in the encroaching verdure. At times I passed through the outskirts of a town, where the silent streets and shuttered windows seemed to reproach me with the memory of the living. The very air was heavy with the scent of decay, and the birds, singing in the copses, seemed to chant a requiem over the departed. I met other wanderers, too—a young mother, carrying a child in her arms, whose eyes were wild with the fever of starvation; a man of middle age, seated by the wayside, who gazed upon the heavens with the vacant stare of one whose reason had been overthrown by grief; and a girl, scarcely older than myself, who wept bitterly over a grave she had just dug for her last companion. To each I gave what succor I could, and from each I received the same tale of loss and bereavement. We were all pilgrims in a land of shadows, bound together by the common chain of sorrow, and seeking, in the midst of desolation, for some faint gleam of hope.

It was on the tenth day of my journey, as I approached the ruins of the ancient city of Aquae, that I encountered a solitary traveler upon the road. He was a young man, tall and gaunt, with a face of striking beauty, but worn by suffering and privation. His eyes, dark and luminous, gleamed with an intensity that seemed to penetrate my very soul; and as he drew near, he paused and regarded me with an air of startled recognition. "You are Clara Vane," he said, in a voice that trembled with emotion. "I know you by your resemblance to your brother." At the sound of Lionel's name, my heart leaped within my breast, and I seized the stranger's hand with an eagerness I could not repress. "Tell me," I cried, "is he living? Where shall I find him?" The stranger regarded me with a look of profound compassion, and I saw that his eyes were filled with tears. "He was living," he replied, "when I last beheld him, three months since, in the western settlement of Portus. He was then in feeble health, but his spirit was unbroken, and he spoke of you continually, praying that you might yet be spared to rejoin him. I am Ambrose Lynd, and I was his companion in many a weary march. I left him to seek aid among the eastern survivors, but I have been thwarted in my mission, and now I return with nothing but grief."

He spoke with an eloquence and a pathos that moved me to the depths of my being. He told me of Lionel's sufferings—of his illness, his privations, and his unshaken faith that I would come to him; and as he spoke, the darkness that had gathered about my soul seemed to lift. I thanked him with a fervor that words could scarcely express, and implored him to guide me to Portus. He consented readily, and for many days we journeyed together through the desolate land, sharing our provisions and our hopes. Ambrose Lynd was a man of deep reflection and ardent sensibility; he had been a scholar in the old world, and his mind was stored with the treasures of ancient learning. He spoke of the fallen race of man with a melancholy admiration, and of the future with a grave and uncertain hope. "We are the remnants of a mighty nation," he said, "and upon us devolves the task of rebuilding the fabric of human society. But alas, how faint are our powers, how shattered our resources! The past is a tomb, and the future is a wilderness. Yet the divine spark within us is not extinguished, and so long as love and pity remain, there is yet a foundation upon which to build."

His words sank deep into my heart, and I pondered them as we pursued our way. At length, upon a clear and frosty morning, we descended the western slopes and beheld the sea. The settlement of Portus lay beside the shore, a cluster of humble dwellings, half hidden among the ruins of the ancient harbor. My heart beat wildly as I hastened toward the village, and Ambrose, with a smile of tender sympathy, pointed out the dwelling where Lionel had been lodged. I rushed forward, and as I entered the lowly door, I beheld him—pale, emaciated, but living! He started up at my approach, and with a cry of joy that rang through the silent chamber, he clasped me in his arms. In that moment of reunion, the agony of the past was forgotten; the storm, the desolation, the universal woe, were as nothing compared to the bliss of that embrace. And yet, as I looked upon his wasted form, and thought of the ruined world without, a deeper sorrow mingled with my joy—a sorrow for the race that had perished, for the hopes that had been blighted, and for the heavy destiny that awaited us, the lonely survivors of a fallen world.
